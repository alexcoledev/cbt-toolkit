Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.13) is defined by a full manic or hypomanic episode that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition. This is a secondary bipolar disorder — the mood episode is caused by the medical condition, not by the primary bipolar disease process. The medical condition must be established as the cause through history, physical examination, or laboratory findings. Common causes include neurological conditions (stroke, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, brain tumors, autoimmune encephalitis, Huntington's disease), endocrine conditions (hyperthyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, pheochromocytoma), infectious conditions (HIV/AIDS, neurosyphilis, viral encephalitis), metabolic conditions (hepatic encephalopathy, uremic encephalopathy, vitamin B12 deficiency, Wilson's disease), and medication/substance-induced (corticosteroids, levodopa, stimulants — though these are coded separately as substance/medication-induced). Specifiers include with manic features, with hypomanic features, and with mixed features. The critical distinction from primary bipolar is etiology: in 293.13, treating the underlying medical condition is the primary intervention, with mood stabilizers and antipsychotics as adjuncts for acute symptom control. However, the mood episode produces cognitive distortions that block integrated care — the dismissal of secondary bipolar as not real, the fusion of the medical condition with identity, the reductionism that dismisses CBT, the willpower demand, the fatalism from cause permanence, and the mind-body dualism that fragments treatment. What is Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.13) is a Bipolar & Related Disorder characterized by a manic or hypomanic episode caused by an identifiable medical condition. The DSM-5 criteria require: (a) a full manic or hypomanic episode — meeting the full DSM-5 criteria for a manic episode (1+ week of elevated/expansive/irritable mood + increased energy/activity, marked impairment, possible psychosis) or a hypomanic episode (4+ days, 3+ DIGFAST symptoms, no marked impairment, no psychosis); (b) the mood episode is the direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition — established through history, physical examination, or laboratory findings; (c) the disturbance is not better explained by a primary mental disorder (primary Bipolar I or II); (d) the symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment. The medical conditions that most commonly cause bipolar due to medical condition include: stroke (especially right hemisphere and orbitofrontal lesions), traumatic brain injury (particularly frontal and temporal lobe injuries), multiple sclerosis (demyelinating lesions in mood-regulating regions), epilepsy (postictal mania, interictal mood episodes), hyperthyroidism (thyroid storm can produce mania), Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol), HIV/AIDS (HIV encephalopathy, opportunistic CNS infection), hepatic encephalopathy (liver failure with CNS effects), vitamin B12 deficiency (can produce mania and depression), brain tumors (especially frontal and temporal lobe tumors), autoimmune encephalitis (e.g. anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis), and neurosyphilis. The temporal relationship is key: the mood episode begins during or shortly after the medical condition is present, and may resolve as the medical condition is treated. Specifiers: with manic features (full manic episode), with hypomanic features (full hypomanic episode), with mixed features (simultaneous manic and depressive symptoms). The prognosis depends on the medical condition: if the medical condition is treatable and resolves (e.g. hyperthyroidism treated with antithyroid medication, vitamin B12 deficiency repleted, brain tumor resected), the mood symptoms may resolve completely without recurrence; if the medical condition is chronic or progressive (e.g. MS, TBI sequelae, HIV), the mood symptoms may require ongoing management. CBT adapted for bipolar due to medical condition is evidence-based as an adjunct to medical treatment: it targets the cognitive distortions that block integrated care, supports medical adherence, builds mood monitoring that detects relapse early, provides grief work for the loss of the pre-illness health identity, and coordinates with the treating physician and psychiatrist.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

CBT for Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.13) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the dismissal and reductionism distortions that block integrated care, the brokenness fusion and willpower demand that block self-compassion and adherence, the fatalism and dualism that block coordinated treatment. CBT is an adjunct to medical treatment of the underlying condition and psychiatric medication for acute symptom control — it does not replace either, but improves outcomes, adherence, and quality of life.

1. Psychoeducation, Medical-Mood Link Education, Mood Monitoring & Medical Condition Monitoring

The foundation of CBT for bipolar due to medical condition is understanding the medical-mood link, identifying the cognitive distortions that block integrated care, tracking both mood and medical condition status, and detecting relapse early. Psychoeducation: learn the DSM-5 criteria (full manic or hypomanic episode that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition), the distinction from primary bipolar (primary has no identified medical cause; secondary does), the range of medical conditions that cause secondary bipolar (stroke, TBI, MS, epilepsy, hyperthyroidism, Cushing's, HIV, hepatic encephalopathy, B12 deficiency, brain tumors, autoimmune encephalitis), the prognosis distinction (if the medical condition resolves, the mood may resolve completely; if chronic, the mood requires ongoing management), the role of CBT as adjunct (improves outcomes, adherence, and quality of life even when the cause is physical), and the importance of integrated care (the medical team and psychiatric team must communicate and coordinate). Medical-mood link education is the diagnostic cornerstone: many people with bipolar due to medical condition have never understood how their medical condition produces mood symptoms — the The-Medical-Condition-Explains-Everything distortion dismisses the link as irrelevant, while the The-Mood-Is-Separate-From-The-Body distortion fragments the link into separate problems. CBT helps the person understand the specific mechanism by which their medical condition produces mood symptoms (e.g. stroke affecting frontal lobe mood regulation circuits, hyperthyroidism increasing adrenergic tone that mimics mania, MS demyelinating mood-regulating pathways, TBI damaging frontal and temporal lobe function), which makes the mood symptoms comprehensible rather than mysterious and reduces the self-blame of the I-Should-Be-Able-To-Control-This distortion. Daily mood charting: rate mood on a scale (e.g., -3 to +3, 0 euthymia) each day alongside medical condition status, medications taken, sleep hours, energy, and significant events — this reveals the relationship between medical condition fluctuations and mood symptoms, and is essential for detecting relapse early. Medical condition monitoring: track the medical condition status (e.g. thyroid function labs, MS relapse markers, seizure frequency, TBI symptom checklist) alongside mood — this integrates the two and detects when medical changes predict mood changes. Early warning sign identification: identify the specific early signs of mood episode relapse (decreased need for sleep, increased energy, racing thoughts, elevated mood for manic/hypomanic; increased sleep, anhedonia, low mood for depressive) and the specific signs of medical condition change that may trigger mood change — with a written action plan for each.

2. Cognitive Restructuring of Dismissal, Brokenness, Reductionism, Willpower, Fatalism & Dualism Distortions + Medical Adherence Support

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain the condition. Keep a secondary bipolar belief log: each day, write down the distressing thoughts (the it is not real bipolar, my body is broken so I am broken, the medical condition explains everything, I should be able to control this, treatment is hopeless because the cause is permanent, the mood is separate from the body), identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the mood episode causes real distress, impairment, and risk including suicide risk during mixed states that warrants direct treatment regardless of cause; the self persists across the medical condition and is not identical to it; CBT improves outcomes, adherence, and quality of life for medically-caused mood symptoms across stroke, TBI, MS, epilepsy, and other conditions; self-blame over medically-caused symptoms worsens outcomes by reducing adherence and increasing depression; chronic medical conditions can have well-managed mood symptoms and mood treatment improves quality of life regardless of cause permanence; secondary bipolar requires integrated care where mood treatment supports medical adherence and medical treatment supports mood stability), and generate a reframe. Use Socratic questioning: If this is not real bipolar, why does the mood episode meet full DSM-5 criteria for a manic or hypomanic episode, why does it cause marked impairment, and why does dismissing it as not real lead to undertreatment of real symptoms? If my body is broken so I am broken, why does the self persist across the condition — why do I still have values, relationships, preferences, and goals that are not the medical condition, and why would the condition be the self rather than something the self has? If the medical condition explains everything and CBT is irrelevant, why do randomized trials show CBT improves outcomes for post-stroke depression, post-TBI mood symptoms, and MS-related mood disorders, and why does medical treatment alone often leave mood symptoms undertreated? If I should be able to control this through willpower, why does the medical condition produce the mood symptoms through physiological mechanisms that willpower does not reverse, and why does self-blame worsen outcomes by reducing adherence and increasing depression? If treatment is hopeless because the cause is permanent, why do people with chronic medical conditions (MS, TBI sequelae, HIV) achieve well-managed mood symptoms with ongoing treatment, and why does mood treatment improve quality of life even when the cause cannot be cured? If the mood is separate from the body, why does medical condition status predict mood symptoms, why does medical adherence influence mood stability, and why does mood influence self-care and adherence — and why does fragmented care produce worse outcomes than coordinated care? Medical adherence support: the mood symptoms (especially depression and mania) directly undermine medical adherence — depression reduces motivation and self-care, mania produces treatment refusal and grandiose beliefs about being cured. CBT addresses adherence through motivational interviewing, adherence tracking, problem-solving barriers (side effects, cost, complexity, forgetfulness), and cognitive restructuring of adherence-blocking beliefs. Grief work for health identity loss: the medical condition and the mood episode both involve loss — loss of the pre-illness self, loss of health, loss of roles and capabilities. CBT provides a structured space to grieve these losses, distinguish grief (the healthy processing of loss) from the My-Body-Is-Broken-So-I-Am-Broken distortion (the fusion of loss with identity collapse), and build a valued identity that incorporates the condition without being defined by it.

3. Behavioral Regulation, Medical-Mood Integration & Relapse Prevention

Sleep regulation is critical in bipolar due to medical condition. Sleep disruption is both a symptom of the mood episode (decreased need for sleep in mania/hypomania, hypersomnia in depression) and a trigger for episode relapse (sleep loss can precipitate manic switches). CBT builds sleep hygiene: consistent sleep/wake times, wind-down routine, no stimulating activity when sleep is threatened, and immediate clinician contact if sleep need drops significantly. Activity regulation: during manic/hypomanic periods, do not increase goal-directed activity and stimulation (the increased energy is a symptom, not a resource); during depressive periods, use behavioral activation principles (activity scheduling, graded task assignment). Substance reduction: alcohol, cannabis, and stimulants destabilize mood, interact with medical medications, and can trigger relapse — CBT addresses use patterns as relapse risk factors. Medical-mood integration: the single most important behavioral intervention is integrating medical and mood care. This includes: bringing the mood chart and medical condition tracker to every appointment (both medical and psychiatric), requesting that the medical team and psychiatric team communicate (care coordination, shared records, joint treatment planning), scheduling medical and psychiatric appointments in a coordinated way rather than fragmenting care, and treating medical adherence and mood management as one integrated project rather than two separate problems. Relapse prevention plan: a written plan that includes the early warning signs of mood episode relapse (decreased need for sleep, increased energy, racing thoughts for manic; increased sleep, anhedonia, low mood for depressive), the early warning signs of medical condition change that may trigger mood change (specific to the condition — e.g. thyroid symptom flare, MS relapse signs, seizure frequency increase, TBI symptom worsening), the action plan for each sign (when to contact the psychiatrist, when to contact the medical specialist, what medication adjustments to make, what behavioral changes to make), both clinicians' contact information, a list of decisions to defer during mood episodes (medical decisions, financial decisions, relationship decisions), and the specific integration steps (share records, coordinate appointments, joint treatment planning). Family/systemic support: family psychoeducation about bipolar due to medical condition, the medical-mood link, the importance of integrated care, early warning signs, and the role of adherence — family can support adherence, detect early warning signs, and facilitate care coordination. Comorbid treatment: address comorbid depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use, and grief that frequently co-occur with bipolar due to medical condition and complicate the clinical picture.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

Each distortion maintains the condition by blocking integrated care, blocking adherence, blocking self-compassion, or blocking coordinated treatment. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. It-Is-Not-Real-Bipolar (secondary-dismissal / cause-dismissal): "This isn't real bipolar. It's just from my medical condition. It's a symptom of the stroke/TBI/MS/thyroid problem, not a psychiatric condition that needs its own treatment. I'll just treat the medical condition and the mood will take care of itself." The person dismisses the mood episode as not real bipolar because it is secondary, blocking direct psychiatric and psychological treatment.
Reframe: "The mood episode is real — it meets full DSM-5 criteria for a manic or hypomanic episode, causes marked impairment, and carries real risk including suicide risk during mixed states. The secondary nature means the cause is medical, not that the symptoms are insignificant or that treatment is unnecessary. In fact, secondary bipolar is often undertreated precisely because of this dismissal — the mood episode persists or relapses even when the medical condition is being treated, because the mood symptoms are not being directly addressed. Treating the medical condition is necessary but often not sufficient: mood stabilizers and antipsychotics may be needed for acute symptom control, and CBT improves outcomes, adherence, and quality of life even when the cause is physical. The It-Is-Not-Real-Bipolar is a distortion that confuses the cause with the significance; the antidote is recognizing that secondary bipolar warrants direct treatment alongside the medical treatment, not dismissal. The key question: if this is not real bipolar, why does it meet full diagnostic criteria, why does it cause marked impairment, and why does dismissing it lead to undertreatment of real symptoms?"
2. My-Body-Is-Broken-So-I-Am-Broken (brokenness-fusion / medical-identity-fusion): "My body is broken. The stroke damaged my brain, the MS is progressive, the TBI changed who I am. The medical condition defines me now. I am a broken person. I am my diagnosis." The person fuses the medical condition with identity, producing shame, hopelessness, and treatment disengagement.
Reframe: "The medical condition is something I have, not something I am. The self persists across the condition — the values, relationships, preferences, capabilities, and goals that are not the medical condition are still present, still mine, and still the basis of identity. The condition produces symptoms, limitations, and losses that are real and that require grief — but grief is the healthy processing of loss, not identity collapse. The My-Body-Is-Broken-So-I-Am-Broken is a distortion that equates a condition with the self; the antidote is recognizing that the self is what persists across the condition, that the condition is something the self has rather than something the self is, and that building a valued identity that incorporates the condition without being defined by it is the goal. The key test: if the condition is my identity, why do I still have values, relationships, and preferences that are not the condition, and why would treating the condition eliminate the self rather than the symptoms?"
3. The-Medical-Condition-Explains-Everything (medical-reductionism / psychological-dismissal): "It's all just the medical condition. The mood, the cognition, the behavior — it's all caused by the stroke/TBI/MS/thyroid. Psychology is irrelevant to a physical cause. CBT is for real mental illness, not for organic mood symptoms. I just need the neurologist/endocrinologist to fix the medical problem." The person dismisses CBT as irrelevant because the cause is physical, blocking the cognitive and behavioral interventions that improve outcomes.
Reframe: "CBT improves outcomes for medically-caused mood symptoms across conditions. Randomized trials show CBT reduces post-stroke depression, improves post-TBI mood and function, reduces MS-related depression and anxiety, and improves epilepsy-related mood symptoms — even though the cause is physical in each case. The reason is that mood symptoms are not just the direct product of the medical cause — they are maintained by cognitive distortions (the dismissal, the brokenness fusion, the fatalism), behavioral patterns (sleep disruption, social withdrawal, substance use), and care fragmentation that CBT directly addresses. Medical treatment alone often leaves these maintaining factors untreated, producing worse outcomes than combined care. The The-Medical-Condition-Explains-Everything is a distortion that confuses the cause with the maintaining factors; the antidote is recognizing that even when the cause is physical, the cognitive, behavioral, and systemic factors that maintain the mood symptoms are legitimate targets of CBT, and treating them improves outcomes. The key question: if CBT is irrelevant to a physical cause, why do trials show it improves outcomes for post-stroke, post-TBI, and MS mood symptoms, and why does medical treatment alone often leave mood undertreated?"
4. I-Should-Be-Able-To-Control-This (willpower-control / medical-willpower-distortion): "I should be able to control these mood symptoms through willpower. The medical cause doesn't excuse losing control. Other people with this condition manage it — I should too. I'm being weak." The person demands willpower control over medically-caused mood symptoms, producing self-blame, shame, and reduced adherence.
Reframe: "The mood symptoms are produced by physiological mechanisms — the medical condition affecting mood-regulating circuits, hormones, or neurotransmitters — that willpower does not reverse. Willpower cannot restore frontal lobe function after a stroke, cannot remyelinate MS lesions, cannot reduce thyroid hormone levels, cannot repair TBI damage. The demand for willpower control over physiologically-caused symptoms is a category error: it treats a medical symptom as a character failing. Self-blame over medically-caused symptoms worsens outcomes by producing shame (which blocks help-seeking and disclosure), reducing adherence (why treat a condition I should be able to control through willpower?), and increasing depression (self-blame is a depressogenic cognition). The I-Should-Be-Able-To-Control-This is a distortion that applies a willpower frame to a medical problem; the antidote is recognizing that the medical cause does not make willpower effective, that self-compassion (not self-blame) is the appropriate response to medically-caused symptoms, and that effective management requires medical and psychological treatment, not willpower. The key question: if I should be able to control this through willpower, why does the medical condition produce the symptoms through physiological mechanisms, why does willpower not reverse those mechanisms, and why does self-blame worsen rather than improve outcomes?"
5. Treatment-Is-Hopeless-Because-The-Cause-Is-Permanent (cause-permanence-fatalism / treatment-hopelessness): "The medical condition is permanent — the brain damage from the stroke is permanent, the MS is progressive, the TBI is permanent. So the mood symptoms will never get better. Treating the mood is pointless when the cause is forever. I'll just have to live with it." The person fatalizes mood treatment from the permanence of the medical cause, blocking treatment engagement.
Reframe: "Chronic and permanent medical conditions can have well-managed mood symptoms. The permanence of the cause does not determine the manageability of the symptoms: people with MS, TBI sequelae, post-stroke deficits, HIV, and other chronic conditions achieve meaningful mood symptom reduction and quality of life improvement with ongoing treatment — mood stabilizers, CBT, behavioral regulation, and integrated care. The fatalism confuses cause permanence with symptom permanence: the cause may be permanent, but the symptoms are maintained by cognitive, behavioral, and systemic factors that are treatable even when the cause is not. Mood treatment improves quality of life regardless of cause permanence — reducing depression, controlling mania, improving sleep, restoring function, and enabling engagement with life even within the constraints of the medical condition. The Treatment-Is-Hopeless-Because-The-Cause-Is-Permanent is a distortion that treats the cause as the only legitimate target of treatment; the antidote is recognizing that symptom management, quality of life, and functional improvement are legitimate treatment goals even when the cause cannot be cured, and that mood treatment achieves them. The key question: if treatment is hopeless because the cause is permanent, why do people with chronic medical conditions achieve well-managed mood symptoms, and why does mood treatment improve quality of life even when the cause cannot be reversed?"
6. The-Mood-Is-Separate-From-The-Body (mind-body-dualism / integration-failure): "My mood problems are separate from my medical condition. The mood is psychiatric, the body is medical — they're different problems. I'll see the psychiatrist for the mood and the neurologist for the MS, and they don't need to talk. I'll treat them separately." The person treats the mood and the medical condition as independent problems, blocking the integrated care that secondary bipolar requires.
Reframe: "Secondary bipolar requires integrated care because the mood and the medical condition are causally and clinically linked. The medical condition status predicts mood symptoms (a thyroid flare predicts mania, an MS relapse predicts mood change, a seizure increase predicts mood instability); the mood symptoms influence medical adherence (depression reduces self-care and medication adherence, mania produces treatment refusal); and treating one without the other produces worse outcomes than coordinated care. The mind-body dualism — that the mood is psychiatric and the body is medical and they are separate — is a distortion that fragments care: the psychiatrist treats the mood without understanding the medical context, the neurologist/endocrinologist treats the medical condition without addressing the mood, and neither addresses the integration that the condition requires. The The-Mood-Is-Separate-From-The-Body is a distortion that treats a linked system as two independent problems; the antidote is recognizing that secondary bipolar is a medical-mood condition that requires coordinated care — shared records, care coordination, joint treatment planning, and a clinical team that communicates. The key question: if the mood and the body are separate, why does medical condition status predict mood symptoms, why does mood influence medical adherence, and why does fragmented care produce worse outcomes than integrated care?"

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition requires coordinated care from the treating physician (neurologist, endocrinologist, internist), a psychiatrist, and a CBT-trained clinician. Seek help if: you have a medical condition (stroke, TBI, MS, epilepsy, thyroid disorder, Cushing's, HIV, brain tumor, autoimmune encephalitis, B12 deficiency) and have developed mood symptoms (elevated/irritable mood with increased energy and decreased need for sleep, or depressed mood with anhedonia) — this may be bipolar due to medical condition and warrants evaluation; the mood symptoms are severe (marked impairment, psychotic features, hospitalization needed) — this requires immediate psychiatric evaluation; there are thoughts of self-harm or suicide (seek immediate help — call a crisis line or go to an emergency department); sleep has decreased and you feel less need for sleep (this is a warning sign of manic escalation — contact your clinician immediately); you have been dismissing the mood symptoms as 'not real bipolar' or 'just from the medical condition' (this is the It-Is-Not-Real-Bipolar distortion — the mood episode warrants direct treatment regardless of cause); you have been blaming yourself for medically-caused mood symptoms (this is the I-Should-Be-Able-To-Control-This distortion — self-blame worsens outcomes); your medical and psychiatric care are fragmented across providers who do not communicate (this is the The-Mood-Is-Separate-From-The-Body distortion — request care coordination); you have stopped mood treatment because you believe the cause is permanent (this is the Treatment-Is-Hopeless-Because-The-Cause-Is-Permanent distortion — chronic causes can have well-managed symptoms); you are struggling with grief over the loss of your pre-illness health identity (this is legitimate grief that warrants support, distinct from the My-Body-Is-Broken-So-I-Am-Broken identity collapse); there is a comorbid condition (depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use) that complicates the clinical picture; or the medical condition is progressing or relapsing (this may trigger mood relapse and requires proactive monitoring).

Frequently Asked Questions

How is Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition different from primary Bipolar I or Bipolar II?

The distinction is etiological. Primary Bipolar I (296.x) and Bipolar II (296.89) have no identified medical cause — the mood episodes arise from the bipolar disease process itself, which is thought to involve genetic vulnerability, neurobiological dysregulation, and psychosocial stressors. Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (293.13) is SECONDARY — the manic or hypomanic episode is the direct pathophysiological consequence of an identifiable medical condition (stroke, TBI, MS, epilepsy, hyperthyroidism, Cushing's, HIV, B12 deficiency, brain tumor, autoimmune encephalitis, etc.), established through history, physical examination, or laboratory findings. The distinction matters for treatment: in primary bipolar, mood stabilizers and CBT target the bipolar disease process directly; in secondary bipolar, the PRIMARY treatment is treating the underlying medical condition (antithyroid medication for hyperthyroidism, anticonvulsants for epilepsy, immunomodulators for MS, B12 repletion for deficiency), with mood stabilizers and antipsychotics as adjuncts for acute symptom control and CBT supporting medical adherence, mood monitoring, and the cognitive distortions specific to secondary bipolar. The prognosis differs: if the medical condition is treatable and resolves, the mood symptoms may resolve completely; if the medical condition is chronic, the mood symptoms require ongoing management. The cognitive distortions also differ: primary bipolar involves identity-fusion with mania and medication-flattening fears; secondary bipolar involves brokenness-fusion with the medical condition, medical-reductionism that dismisses CBT, cause-permanence fatalism, and mind-body dualism that blocks integrated care.

What medical conditions most commonly cause Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

The most common causes fall into several categories. Neurological conditions: stroke (especially right hemisphere and orbitofrontal lesions), traumatic brain injury (particularly frontal and temporal lobe injuries), multiple sclerosis (demyelinating lesions in mood-regulating regions), epilepsy (postictal mania, interictal mood episodes), Parkinson's disease (medication-induced mania from levodopa/dopamine agonists), brain tumors (especially frontal and temporal lobe tumors), autoimmune encephalitis (e.g. anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis), Huntington's disease, and neurosyphilis. Endocrine conditions: hyperthyroidism (thyroid storm can produce mania), Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol produces mood elevation), and pheochromocytoma (catecholamine excess). Infectious conditions: HIV/AIDS (HIV encephalopathy, opportunistic CNS infections), viral encephalitis, and neurosyphilis. Metabolic conditions: hepatic encephalopathy (liver failure with CNS effects), uremic encephalopathy (kidney failure), vitamin B12 deficiency (can produce both mania and depression), and Wilson's disease (copper accumulation). Medication/substance-induced (coded separately as 292.61/292.64 but related): corticosteroids (prednisone can trigger mania), levodopa and dopamine agonists, stimulants, and sympathomimetics. The temporal relationship is key: the mood episode begins during or shortly after the medical condition is present, and may resolve as the medical condition is treated. If you have a medical condition in any of these categories and develop mood symptoms (elevated/irritable mood with increased energy and decreased need for sleep, or depressed mood with anhedonia), evaluation for bipolar due to medical condition is warranted.

Can the mood symptoms resolve if the medical condition is treated?

Yes — the prognosis depends on the medical condition. If the medical condition is treatable and resolves completely, the mood symptoms may resolve completely without recurrence. For example: hyperthyroidism treated with antithyroid medication or radioactive iodine may resolve the manic symptoms entirely; vitamin B12 deficiency repleted with B12 injections may resolve both mania and depression; a brain tumor resected may resolve the mood symptoms if the mood-regulating circuits recover; autoimmune encephalitis treated with immunotherapy may resolve the mood symptoms if the autoimmune process is halted. In these cases, ongoing mood treatment may not be needed after the medical condition resolves, though monitoring for recurrence is warranted. However, if the medical condition is chronic or progressive (e.g. MS, TBI sequelae, HIV, Parkinson's, Huntington's), the mood symptoms may require ongoing management even when the medical condition is being treated — the medical treatment reduces but does not eliminate the mood symptoms, and mood stabilizers, CBT, and behavioral regulation may be needed long-term. The Treatment-Is-Hopeless-Because-The-Cause-Is-Permanent distortion blocks engagement with this ongoing management by fatalizing mood treatment from cause permanence; the antidote is recognizing that chronic causes can have well-managed mood symptoms and that mood treatment improves quality of life regardless of whether the cause can be cured. The prognosis should be discussed with both the treating physician (who can assess the medical condition's trajectory) and a psychiatrist (who can assess the mood symptom trajectory) — integrated care produces better prognostic assessment than either alone.
