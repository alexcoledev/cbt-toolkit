What is Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.89) is a psychotic disorder in the Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders category, diagnosed when: (1) Criterion A — prominent hallucinations or delusions are present; (2) Criterion B — evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the disturbance is the direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition that is etiologically related to the symptoms; (3) Criterion C — the disturbance is not better explained by another mental disorder (schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder); (4) Criterion D — the disturbance does not occur exclusively during delirium; (5) Criterion E — causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. Common medical conditions that can cause psychosis include: neurological conditions (brain tumors, epilepsy especially temporal lobe, head trauma/TBI, Huntington's disease, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke/cerebrovascular disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus, Wilson's disease); infections (encephalitis, neurosyphilis, HIV/AIDS, meningitis, Lyme disease); autoimmune disorders (systemic lupus erythematosus with CNS involvement, NMDA receptor encephalitis, Hashimoto's encephalopathy); endocrine disorders (thyroid dysfunction — both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, Addison's disease, hyperparathyroidism); metabolic disorders (hepatic encephalopathy, uremic encephalopathy, hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbances); vitamin deficiencies (B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, thiamine deficiency/Wernicke-Korsakoff); and genetic disorders (porphyria, Niemann-Pick disease, metachromatic leukodystrophy). The hallmark is the identified organic cause: the psychosis is caused by a medical condition, not by an endogenous psychiatric process. However, the distinction is not always clean: some medical conditions produce persistent psychosis that may outlast the acute medical episode, particularly if there has been structural brain change or ongoing neurodegeneration, and the cognitive distortions layered on top of the medical etiology are the primary treatment target for CBTp.

The "The-Medical-Condition-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis" Distortion (etiology-dismissal / medical-condition-as-not-real-gating)

Distortion: "The medical condition caused it, so it is not real psychosis. Real psychosis comes from within the mind — mine was caused by a brain tumor/epilepsy/thyroid problem, so it is just a medical symptom, not a real psychotic episode. Because it was medically caused, it is not a mental health problem, and I do not need psychiatric or psychological treatment."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-Medical-Condition-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis. The The-Medical-Condition-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis (the medical condition caused it, so it is not real psychosis) was an etiology-dismissal and a medical-condition-as-not-real-gating: the mind treats the medical etiology as evidence the psychosis is not real, and because real psychosis is assumed to be endogenous (from within the mind), the inference is that organic (medically-caused) psychosis is not a real psychotic episode. The etiology-dismissal (medically-caused = not real) is the distortion: psychotic disorder due to another medical condition is a recognized DSM-5 diagnosis (293.89) whose defining feature is that prominent hallucinations or delusions are caused by another medical condition — the medical etiology is the diagnostic criterion, not evidence against the reality of the symptoms. The medical-condition-as-not-real-gating (organic effect = not psychosis) is the distortion: the hallucinations and delusions are real symptoms regardless of whether the cause is endogenous or organic — the person experiences them as real, they cause real distress and impairment, and they meet Criterion A (prominent hallucinations or delusions). CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that medically-caused psychosis is not real versus the evidence that the hallucinations and delusions are experienced as real and cause functional impairment), challenging the etiology-dismissal (the medical condition caused the symptoms, but the conclusion that they are not real is the distortion), and validating the reality (the hallucinations and delusions are real symptoms, and the medical etiology does not make them less real or less distressing) — so the mind learns that the etiology is a feature of the diagnosis, not evidence against the symptoms. The medical condition caused the symptoms; the conclusion that they are not real is the distortion.

The treatment-certainty (medical treatment = resolution) is the distortion: while psychosis due to a medical condition often improves or resolves after the underlying medical condition is treated, this is not guaranteed — some cases persist for weeks, months, or longer after medical treatment (particularly with brain tumors, head trauma, or chronic neurodegenerative conditions like Huntington's or Parkinson's, where structural brain change may produce lasting psychosis), and a subset of cases do not fully resolve and may represent lasting neurological change or a medical-condition-triggered underlying psychotic disorder. Is CBTp evidence-based for psychotic disorder due to another medical condition?

Yes. CBT for psychosis (CBTp) is evidence-based for psychotic disorder due to another medical condition. NICE guidelines recommend CBTp for all people with psychosis, including psychosis due to a medical condition, as a first-line adjunct to medication. The approach is particularly valuable after the acute medical episode has been addressed, when the person has the cognitive resources to engage in attribution work, meaning-making, and integrated coping. The If-I-Treat-The-Medical-Condition-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop distortion defers this work until after medical treatment; the evidence shows that CBTp engagement during and after the acute period improves outcomes regardless of whether the symptoms resolve quickly or persist. The identified organic cause makes CBTp particularly suitable: the cognitive distortions layered on top of the medical etiology (the etiology-dismissal, the treatment-certainty, the category-split, the medical-reductionism, the symptom-minimization, the conflation) are the primary treatment target, and addressing them improves the outcome whether the symptoms resolve with medical treatment or persist and require ongoing psychiatric treatment.

What happens in a CBTp session for psychotic disorder due to another medical condition?

A CBTp session for psychotic disorder due to another medical condition involves: (1) Engagement and assessment — the therapist builds a therapeutic relationship, assesses the current symptoms, the medical condition involved, the timeline of symptom onset relative to the medical condition, the functional impact, and the meaning the person has made of the medical etiology. (2) Formulation — the therapist builds a personalized model of the medical-condition-symptom relationship, the attribution patterns, and the cognitive distortions layered on top (the The-Medical-Condition-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis, the If-I-Treat-The-Medical-Condition-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop, etc.). (3) Psychoeducation about medically-caused psychosis — helping the person understand that the symptoms are real regardless of the cause, that medical treatment is necessary but not always sufficient, and that the medical condition and the psychosis are related but distinct. (4) Cognitive restructuring — targeting the specific distortions (the etiology-dismissal, the treatment-certainty, the category-split, the medical-reductionism, the symptom-minimization, the conflation). (5) Integrated coping skills — coping with persisting psychotic symptoms, managing the medical condition, and building skills that address both problems together. (6) Relapse-prevention planning — identifying early warning signs, developing a plan for medical condition changes or recurrences, and building skills that support recovery. CBTp for psychotic disorder due to another medical condition is typically 8-16 sessions, delivered by a therapist trained in CBTp, often in collaboration with the medical team treating the underlying condition. The approach is collaborative and integrated — the goal is to support engagement, address the meaning made of the cause, and build coping skills that improve the outcome whether the symptoms resolve with medical treatment or persist.

Why does psychotic disorder due to another medical condition need CBTp instead of just treating the medical condition?

Treating the medical condition (medical treatment) is the necessary first step and can reduce the psychotic symptoms, but it does not address the cognitive distortions that maintain the meaning made of the medical etiology and the attribution of symptoms. The The-Medical-Condition-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis, the If-I-Treat-The-Medical-Condition-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop, the My-Psychosis-Is-Only-A-Medical-Problem-Not-A-Mental-Health-Problem, the The-Medical-Condition-Explains-Everything-So-I-Do-Not-Need-Psychiatric-Care, the My-Psychosis-Is-Just-A-Symptom-Not-A-Real-Condition, and the My-Medical-Condition-And-My-Psychosis-Are-The-Same-Thing are thought patterns that medical treatment does not directly target — they require cognitive restructuring. CBTp addresses these distortions by examining evidence, building integrated coping skills, and supporting psychiatric treatment engagement. Additionally, the If-I-Treat-The-Medical-Condition-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop can itself delay psychiatric treatment engagement past the point where symptoms are persisting — medical treatment alone does not address this deferral pattern. The persisting symptoms (which can last weeks to months after medical treatment, particularly with structural brain change or chronic neurodegeneration) require coping skills that medical treatment does not provide. The relationship between the medical condition and psychotic symptoms is often bidirectional (the psychosis may cause stress that worsens the medical condition) — medical treatment alone does not address this interaction. The integrated approach (medical treatment for the cause + CBTp for the meaning, the attribution, and the integrated coping + psychiatric medication for the symptoms) produces better outcomes than medical treatment alone: medical treatment removes the precipitant, CBTp addresses the meaning and the coping, psychiatric medication addresses the symptoms, and the combination improves the outcome whether the symptoms resolve quickly or persist.
