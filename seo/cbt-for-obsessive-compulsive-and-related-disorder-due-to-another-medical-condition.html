Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 294.9) is an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder characterized by prominent obsessions or compulsions that are the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition. The OCD-spectrum symptoms (obsessions, compulsions, body-focused repetitive behaviors, hoarding, ordering, checking) are caused by the physiological effects of a medical condition — not by a primary mental disorder, not by a substance or medication (that would be DSM-5 292.9), and not during delirium. Common medical conditions that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms include brain lesions (tumors, especially frontal lobe and basal ganglia tumors), autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, anti-basal ganglia encephalitis), PANDAS/PANS (Pediatric Acute-onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome — sudden-onset obsessions/compulsions in children following streptococcal infection), traumatic brain injury (especially frontal lobe and basal ganglia injury), carbon monoxide poisoning (delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae with basal ganglia damage), Sydenham chorea (post-streptococcal autoimmune basal ganglia disorder), stroke (basal ganglia and frontal lobe infarcts), multiple sclerosis, Huntington's disease, and epilepsy. The PRIMARY treatment is treating the underlying medical condition (e.g., immunotherapy for autoimmune encephalitis, surgical resection for brain tumors, antibiotics for PANDAS/PANS, hyperbaric oxygen for carbon monoxide poisoning); CBT (including ERP) is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment for residual obsessions/compulsions, adherence, and relapse prevention.

What is Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 294.9) is an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder where the obsessions or compulsions are the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition. The DSM-5 criteria require: (a) prominent obsessions or compulsions; (b) there is evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the disturbance is the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition; (c) the disturbance is not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., primary OCD 300.3, where the symptoms are not attributable to a medical condition); (d) the disturbance does not occur exclusively during delirium; (e) the disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. The critical feature is the causal medical condition link: the obsessions/compulsions are not coincidental with a medical condition but are PRODUCED BY the medical condition through a known physiological mechanism. For example, in autoimmune encephalitis, antibodies attack brain tissue (especially basal ganglia and limbic structures) producing obsessions, compulsions, and other psychiatric symptoms — the OCD-spectrum symptoms are the physiological consequence of the autoimmune brain inflammation. In PANDAS/PANS, a streptococcal infection triggers autoimmune cross-reactivity with basal ganglia tissue, producing sudden-onset obsessions and compulsions in a child — the OCD-spectrum symptoms are the physiological consequence of the post-streptococcal autoimmune process. In a frontal lobe or basal ganglia brain tumor, the lesion directly disrupts the neural circuits that generate and regulate obsessions and compulsions — the OCD-spectrum symptoms are the physiological consequence of the structural brain pathology. In carbon monoxide poisoning, hypoxic injury to the basal ganglia (especially the globus pallidus) produces delayed-onset OCD-spectrum symptoms — the obsessions/compulsions are the physiological consequence of the hypoxic brain damage. The disturbance is distinct from Substance/Medication-Induced OCD (292.9): in 294.9 the cause is a medical condition, in 292.9 the cause is a substance or medication. The disturbance is distinct from primary OCD (300.3): in 294.9 the symptoms are attributable to a medical condition, in 300.3 they are not.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

CBT for Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 294.9) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the medical-etiology-dismissal and condition-fatalism distortions that block identification of the causative condition and treatment engagement, the monocausal-reductionism and treatment-hopelessness distortions that dismiss CBT and block psychological treatment, the dichotomous-attribution and brokenness-fusion distortions that block integrated care and recovery identity. CBT with medical-psychological integration is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment that addresses residual obsessions/compulsions, adherence, and relapse prevention alongside medical treatment of the underlying condition.

1. Psychoeducation, Medical Condition-OCD Link Education, Medical Treatment Engagement & Symptom Monitoring

The foundation of CBT for Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition is understanding the medical condition-OCD link, identifying the cognitive distortions that maintain the obsessions/compulsions and interfere with medical treatment adherence, ensuring the causative medical condition is being treated, and learning symptom monitoring skills. Psychoeducation: learn the DSM-5 criteria (prominent obsessions/compulsions, direct physiological consequence of a medical condition, not better explained by another mental disorder, not during delirium, clinically significant impairment), the common medical conditions that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms (brain lesions, autoimmune encephalitis, PANDAS/PANS, traumatic brain injury, carbon monoxide poisoning, Sydenham chorea, stroke, multiple sclerosis, Huntington's disease, epilepsy), the neural circuits involved (basal ganglia, frontal lobe, limbic system — the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit that is implicated in primary OCD), the treatment sequence (medical condition treated FIRST, CBT as adjunct), and the good prognosis with integrated care. Medical condition-OCD link education is the cornerstone: many people with medically-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms have never understood that their obsessions and compulsions are the DIRECT physiological consequence of a medical condition — the It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition distortion frames the symptoms as primary OCD, the My-OCD-Is-All-Medical-Or-All-Psychological distortion splits OCD dichotomously, and the Treating-The-Medical-Condition-Is-Enough distortion dismisses the psychological treatment that addresses residual symptoms. CBT helps the person understand the specific mechanism by which the medical condition produces OCD-spectrum symptoms (e.g., autoimmune encephalitis produces antibodies that attack basal ganglia tissue disrupting the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit; PANDAS/PANS produces autoimmune cross-reactivity between streptococcal antibodies and basal ganglia neurons; a frontal lobe tumor directly compresses or disrupts the neural tissue generating obsessions; carbon monoxide poisoning produces hypoxic injury to the globus pallidus disrupting basal ganglia output). Medical treatment engagement: ensure the underlying medical condition is being evaluated and treated by the appropriate medical specialist (neurology, immunology, infectious disease, neurosurgery, neuropsychiatry), attend medical appointments, adhere to medical treatment (immunotherapy, antibiotics, antiepileptics, surgery, disease-modifying therapy), and communicate symptoms to the medical team. Symptom monitoring: track obsession intensity, compulsion frequency, medical condition status, and the correlation between the two — this data informs both the medical treatment and the CBT.

2. Cognitive Restructuring of Medical-Etiology-Dismissal, Condition-Fatalism, Monocausal-Reductionism, Treatment-Hopelessness, Dichotomous-Attribution & Brokenness-Fusion Distortions + ERP for Residual Compulsions

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain the condition. Keep an OCD-medical condition belief log: each day, write down the distressing thoughts (it is just the medical condition, my OCD is permanent because my condition is, treating the medical condition is enough, the OCD will never improve, my OCD is all medical or all psychological, I am broken at the root), identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the obsessions/compulsions are the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition requiring identification of the causative condition — the symptoms have a physiological mechanism, they correlate with the medical condition, and they require treating the causative condition; CBT reduces residual OCD-spectrum symptoms even when the medical condition is ongoing or chronic — the nervous system sensitization and learned OCD patterns respond to CBT even when the medical condition persists; residual obsessions/compulsions and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment — the symptoms that persist after the medical condition is treated or stabilized, the distortions that interfere with medical treatment adherence, and the relapse symptoms when the medical condition fluctuates all require CBT; CBT produces meaningful improvement in medically-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms — cognitive restructuring reduces symptom amplification, ERP reduces residual compulsions, and adherence improves outcomes; obsessions/compulsions are produced by both physiological and psychological factors requiring integrated care — the medical condition produces the physiological OCD-spectrum symptoms, the psychological interpretation amplifies them, and both must be addressed; the medical condition is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person — the condition is treatable or manageable, the person is not defective, and the identity is separate from the condition), identify the reframe, and practice the reframe in daily life. ERP for residual compulsions: many people with medically-induced OCD have residual compulsions that persist after the medical condition is treated or stabilized — these compulsions have become learned responses that ERP can extinguish. Build an ERP hierarchy (trigger situations ranked by SUDS 0-100), practice exposure (in vivo and imaginal) while refraining from the compulsion, track habituation, and generalize the learning. Medical-psychological integration: recognize that the OCD-spectrum symptoms are produced by BOTH the medical condition (physiological) AND the psychological interpretation (amplification), and that integrated care (medical treatment + CBT) outperforms either alone.

3. Behavioral Regulation, Medical Treatment Adherence, ERP Practice & Relapse Prevention

Behavioral regulation: establish regular sleep (sleep deprivation amplifies obsessions and worsens medical condition symptoms), regular meals (irregular eating destabilizes blood sugar and triggers anxiety), regular activity (moderate exercise supports both medical recovery and OCD symptom reduction), and stress reduction (stress amplifies both the medical condition and the OCD-spectrum symptoms). Medical treatment adherence: if the OCD-spectrum symptoms are caused by a medical condition, engage the appropriate medical treatment — immunotherapy for autoimmune encephalitis (corticosteroids, IVIG, plasma exchange, rituximab), antibiotics for PANDAS/PANS (penicillin, azithromycin, or prophylactic antibiotics to prevent streptococcal recurrence), surgical resection or radiation for brain tumors, hyperbaric oxygen for carbon monoxide poisoning, antiepileptics for epilepsy, disease-modifying therapy for multiple sclerosis. Attend all medical appointments, adhere to the treatment plan, track medical symptoms, and communicate OCD-spectrum symptom changes to the medical team. ERP practice for residual compulsions: between-session exposure homework, SUDS tracking, response prevention practice, and habituation monitoring. Relapse prevention plan: a written plan that includes the early warning signs of OCD-spectrum symptoms returning (increased obsession intensity, increased compulsion frequency, medical condition flare or progression, increased catastrophizing about symptoms), the action steps for each warning sign (return to CBT skills, contact clinician, resume ERP, contact medical specialist, review medical treatment), the triggers for relapse (medical condition progression, medical treatment changes, stress, sleep disruption, major life transitions), and a treatment contact plan. The relapse prevention plan is especially important for conditions that fluctuate (autoimmune encephalitis with relapsing-remitting course, PANDAS/PANS with streptococcal recurrence, multiple sclerosis with relapses) or progress (Huntington's disease, progressive neurodegenerative conditions).

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

Each distortion maintains the condition by blocking identification of the causative medical condition, dismissing CBT, blocking integrated care, or preventing recovery identity. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition (medical-etiology-dismissal / physiology-etiology-dismissal): "It is just my OCD. The medical condition is not the problem. My brain is fine. I have always had obsessions, the medical condition just makes them worse." The person dismisses the medical etiology, framing the OCD-spectrum symptoms as primary and blocking the investigation that would identify the causative medical condition.
Reframe: "The obsessions/compulsions are the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition requiring identification of the causative condition. The It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition distortion is a medical-etiology-dismissal: the person has dismissed the medical etiology, treating the OCD-spectrum symptoms as purely psychological when they are the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition. But the symptoms in this disorder have a known physiological mechanism — autoimmune encephalitis produces antibodies that attack basal ganglia tissue, PANDAS/PANS produces autoimmune cross-reactivity with basal ganglia neurons, a brain tumor directly disrupts the neural circuits generating obsessions, carbon monoxide poisoning produces hypoxic basal ganglia injury, Sydenham chorea produces autoimmune basal ganglia inflammation, traumatic brain injury damages frontal lobe and basal ganglia circuits. The symptoms correlate with the medical condition (onset follows or correlates with the condition, symptoms may improve as the condition is treated), they have a physiological mechanism (not just psychological), and they require treating the causative medical condition (treating the autoimmune encephalitis, resecting the brain tumor, antibiotic treatment for PANDAS/PANS reduces the OCD-spectrum symptoms). The distortion is dangerous because it blocks the identification of the causative medical condition — the person attributes the symptoms to primary OCD and never receives the neurological evaluation, the autoimmune panel, the brain imaging, or the streptococcal testing that the symptoms warrant. The antidote is medical condition-OCD link education: understand the specific mechanism by which medical conditions produce OCD-spectrum symptoms, recognize that the symptom pattern indicates medical etiology (especially sudden onset in a child — think PANDAS/PANS; new-onset obsessions with neurological signs — think autoimmune encephalitis or brain lesion), and pursue the medical evaluation that the symptoms warrant."
2. My-OCD-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Condition-Is (condition-fatalism / chronicity-transfer): "My medical condition is chronic, so my OCD is chronic. Nothing will help the obsessions because the condition is permanent. I am stuck with these compulsions forever because the condition is forever." The person fatalizes the OCD-spectrum symptoms as permanent by transferring the chronicity of the medical condition to the symptoms, blocking CBT engagement.
Reframe: "CBT reduces residual OCD-spectrum symptoms even when the medical condition is ongoing or chronic. The My-OCD-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Condition-Is distortion is a condition-fatalism: the person has transferred the chronicity of the medical condition to the OCD-spectrum symptoms, treating them as equally permanent. But even when the medical condition is chronic (Huntington's disease is progressive, multiple sclerosis is relapsing-remitting, traumatic brain injury produces lasting structural changes), the OCD-spectrum symptoms are NOT equally permanent — the symptoms have two components: (1) the direct physiological symptoms from the medical condition (which may persist or fluctuate with the condition), and (2) the residual symptoms from nervous system sensitization and learned OCD patterns (which are reduced by CBT and ERP). The residual symptoms respond to CBT even when the medical condition is ongoing — cognitive restructuring reduces the catastrophizing that amplifies symptoms, ERP reduces residual compulsions, and exposure to medical condition triggers reduces the OCD-spectrum symptoms about them. The distortion is harmful because it blocks CBT engagement — if the OCD is permanent, treatment is futile. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful OCD-spectrum symptom reduction in medically-induced OCD. The antidote is recognizing that the OCD-spectrum symptoms and the medical condition are not equally chronic — the medical condition may be ongoing, but the symptom component is treatable."
3. Treating-The-Medical-Condition-Is-Enough (monocausal-reductionism / psychological-treatment-dismissal): "I just need to treat the medical condition. Therapy is not for me. Treating the condition will solve everything. Once the condition is treated, the OCD will go away on its own." The person dismisses CBT as unnecessary, treating medical treatment as sufficient and blocking the psychological treatment that addresses residual symptoms and adherence.
Reframe: "Residual obsessions/compulsions and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment. The Treating-The-Medical-Condition-Is-Enough distortion is a monocausal-reductionism: the person has reduced the OCD-spectrum symptoms to a single cause (the medical condition) and dismissed the psychological treatment, treating medical treatment as sufficient. But three problems require CBT even after the medical condition is treated or stabilized: (1) residual OCD-spectrum symptoms persist — the nervous system has been sensitized, the obsessions/compulsions have become learned responses, and the symptom interpretation patterns amplify residual symptoms; (2) adherence-interfering distortions — the It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition blocks identification of the causative condition, the I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root produces avoidance of treatment settings, and these distortions interfere with the ongoing medical treatment; (3) relapse symptoms — when the medical condition fluctuates (autoimmune relapse, PANDAS/PANS streptococcal recurrence, MS relapse) or progresses, the OCD-spectrum symptoms flare with it, and without CBT and ERP skills the person catastrophizes the flare, the symptoms amplify, and the recovery is slower. Integrated care (medical treatment + CBT) outperforms medical-only care for OCD-spectrum symptoms — the CBT addresses the psychological factors that medical treatment cannot reach. The antidote is recognizing that treating the medical condition is necessary but not sufficient — the OCD-spectrum symptoms have both physiological and psychological components, and both require treatment."
4. The-OCD-Will-Never-Improve (treatment-hopelessness / treatment-resistance-fatalism): "Nothing works for my OCD. I have tried everything. My case is different. The obsessions will never get better because they are caused by a medical condition." The person fatalizes the OCD-spectrum symptoms as treatment-resistant, using the medical etiology as evidence that psychological treatment cannot help.
Reframe: "CBT produces meaningful improvement in medically-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms. The The-OCD-Will-Never-Improve distortion is a treatment-hopelessness: the person has fatalized the OCD-spectrum symptoms as treatment-resistant, using the medical etiology as evidence that CBT cannot help. But CBT produces meaningful improvement through four mechanisms: (1) cognitive restructuring reduces the symptom amplification that the catastrophizing produces — even when the medical condition causes the physiological symptoms, the person's interpretation of the symptoms amplifies and prolongs them, and restructuring reduces the amplification; (2) ERP reduces residual compulsions that persist after the medical condition is treated or stabilized — exposure and response prevention breaks the compulsion cycle; (3) medical treatment adherence improves — the distortions that interfere with adherence are restructured, and better adherence reduces the physiological symptoms; (4) relapse prevention skills reduce the impact of medical condition fluctuations or progression. The distortion is a self-fulfilling prophecy — if the OCD will never improve, the person does not engage with CBT, and the symptoms do not improve. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful OCD-spectrum symptom reduction in medically-induced OCD. The antidote is recognizing that the medical etiology does not make the symptoms treatment-resistant — the psychological components respond to psychological treatment."
5. My-OCD-Is-All-Medical-Or-All-Psychological (dichotomous-attribution / medical-psychological-split): "My OCD is either medical or mental, not both. Either it is the condition or it is me. I need to figure out which is which and treat them separately." The person splits OCD dichotomously into all-medical or all-psychological, blocking the integrated care that addresses both.
Reframe: "Obsessions/compulsions are produced by both physiological and psychological factors requiring integrated care. The My-OCD-Is-All-Medical-Or-All-Psychological distortion is a dichotomous-attribution: the person has split OCD-spectrum symptoms into mutually exclusive categories (medical OR psychological), blocking the recognition that the symptoms are produced by BOTH. But the OCD-spectrum symptoms in OCD Due to Another Medical Condition are inherently both — the medical condition produces the physiological symptoms (obsessions, compulsions, repetitive behaviors through basal ganglia, frontal lobe, or limbic system pathology), AND the psychological interpretation amplifies them (catastrophizing about the obsessions increases their severity and duration). The obsession is medical (the autoimmune encephalitis produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation 'this obsession means I am dangerous' amplifies the anxiety about it). The compulsion is medical (the basal ganglia pathology produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation 'I must perform this compulsion' amplifies the drive). The dichotomous split is harmful because it blocks integrated care — if OCD is all-medical, only the neurologist or immunologist addresses it; if all-psychological, only the CBT clinician addresses it. But the symptoms require BOTH — the medical specialist addresses the physiological symptoms, and the CBT clinician addresses the psychological interpretation that amplifies them. Integrated care outperforms either alone. The antidote is recognizing that OCD-spectrum symptoms are produced by both factors simultaneously."
6. I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root (brokenness-fusion / medical-condition-as-identity): "I am defective. My brain is broken. I am damaged goods. The medical condition means I am a broken person, not a person with a medical condition." The person fuses the medical condition with personal brokenness, treating a medical event as evidence of fundamental defectiveness.
Reframe: "The medical condition is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person. The I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root distortion is a brokenness-fusion: the person has fused the medical condition with their identity, treating the condition as evidence of fundamental brokenness rather than a medical event that happened to a person. But a medical condition is a medical event — autoimmune encephalitis is an autoimmune disease, PANDAS/PANS is a post-infectious autoimmune process, a brain tumor is a neoplasm, traumatic brain injury is an injury, carbon monoxide poisoning is a toxic exposure, Sydenham chorea is a post-infectious movement disorder; none of these are character defects or evidence of personal brokenness. The person is a person who has a medical condition, not a broken person. The condition is treatable or manageable (immunotherapy manages autoimmune encephalitis, antibiotics and prophylaxis manage PANDAS/PANS, surgery and radiation manage brain tumors, rehabilitation manages TBI, hyperbaric oxygen manages carbon monoxide poisoning), the person is not defective (having a medical condition is a human experience, not a defect), and the identity is separate from the condition. The distortion is harmful because it blocks recovery — if the person is broken at the root, treatment is futile, treatment settings are avoided, and the identity collapses into the condition. But the evidence shows that people with medically-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms recover, manage, and live full lives — the condition is a medical event, not an identity. The antidote is recognizing that the condition is a medical event that happened to a person, that the person is not the condition, and that treatment addresses the condition while the person remains a whole person."

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition requires coordinated care from both a medical specialist (neurologist, immunologist, infectious disease specialist, neurosurgeon, or neuropsychiatrist for the underlying medical condition) and a CBT-trained clinician (for the OCD-spectrum symptoms), especially when the obsessions/compulsions are severe or the medical condition is complex or progressive. Seek help if: the OCD-spectrum symptoms have sudden onset (especially in a child — sudden-onset obsessions/compulsions may indicate PANDAS/PANS and warrant streptococcal testing and neurological evaluation); the obsessions/compulsions are accompanied by neurological symptoms (movement abnormalities, cognitive changes, seizures, weakness, sensory changes — these may indicate a brain lesion, autoimmune encephalitis, or neurodegenerative condition and warrant urgent neurological evaluation and brain imaging); you have a known medical condition (autoimmune disease, brain injury, history of carbon monoxide exposure, streptococcal infection, stroke, multiple sclerosis) and new-onset or worsening obsessions/compulsions; you have OCD-spectrum symptoms and have NOT had a medical evaluation (the It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition distortion may be blocking the investigation that would identify a causative medical condition); the obsessions/compulsions are producing significant distress or interfering with daily functioning; the cognitive distortions are interfering with medical treatment adherence; you have treated the medical condition but residual OCD-spectrum symptoms persist (the residual symptoms require CBT and ERP — the Treating-The-Medical-Condition-Is-Enough distortion may be blocking the psychological treatment); the OCD-spectrum symptoms are producing secondary problems (depression, relationship strain, occupational impairment); or you have thoughts of self-harm or suicide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is OCD Due to Another Medical Condition different from primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder?

Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 294.9) and primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) both produce prominent obsessions and compulsions, but the etiology differs fundamentally. In OCD Due to Another Medical Condition, the obsessions/compulsions are the DIRECT PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCE of a medical condition — the medical condition (e.g., autoimmune encephalitis, brain tumor, PANDAS/PANS) is PRODUCING the OCD-spectrum symptoms through a known physiological mechanism (basal ganglia, frontal lobe, or limbic system pathology disrupting the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit). The symptoms are a physiological consequence of the medical condition. In primary OCD, the obsessions and compulsions are not attributable to the physiological effects of a medical condition. The treatments differ: OCD Due to Another Medical Condition requires treating the medical condition first (e.g., immunotherapy for autoimmune encephalitis, surgery for brain tumor, antibiotics for PANDAS/PANS) with CBT (including ERP) as adjunct; primary OCD is treated primarily with CBT with ERP and/or SSRIs without medical treatment as the primary intervention.

What medical conditions most commonly cause OCD-spectrum symptoms?

The medical conditions that most commonly produce obsessions or compulsions (Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition, DSM-5 294.9) involve pathology of the basal ganglia, frontal lobe, or limbic system — the neural circuits implicated in OCD. Common conditions include: brain lesions (tumors, especially frontal lobe and basal ganglia tumors), autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, anti-basal ganglia encephalitis — antibodies attack brain tissue producing obsessions and compulsions), PANDAS/PANS (Pediatric Acute-onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome — sudden onset of obsessions and compulsions in children following streptococcal infection, autoimmune basal ganglia cross-reactivity), traumatic brain injury (especially frontal lobe and basal ganglia injury), carbon monoxide poisoning (delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae with basal ganglia damage), Sydenham chorea (post-streptococcal autoimmune basal ganglia disorder), stroke (basal ganglia and frontal lobe infarcts), multiple sclerosis (basal ganglia demyelination), Huntington's disease (basal ganglia degeneration), and epilepsy (especially temporal lobe and frontal lobe). The key diagnostic feature is that the OCD-spectrum symptoms correlate with the medical condition and have a known physiological mechanism.

If the OCD-spectrum symptoms are caused by a medical condition, do I still need CBT?

Yes, CBT is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment for Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition, even though the medical condition is the primary cause. Three reasons. First, residual OCD-spectrum symptoms often PERSIST after the medical condition is treated or stabilized — the nervous system has been sensitized, the obsessions/compulsions have become learned responses, and the symptom interpretation patterns amplify residual symptoms. CBT and ERP extinguish the residual through cognitive restructuring and exposure. Second, the cognitive distortions interfere with MEDICAL TREATMENT ADHERENCE — the person does not attend medical appointments, does not take medication consistently, does not follow through with rehabilitation. CBT corrects these distortions, improving adherence and outcomes. Third, medical conditions FLUCTUATE and PROGRESS, and the OCD-spectrum symptoms flare with each change. CBT provides relapse prevention skills that reduce the symptom duration and intensity of each flare. Integrated care (medical treatment + CBT) outperforms medical-only care for OCD-spectrum symptoms.

What is PANDAS/PANS and how does it cause OCD-spectrum symptoms?

PANDAS (Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal infections) and PANS (Pediatric Acute-onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome) are conditions in which a child suddenly develops obsessions, compulsions, and other neuropsychiatric symptoms following an infection. In PANDAS, the trigger is a Group A streptococcal infection (strep throat); in PANS, the trigger may be other infections or inflammatory processes. The proposed mechanism is autoimmune cross-reactivity: antibodies produced against the streptococcal bacteria cross-react with basal ganglia tissue (molecular mimicry), producing autoimmune inflammation in the basal ganglia that drives the sudden-onset obsessions and compulsions. The hallmark is SUDDEN onset (overnight or within 24-48 hours) of severe OCD-spectrum symptoms in a child who previously had no or minimal OCD symptoms, often accompanied by other symptoms (tics, emotional lability, separation anxiety, behavioral regression, sensory sensitivities, urinary frequency). Treatment includes antibiotics (to treat the streptococcal infection and prevent recurrence), and in severe cases immunotherapy (IVIG, plasma exchange, corticosteroids). CBT with ERP is an adjunctive treatment for the OCD-spectrum symptoms. The It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition distortion is especially dangerous in PANDAS/PANS because it frames the sudden-onset obsessions as primary childhood OCD and blocks the streptococcal testing and antibiotic treatment that could address the autoimmune trigger.

Can I have both OCD Due to Another Medical Condition AND primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder?

Yes. A person can have both Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 294.9) AND primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3). For example, a person with traumatic brain injury may have OCD-spectrum symptoms directly caused by the brain injury (the 294.9 diagnosis) AND additional obsessions and compulsions that are not attributable to the brain injury (the primary OCD diagnosis). The DSM-5 allows both diagnoses when each set of symptoms meets full criteria and is not better explained by the other. The treatment addresses both: the medical condition is treated (reducing the physiologically-caused symptoms), the primary OCD is treated with CBT with ERP and/or SSRIs (reducing the primary OCD), and the integrated plan coordinates the medical treatment and psychological treatment.
