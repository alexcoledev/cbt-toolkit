Cognitive Behavioral Therapy is an evidence-based treatment for HPD, with growing evidence that it reduces attention-seeking behavior, improves emotion regulation, increases the capacity for authentic (non-performative) connection, and improves interpersonal functioning. The core maintaining mechanisms are three: attention-seeking with external-worth (the self is experienced as real only insofar as it is the center of attention, which makes self-esteem entirely contingent on being noticed and produces the constant performance and the collapse when the attention is withdrawn), impression-management with shallow-affect (the self is curated presentation rather than substance, which produces the rapidly shifting relationships, the theatrical emotional expression, and the inability to sustain intimacy that requires depth), and emotionality-as-identity with drama-dependence (feelings must be expressed dramatically to be real, which produces the exaggerated emotional displays, the rapid emotional shifts, and the experience of ordinary expression as inauthentic or inadequate). CBT works by restructuring the attention-seeking core beliefs through Socratic questioning and evidence examination, by training emotion regulation through the capacity to feel and express at appropriate intensity, and by building authentic-connection capacity through non-performative intimacy exercises. HPD is a real and treatable condition — not a fixed character, not a permanent theatricality, not a life sentence of shallow relationships. The suffering is real (both the suffering caused and the suffering of maintaining the performance); the conclusions that you are only real when noticed, that feelings must be dramatic to be valid, and that relationships are stages are the distortions. The CBT for Histrionic Personality Disorder Protocol

CBT adapted for HPD is not a single technique but a 3-component protocol designed for the attention-seeking, the impression-management, and the emotionality-as-identity presentation. Each component addresses a specific mechanism that maintains the disorder. The components work together — cognitive restructuring without emotion regulation leaves the theatrical expression intact, emotion regulation without authentic-connection training leaves the shallow relationships intact, and authentic-connection training without the other two leaves the person able to be present but still fused to the attention-seeking core-belief.

Component 1: Cognitive Restructuring

Identify and challenge the attention-seeking core beliefs that structure the HPD experience — the deep, often pre-verbal beliefs about the self, others, and the world. The primary core beliefs are: I must be the center of attention (the self is real only when noticed), my worth depends on being noticed (self-esteem is external and contingent on attention), I am as fascinating as I appear (the self is presentation rather than substance), and my feelings must be expressed dramatically (ordinary expression is inadequate). Examine the evidence through Socratic questioning: what is the evidence that you are only real when noticed vs. that you are real regardless of attention, what is the evidence that your worth depends on being the center vs. that worth is an internal property that holds across attention and inattention, what is the evidence that the presentation is the self vs. that the self has substance behind the presentation. The key technique is the core-belief log: track situations that activate the attention-seeking core-belief, identify the belief, examine the evidence for and against, and build an alternative belief that is more accurate and more functional. The distortion here is the attention-seeking, the external-worth, and the impression-management; the reframe is that the self is real when not the center, that worth is internal and stable, and that substance exists behind the presentation.

Component 2: Emotion Regulation

The emotionality in HPD is not typically a complete inability to feel but a deficit in feeling and expressing at appropriate intensity, with emotions experienced as real only when expressed dramatically. Emotion regulation training builds the capacity to feel at the actual intensity of the feeling, to express at an intensity that matches the feeling rather than amplifying it for an audience, and to tolerate the experience of ordinary expression as valid. Three sub-skills: emotion awareness (the capacity to identify the actual intensity of a feeling, distinguishing the felt intensity from the performed intensity), intensity matching (the capacity to express at the intensity that matches the feeling, including the capacity to express quietly and understatedly without the expression feeling inauthentic), and distress tolerance for understatement (the capacity to sit with the discomfort of expressing at ordinary intensity, including the discomfort of not being the center of the emotional display). The key techniques are emotion logging (track the actual intensity of feelings separate from the performed intensity), the quiet-expression experiment (deliberately practice expressing at lower intensity and observe that the feeling does not become less real), and the audience-free feeling exercise (practice feeling without any audience, building the capacity for private emotional experience). This directly targets the emotionality-as-identity and the drama-dependence distortions: the feeling is real at its actual intensity, not only when performed dramatically. The distortion here is the emotionality-as-identity and the drama-dependence; the reframe is that feelings are real at their actual intensity and that ordinary expression is valid.

Component 3: Authentic-Connection Training

Build the capacity for non-performative intimacy, directly countering the relationships-exist-to-admire-me others-as-audience and the impression-management with shallow-affect. Three sub-skills: substance development (the capacity to cultivate interests, values, and qualities that exist independent of presentation, building the self as substance rather than as curated impression), non-performative presence (the capacity to be present with another person without performing, including the capacity to tolerate the discomfort of being known rather than merely watched), and intimacy depth (the capacity to sustain relationships that require depth, including the capacity to share at a level that cannot be performed and to receive another person at a level that cannot be watched). The key techniques are the substance inventory (identify interests, values, and qualities that exist independent of whether anyone is watching), the non-performative conversation exercise (engage in conversation with the explicit goal of being known rather than being fascinating, observing the discomfort and building the capacity for it), and the intimacy ladder (progressively deeper sharing with a trusted person, building the capacity for intimacy that does not require an audience). The key is to build the capacity to hold the self as substance that can be known, which is the space where genuine intimacy becomes possible. The distortion here is the others-as-audience and the impression-management; the reframe is that relationships are mutual, that others are partners rather than spectators, and that the self has substance that can be shared.

Why Histrionic Personality Disorder Needs Specific CBT

General anxiety tools say "reduce your worry." General depression tools say "challenge your negative thoughts." Neither captures HPD: the i-must-be-the-center-of-attention attention-seeking produces a specific center-as-existence distortion that general social anxiety tools conflate with ordinary extroversion, the my-worth-depends-on-being-noticed external-worth produces a specific self-esteem-as-attention distortion that general self-esteem tools cannot address because the contingency on being the center is structurally different from ordinary low self-esteem, the i-am-as-fascinating-as-i-appear impression-management produces a specific self-as-presentation distortion that general authenticity tools conflate with ordinary impression management, the if-i-am-not-the-center-i-am-nothing center-dependence produces a specific self-as-performance distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the existential collapse without an audience is not ordinary social discomfort, the my-feelings-must-be-expressed-dramatically emotionality-as-identity produces a specific drama-as-validity distortion that general emotion regulation tools conflate with ordinary expressiveness, and the relationships-exist-to-admire-me others-as-audience produces a specific relationship-as-stage distortion that general relationship tools cannot address because the conversion of relationships into performance venues is not ordinary social difficulty. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT for HPD can target directly:

The "I Must Be The Center Of Attention" Attention-Seeking

Distortion: "I must be the center of attention. If I am not the center, I am not real. Being an ordinary participant is being invisible, and being invisible is being nothing. The room must be watching me for me to exist. When the conversation moves on, when someone else becomes the center, when I am not the focus, I am annihilated — not metaphorically, but as a felt experience of ceasing to be. I must make myself the center by whatever means: by being dramatic, by being fascinating, by being seductive, by being provocative, by being intense. If I cannot be the center through charm, I will be the center through crisis. Attention is not a preference, it is a necessity — the substance of the self, without which there is only the void where I should be. I cannot tolerate being overlooked, because being overlooked is nonexistence."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that I am real regardless of whether I am the center of attention, not real only when noticed, through the attention-seeking lens. The attention-seeking (I must be the center) treats the desire to be seen as the necessity of being seen, when the reality is that one can be a valuable participant without being the center and that ordinary participation is not invisibility. The all-or-nothing (either I am the center or I am nothing) treats the self as binary, when the reality is that the self is constituted by many qualities that exist regardless of whether anyone is currently watching. The external-worth (my value is determined by whether eyes are on me) treats worth as a function of attention, when the reality is that worth is an internal property that does not depend on being noticed. CBT helps by examining the evidence for and against the attention-seeking core-belief, by tracing it to its origin (often early experiences where attention was the only available form of validation or where being overlooked was experienced as nonexistence), by distinguishing genuine connection from performance, and by building the capacity to hold the self as real when not the center. The desire to be seen is real; the conclusion that being unseen is being nothing is the distortion."

The "My Worth Depends On Being Noticed" External-Worth

Distortion: "My worth is not an internal property; it is a reading on the room's focus. When I am the center, I am worth something. When I am not the center, I am worth nothing. When the attention moves to someone else, my value moves with it. I cannot feel good about myself without being noticed, because the good feeling is the attention, not a response to it. I cannot hold my value across being overlooked, because being overlooked is the value. I cannot rest in my worth when no one is watching, because the worth is the watching. The self is a performance that requires an audience; without the audience there is no performer. I must constantly seek attention because the attention is the self."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that worth is an internal property that holds across attention and inattention, not a reading on the room's focus, through the external-worth lens. The external-worth (my value is determined by whether I am noticed) treats worth as a function of attention, when the reality is that worth is an internal property that does not depend on external focus and that holds across both being the center and being an ordinary participant. The center-dependence (without attention I am nothing) treats the self as a performance that requires an audience, when the reality is that the self is a substantive entity that exists and has value regardless of whether anyone is currently watching. The all-or-nothing (either I am noticed or I am nothing) treats the self as binary, when the reality is that the self can be unnoticed in a moment and still be whole. CBT helps by building the capacity to hold worth as internal and stable, by practicing internal validation (recognizing one's own value independent of attention), and by building the capacity to tolerate the discomfort of not being noticed without the self collapsing. The desire to be noticed is real; the conclusion that worth is the attention is the distortion."

The "I Am As Fascinating As I Appear" Impression-Management

Distortion: "I am as fascinating as I appear to be. The presentation is the self. If I appear interesting, I am interesting. If I appear deep, I am deep. If I appear passionate, I am passionate. The curated impression is the real me, and the real me is the curated impression. Substance is secondary to style; what matters is the appearance of depth, the appearance of intensity, the appearance of being fascinating. I do not need to develop qualities; I need to display the appearance of qualities. I do not need to be interesting; I need to seem interesting. The gap between the presentation and the substance does not concern me, because the presentation is the substance. If I can maintain the impression, I have maintained the self."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that the self has substance independent of the presentation, not that the presentation is the self, through the impression-management lens. The impression-management (I am as fascinating as I appear) treats the presentation as the self, when the reality is that the self has qualities, interests, and values that exist independent of whether they are displayed and that the gap between presentation and substance is real and consequential. The shallow-affect (the appearance of depth is depth) treats the appearance as the reality, when the reality is that depth requires substance and that the appearance of depth without substance produces the inability to sustain intimacy. The all-or-nothing (either I am fascinating or I am nothing) treats the self as binary, when the reality is that the self has many qualities, some fascinating and some ordinary, and the ordinary ones do not negate the fascinating ones. CBT helps through substance development (the substance inventory, cultivating interests and values that exist independent of presentation) and non-performative presence (the capacity to be known rather than merely watched). The desire to be fascinating is real; the conclusion that the appearance is the self is the distortion."

When to See a Professional

These tools are self-help based on CBT for HPD principles, useful as a complement to therapy with a mental health professional, especially one trained in CBT for personality disorders. HPD can involve significant interpersonal impairment, depression when the attention is not forthcoming, and crises that warrant professional treatment. These tools are a self-help starting point, not a replacement for professional treatment. If you are experiencing suicidal ideation, severe depression, or any condition affecting your safety, please contact a licensed mental health professional or a crisis line immediately. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. In the UK, call 116 123 for Samaritans. NAMI (nami.org) provides mental health resources and support. Seeking help is a sign of good judgment, not a failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can CBT help with histrionic personality disorder?

Yes. CBT is an evidence-based treatment for HPD, with growing evidence that it reduces attention-seeking behavior, improves emotion regulation, increases the capacity for authentic (non-performative) connection, and improves interpersonal functioning. HPD produces specific cognitive distortions (attention-seeking, external-worth, impression-management, center-dependence, emotionality-as-identity, others-as-audience) that CBT for HPD targets directly through cognitive restructuring, emotion regulation, and authentic-connection training. HPD is a real and treatable condition, not a fixed character or permanent theatricality.

What is the difference between HPD and healthy expressiveness, NPD, or BPD?

Three differences: the structure of the self-esteem, the depth of affect, and the core fear. HPD (DSM-5 301.6) is characterized by a pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking, driven by a self-esteem that requires being the center of attention and a core fear of being unnoticed and irrelevant. Healthy expressiveness is stable across whether one is the center of attention; it coexists with the capacity for quiet, for substance, and for intimacy that does not require performance. NPD (DSM-5 301.81) is driven by a core fear of inadequacy and produces a stable grandiose front with lack of empathy; HPD is driven by a core fear of being unnoticed and produces a shifting theatrical presentation with shallow but intense affect. BPD (DSM-5 301.83) is driven by a core fear of abandonment and produces instability with intense dysphoria; HPD produces shallow rapidly-shifting emotions and attention-seeking without the same depth of dysphoria. About 1-3% of the population meets criteria for HPD, and it is treatable, particularly when the person recognizes the pattern and engages with evidence-based treatment.

I must be the center of attention. Can CBT help with the attention-seeking in HPD?

Yes, and the i-must-be-the-center-of-attention attention-seeking is one of the most maintaining cognitive distortions in HPD. The distortion is a core belief combined with all-or-nothing thinking and external locus of worth. The reality is that one can be a valuable participant without being the center; that ordinary participation is not invisibility; that the constant performance is a defense against the feared conclusion of irrelevance, which the person equates with nonexistence. CBT helps by examining the evidence for and against the attention-seeking core-belief, by tracing it to its origin (often early experiences where attention was the only available form of validation), by distinguishing genuine connection from performance, and by building the capacity to hold the self as real when not the center. The desire to be seen is real; the conclusion that being unseen is being nothing is the distortion.

My feelings must be expressed dramatically. Can CBT help with the emotionality in HPD?

Yes, and the my-feelings-must-be-expressed-dramatically emotionality-as-identity is a core feature of HPD that CBT targets directly through emotion regulation training. The distortion is the treatment of dramatic expression as the only valid expression, when the reality is that feelings are real at their actual intensity and that ordinary expression is valid. CBT helps through emotion awareness (identifying the actual intensity separate from the performed intensity), intensity matching (expressing at the intensity that matches the feeling), and distress tolerance for understatement (sitting with the discomfort of ordinary expression). The desire to express feelings is real; the conclusion that feelings must be dramatic to be real is the distortion.
