Fear of being stuck — the conviction that stasis is permanent and the inability to move is a character defect, the anticipation that being unable to move now means being unable to move ever, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is progressing and the progressing is the not-stuck — is one of the most pervasive and painful emotional patterns in progress-driven culture. It is the emotional root that drives stuckness-as-permanent (the belief that being unable to move now means you will never move, the conversion of a temporary phase into a permanent verdict, the treating of the stuck-moment as the evidence of a forever-stuckness), inability to move forward (the paralysis that traps you when facing a decision or action, the freeze response that locks the body and the mind, the chronic inaction that comes from the fear of the wrong step), anxiety when not progressing (the spike of fear when the life feels static, the threat-signal that fires because the stasis is the exposure the momentum was meant to prevent, the discomfort that treats the not-moving as the dangerous state), treating stuckness as character flaw (the conclusion that being stuck means being broken, the pathologizing of the stasis that treats the stuck as the defect, the framing of the paralysis as the personal failing), equating momentum with worth (the equation of moving-forward with being-worthy, the conclusion that the worth is the progress and the progress is the not-stuck, the belief that the moving self is the only self that matters), and chronic paralysis (the systematic avoidance of any action that might not produce forward motion, the treating of the frozen self as the only self that is allowed to be, the slow erosion of the capacity for movement that comes from the never-starting). It is distinct from fear of failure (which is about not succeeding at a specific task — the self that fears the outcome of one endeavor) and from fear of not being enough (which is about being inadequate as a person — the self that fears the verdict on its being) and from fear of being lazy (which is about rest being morally wrong — the self that fears the sin of stillness). Fear of being stuck is about the conviction that stasis is permanent and the inability to move is a character defect: the self that cannot move at rest because the stasis is the verdict and the verdict is the forever, the self that refuses the small step because the small step is the not-enough and the not-enough is the evidence of brokenness, the self that treats every stuck-moment as the proof of a permanent defect.

Fear of being stuck is the emotional root of three downstream patterns. The stuckness-is-permanent distortion makes every stuck-moment a forever-verdict and every stasis a permanent condition, producing chronic paralysis and inability to move forward (the person concludes the only safety is in the never-getting-stuck and the never-getting-stuck requires the constant-progress, so the rest is refused and the tiny-step is never taken because the tiny-step is the not-enough). The my-value-is-my-momentum distortion makes the forward-motion the measure of the self and the being the irrelevant, producing the equating momentum with worth and the chronic paralysis (the person moves and the moving is the not-stuck, and the not-stuck is the worthy, and the worthy is the never-static — but the fear of the wrong step prevents the moving, and the not-moving is the stuck, and the stuck is the not-worthy, and the paralysis deepens). And the being-stuck-means-i-am-broken distortion makes the stuck the brokenness and the momentum the virtue, producing the treating stuckness as character flaw and the anxiety when not progressing (the person gets stuck and the stuck is the not-virtuous, and the not-virtuous is the threat the momentum was meant to prevent). Fear of being stuck is the upstream cause; chronic paralysis, inability to move forward, and anxiety when not progressing are the downstream effects. The key insight for fear of being stuck: stasis is a phase, not a verdict — the self that can be stuck is the self that is human, not the self that is broken, and the worth is the being not the momentum, and the movement is the expression not the measure. The momentum research provides the deeper frame: momentum is not constant — it is built, lost, and rebuilt; stasis is a phase, not a verdict, and the stasis that feels permanent is the phase that the stuckness-is-permanent distortion converts into the identity.

The Fear of Being Stuck Cycle (How It Develops)

The fear of being stuck cycle is the self-reinforcing loop that produces the chronic paralysis, the inability to move forward, and the anxiety when not progressing that come from concluding that stasis is permanent and the inability to move is a character defect:

A situation with stuckness punished or pathologized, or chronic reinforcement of momentum-as-worth (an early experience where being stuck was treated as a personal failing, where taking time to figure things out was met with impatience or judgment, where the freeze response was punished rather than supported, a chronic reinforcement of progressing where one's sense of value was entirely defined by the forward motion) → stuckness-is-permanent fires (the stasis is the forever, the stuck is the verdict, the not-moving is the brokenness, the paralysis is the identity, the stuck-moment is the evidence of a permanent defect) → stuckness-as-permanent, inability to move forward, anxiety when not progressing, treating stuckness as character flaw, equating momentum with worth, chronic paralysis (the frozen doing, the refusing to step, the spike of fear when the life feels static, the pathologizing of stasis, the equation of moving-forward with being-worthy, the systematic avoidance of any action that might not produce forward motion) → short-term safety achieved (the not-trying feels like protection from the not-succeeding, the frozen feels like the not-broken-yet, the paralysis avoids the wrong step that would confirm the defect) → the safety reinforces the fear (the paralysis prevents the small step that would reveal that the stuck was not the forever, the frozen prevents the movement that would reveal that the stasis was not the verdict, the not-trying prevents the evidence that would disconfirm the stuckness-is-permanent conviction) → the person loses the experience of being-worth and the capacity for movement (the worth that comes from the being is never experienced, because the worth is always the momentum; the movement that comes from the tiny-step is never experienced, because the tiny-step is the not-enough and the not-enough is never taken; and the stuckness-is-permanent conviction deepens because every prevented step is treated as evidence that the stasis was the forever) → chronic paralysis (the endpoint of the stuckness-is-permanent distortion, the freeze that the being-stuck-means-i-am-broken distortion produces, the identity-level stuckness that comes from the my-value-is-my-momentum distortion) → stuckness-is-permanent conviction deepens → repeat

The cycle is driven by the short-term safety that paralysis and not-trying provides (not-trying-because-the-not-trying-is-the-protection-from-the-wrong-step and frozen-because-the-frozen-is-the-not-broken-yet both feel like they are preventing the confirmation of the defect, and the feeling reduces the fear of being stuck temporarily) and maintained by the long-term cost of the stuckness-is-permanent pattern (the being-worth is never experienced, because the worth is always the momentum; the movement is never experienced, because the tiny-step is the not-enough; and the stuckness-is-permanent conviction deepens because every prevented step is treated as evidence that the stasis was the forever). The tragic irony of fear of being stuck is that the safety strategy (paralysis, not-trying, frozen) produces the very outcome it was meant to prevent (the person stays stuck and the staying-stuck produces the very permanence the paralysis was meant to disprove, and the chronic not-trying is the self-imprisonment, not the self-protection — the fear of being stuck prevents the very tiny-step that would have broken the stasis the paralysis was meant to survive). The CBT intervention breaks the cycle at two points: (1) stasis-is-a-phase-clarification — when the stuckness-is-permanent distortion treats every stuck-moment as the forever, the first step is clarifying that stasis is a phase (the self that is stuck now may move in an hour or a day, and the stuck is the temporary, not the permanent, and the worth is the being not the momentum), and recognizing that the self that can be stuck is the self that is human; (2) values-based-tiny-action — when the my-value-is-my-momentum distortion fires, one challenges it with the ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999): the meaningful life is the one lived in valued-action that includes the tiny-step, not the one lived in the constant-progress for worth; the tiny-step from values includes the willingness to be imperfect in the pursuit, and the tiny-action is the chosen-movement, not the momentum-demand.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions & Their Reframes

1. The Stuckness-Is-Permanent (stasis-permanence)

Distortion: "Stuckness is permanent. There is something forever about this stasis, and the stuck is the verdict — not a temporary phase but a permanent condition on my worth. Whatever I face, I cannot move, because the stasis is the identity, and the movement is the impossible. I will be stuck forever, because the stuck is the self, and the self is the not-moving."
Reframe: "Stasis is a phase — and the stuckness-is-permanent distortion converts one specific form of stasis (this moment of being stuck) into a universal verdict (all stasis is the forever, so one is always broken when one is not moving). The stuckness-is-permanent distortion takes a partial truth (some stuck-moments last a while) and universalizes it (all stasis is the permanent, so one can never be stuck without being forever-broken). But stasis is a phase — the worth is the being, and the being is the character and the values and the person one is, and the stuck-moment is one phase of the self's life, not the verdict on it; and the stasis is not the forever but the temporary (one is stuck in this moment, and the stuck is the phase that the tiny-step will break, not the identity that defines the self). The distortion makes the paralysis feel like protection (I cannot move because I am forever-stuck), and the paralysis prevents the very tiny-step that would reveal that the stasis was not the permanent. The reframe: stasis is a phase — the worth is the being, and the being is not the momentum, and the stuck is one phase, not the verdict. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the core frame: the person who meets their own stuckness with kindness recognizes the stuckness-is-permanent as a distortion, not a verdict, and the kindness is the permission to be stuck that the paralysis withheld."

2. The I-Cannot-Move-Forward (forward-paralysis)

Distortion: "I cannot move forward. The stepping is the impossible, the moving is the beyond-me, the forward is the not-mine — and the not-moving is the evidence of my brokenness. I cannot take any step, because the step is the too-much, and the paralysis is the proof."
Reframe: "One small step is possible — and the forward-paralysis distortion converts one specific form of difficulty (some steps are hard) into a universal verdict (all movement is the impossible, so one is always broken when one is not moving). The i-cannot-move-forward distortion takes a partial truth (some actions feel overwhelming) and universalizes it (all forward-motion requires the impossible, so one can never move without failing). But one small step is possible — the self can take one tiny action now, and the tiny action is the movement, not the not-movement; and the self that takes one small step in this moment may take another in the next, and the movement is not diminished by the smallness. The distortion makes the paralysis feel like truth (I cannot move because I am broken), and the paralysis prevents the very tiny-step that would reveal that the movement was not the impossible. The reframe: one small step is possible — the self can move now, and the movement is the tiny-action, and the tiny-action is the chosen-step, not the impossible-demand. ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999) is the core frame: the valued life is the one lived in tiny-action with what one values, not the one lived in the constant-momentum for worth, and the tiny-action includes the willingness to be imperfect in the pursuit."

3. The If-I-Am-Not-Progressing-I-Am-Failing (progress-failure)

Distortion: "If I am not progressing, I am failing. The being-stuck is the being-failing, the stasis is the not-worthy, the not-moving is the verdict — and the stuck is the evidence of my brokenness. I must always progress, because the stasis is the failing, and the momentum is the worth."
Reframe: "Stasis is not failure — and the progress-failure distortion converts one specific form of risk (some stasis can be avoidance) into a universal verdict (all stasis is the failing, so one is always failing when one is not progressing). The if-i-am-not-progressing-i-am-failing distortion takes a partial truth (some stuckness can be procrastination) and universalizes it (all worth requires the never-stuck, so one can never be stuck without failing). But stasis is not failure — the stasis is the phase that precedes the movement, and the movement that follows the stasis is not diminished by the pause; and the self that is stuck in one moment may move in another, and the moving is not diminished by the stuck. The distortion makes the paralysis feel like safety (I must never be stuck to be ok), and the paralysis prevents the very stasis that would reveal that the stuck was not the failing. The reframe: stasis is not failure — the stasis is the phase, and the phase is for the movement that follows, and the movement that follows is not the constant-progress but the tiny-action that the stasis enables. Momentum research is the core frame: momentum is not constant — it is built, lost, and rebuilt, and the stasis is the phase between the momentum, not the failure of it."

4. The Being-Stuck-Means-I-Am-Broken (stuck-broken)

Distortion: "Being stuck means I am broken. The stasis is the defect, the paralysis is the flaw, the stuck is the verdict-on-my-being — and the stuck is the evidence of my brokenness. I must never be stuck, because the stuck is the broken, and the momentum is the virtue."
Reframe: "Stuckness is human — and the stuck-broken distortion converts one specific form of experience (some stuckness is painful) into a universal verdict (all stasis is the defect, so one is always broken when one is not moving). The being-stuck-means-i-am-broken distortion takes a partial truth (being stuck can feel hard) and universalizes it (all worth requires the never-stuck, so one can never be stuck without being broken). But stuckness is human — the stuck is the experience of every person who has faced a hard decision or a difficult season, and the stuck is not the defect but the phase; and the self that is stuck in one moment is the self that is human in that moment, and the humanity is not diminished by the stuck. The distortion makes the paralysis feel like truth (I am stuck because I am broken), and the paralysis prevents the very self-compassion that would reveal that the stuck was not the defect. The reframe: stuckness is human — the stuck is the experience, and the experience is not the defect, and the defect is the distortion, not the stuck. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the core frame: the person who meets their own stuckness with kindness recognizes the stuck-broken as a distortion, not a verdict, and the kindness is the permission to be stuck that the self-judgment withheld."

5. The My-Value-Is-My-Momentum (momentum-value-equation)

Distortion: "My value is my momentum. The worth is the moving, the mattering is the progressing, the counting is the forward — and the being is the irrelevant, the stuck is the not-mattering, the stasis is the not-counting. I must maximize my momentum, because the momentum is the worth, and the stuck is the not-worth."
Reframe: "Worth is being not momentum — and the momentum-value-equation distortion converts one specific form of value (some momentum can matter) into a universal verdict (all worth is the momentum, so all stasis is the not-worth). The my-value-is-my-momentum distortion takes a partial truth (forward motion can affect impact) and universalizes it (all worth requires the never-stuck, so one can never be stuck without losing worth). But worth is being not momentum — the worth is the character and the values and the person one is, and the momentum is one expression of the self, not the measure of it; and the stuck is not the not-worth but the being (one is stuck in this moment, and the stasis is the phase of the being-worth, not the verdict on the self). The distortion makes the paralysis feel like worth-protection (I must maximize my momentum to be worth), and the paralysis prevents the very stasis that would reveal that the worth was not the momentum. The reframe: worth is being not momentum — the worth is the being, and the being is not the momentum, and the momentum is one expression, not the measure. Self-determination theory competence need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: the competence is the effective-action, and the effective-action can include the tiny-step and the stasis, and the stasis is the competence-support, not the competence-failure."

6. The I-Must-Always-Be-Progressing (constant-progress-requirement)

Distortion: "I must always be progressing. The being-stuck is the being-broken, the stasis is the not-ok, the not-moving is the verdict — and the not-progressing is the not-ok. I must always progress, because the progressing is the worth, and the stuck is the broken."
Reframe: "Stasis is allowed — and the constant-progress-requirement distortion converts one specific form of safety (some progressing can protect) into a universal verdict (all moments require the progressing, so one is always failing when one is stuck). The i-must-always-be-progressing distortion takes a partial truth (progressing can feel safe) and universalizes it (all worth requires the never-stuck, so one can never be stuck without failing). But stasis is allowed — the self is ok now, and the ok is the being, not the progressing; and the being-stuck in one moment is the being-human in another, and the humanity is not diminished by the stasis. The distortion makes the paralysis feel like safety-seeking (I must always be progressing to be ok), and the paralysis prevents the very stasis that would reveal that the progressing was not the ok. The reframe: stasis is allowed — the self is ok now, and the ok is the being, and the being is not the progressing, and the progressing is one expression, not the condition. ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999) is the core tool: the meaningful life is the one lived in valued-action that includes the stasis, and the stasis includes the willingness to be stuck, because the being-stuck is the phase that precedes the tiny-action that the paralysis prevents. Momentum research is the core frame: the constant-progress that excludes the stasis is the self-depletion that produces the chronic paralysis, and the paralysis is not the worth but the freeze that the constant-progress-requirement produces."

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Fear of Being Stuck

Component 1 — Assessment & Psychoeducation: Being-stuck auditing (identify the domains where one cannot move, the steps one cannot take, the decisions one cannot make, the actions one cannot start, the tiny-movements one cannot permit). Psychoeducation on the fear of being stuck cycle (stuckness punished or pathologized, or chronic reinforcement of momentum-as-worth → stuckness-is-permanent → stuckness-as-permanent, inability to move forward, anxiety when not progressing, treating stuckness as character flaw, equating momentum with worth, chronic paralysis → short-term safety → safety reinforces fear → lose the experience of being-worth and the capacity for movement → chronic paralysis → stuckness-is-permanent conviction deepens → repeat), the momentum research (momentum is not constant — it is built, lost, and rebuilt; stasis is a phase, not a verdict), and the key insight that fear of being stuck is the emotional root of paralysis and inaction (the stuckness-is-permanent distortion makes the stuck the forever, the my-value-is-my-momentum distortion makes the momentum the worth). Introduce the ACT values-based tiny-action approach (Hayes 1999), the self-determination theory competence-need application (Deci & Ryan 2000), the momentum-awareness practice, and the self-compassion practice (Neff 2003).

Component 2 — Cognitive Restructuring & Skill Building: Identify the 6 cognitive distortions (stuckness-is-permanent, i-cannot-move-forward, if-i-am-not-progressing-i-am-failing, being-stuck-means-i-am-broken, my-value-is-my-momentum, i-must-always-be-progressing) and build balanced beliefs. Practice stasis-is-a-phase clarification ('Stasis is a phase, not a verdict; the self that can be stuck is the self that is human, not the self that is broken') to break the stuckness-is-permanent. Apply the values-based-tiny-action reframe (the meaningful life is the one lived in valued-action that includes the tiny-step, not the one lived in the constant-progress for worth). Practice competence-need restoration (Deci & Ryan 2000: the competence is the effective-action, not the constant-momentum; 'Am I taking one small step from values or demanding constant progress to be ok' rather than 'Must I progress to be ok'). Rehearse self-compassion responses (Neff 2003) for stuck-moment scenarios ('This stuckness is hard, and my fear is worthy of care; the worth is the being not the momentum, and the kindness I offer myself is the permission to be stuck that the momentum-demand withheld'). Distinguish the momentum that is genuinely serving values from the paralysis that is the stuckness-is-permanent-demand (the first is chosen, the second is driven).

Component 3 — Behavioral Practice & Maintenance: Practice one tiny-action per day that intentionally is small in a context where the momentum is the default (one small step taken, one tiny action started, one small decision made, one micro-movement enacted), starting with low-stakes (one tiny step, one safe domain) and escalating to higher-stakes (a significant step taken, a domain where the paralysis is the norm, a tiny-action made without the demand for momentum). Track the outcome (the tiny-action practiced, one's own fear during it, the being-stuck-fear that fired, the distortion that maintained the fear, whether the tiny-action revealed that the stasis was not the forever and whether the practicing shrank the stuckness-is-permanent conviction). Process the discomfort of the tiny-action (the fear that fires is the stuckness-is-permanent distortion, not a signal the step was wrong). Apply self-compassion (Neff 2003) when the fear persists (kindness to the paralysis, not evidence of brokenness). Use ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999) to maintain the practice through the fear ('the tiny-action I enact is the movement, not the failing, and the movement is the acting from values, not the progressing for worth'). Use momentum research to shift from paralysis to sustainable movement: the sustainable movement is the tiny-action that the stasis enables, and the tiny-action requires the willingness to be stuck first. Use self-determination theory competence need (Deci & Ryan 2000) to restore the competence through the tiny-action: the tiny-action restores the capacity for effective-action, and the competence is the effective-action, not the constant-momentum. Generalize to higher-stakes movement as the capacity to act from values strengthens and the stuckness-is-permanent conviction loosens.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fear of being stuck the same as fear of failure?

Fear of being stuck and fear of failure are related but not identical. Fear of failure is the conviction that one will not succeed at a specific task — the anticipation that the outcome of one endeavor will be negative, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self succeeds at this thing. Fear of being stuck is the conviction that stasis is permanent and the inability to move is a character defect — the anticipation that being unable to move now means being unable to move ever, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is progressing. The overlap is substantial: the being-stuck-fearing self may fear failure (the stuck will produce the not-succeeding, so the only safety is in the momentum that prevents the failure), and the failure-fearing self can fear being stuck (the failure may leave one stuck, so the only safety is in the progressing that prevents the stuckness). But the primary distortion differs: fear of failure's distortion is about the outcome of a specific task (will I succeed at this), fear of being stuck's distortion is about the permanence of stasis (is this stuck forever). A person can fear failure without dominant being-stuck-fear (anxious about a specific outcome while being basically able to be stuck without panic), and a person can fear being stuck without dominant failure-fear (unable to be stuck while being basically secure in their not-failing-at-tasks). In practice, the two patterns often co-occur, and CBT for one will often address the other. The self-compassion (Neff 2003), ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999), and momentum research tools on this page serve both patterns: they build the capacity to act from values (fear of being stuck) and the capacity to act without the fear of the outcome (fear of failure), because both capacities come from the same reframe: worth is the being, not the momentum.

How is fear of being stuck different from fear of being lazy?

Fear of being stuck and fear of being lazy overlap but differ in focus. Fear of being lazy is the conviction that rest is morally wrong and the productivity is the worth — the anticipation that stillness will mean being a failure, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is producing. Fear of being stuck is the conviction that stasis is permanent and the inability to move is a character defect — the anticipation that being unable to move now means being unable to move ever, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is progressing. Fear of being lazy is about the moral status of rest (is rest a sin); fear of being stuck is about the permanence of stasis (is this stuck forever). A person can fear being lazy without dominant being-stuck-fear (unable to rest while being basically able to be stuck without panic), and a person can fear being stuck without dominant being-lazy-fear (unable to be stuck while being basically able to rest without guilt). In practice, the two patterns can interact (the being-stuck-fearing self may fear being lazy — the stuck proves the laziness, so the only being-ok is the momentum, and the being-lazy-fearing self may fear being stuck — the being-ok requires the producing, so the being-ok is the not-stuck), and the CBT tools on this page address the underlying stuckness-is-permanent pattern. The self-determination theory competence need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the unifying frame: both patterns involve a threat to the competence (the not-moving and the not-producing both threaten the self), and the restoration — through tiny-action that includes stasis rather than constant-momentum for worth — is the path through both.

What if I really do need to make progress in some situations — isn't some momentum appropriate?

Yes — and this is a crucial nuance. Fear of being stuck is not the claim that all stasis is good or that one should never move. The progressing life is the meaningful life for many people, and the momentum that serves one's values is the valued-action, not the stuckness-is-permanent-driven momentum. The diagnostic question is not 'am I progressing' but 'am I moving from values or from the fear of being stuck'. The paralysis that comes from the stuckness-is-permanent distortion has a specific signature: it is driven (the person cannot move, the stuck produces anxiety, the stasis feels like forever), not chosen (the person could move, the stasis is welcome, the stuck is a phase). The momentum that serves values is chosen and can include stasis; the momentum that serves the stuckness-is-permanent distortion is driven and cannot include stasis. The ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999) framework makes this explicit: the valued life includes both action and stasis, and the stasis is not the not-valued but the phase that precedes the valued-action. The goal of CBT for fear of being stuck is not to eliminate momentum but to liberate it — to move from momentum that is driven by the fear of being stuck (and therefore cannot pause, and therefore freezes, and therefore is unsustainable) to momentum that is chosen from values (and therefore can pause, and therefore is sustainable, and therefore is more effective). The self-determination theory competence need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: the competence is the effective-action, and the effective-action can include the tiny-step and the stasis, and the stasis is the competence-support, not the competence-failure.

What does CBT for Fear of Being Stuck involve and does it work?

CBT for Fear of Being Stuck involves stuckness-is-not-permanent clarification, cognitive restructuring of the being-stuck distortions, ACT values-based tiny-action (Hayes 1999), self-determination theory competence-need application (Deci & Ryan 2000), momentum-awareness practice, and self-compassion practice (Neff 2003). The core components: (1) stuckness-is-not-permanent clarification — the stuckness-is-permanent distortion treats every stuck-moment as the verdict and every stasis as the forever; the first step is clarifying that stasis is a phase (the self that is stuck now may move in an hour or a day, and the stuck is the temporary, not the permanent, and the worth is the being not the momentum), and recognizing that the self that can be stuck is the self that is human, not the self that is broken; (2) cognitive restructuring — identifying the 6 cognitive distortions (stuckness-is-permanent, i-cannot-move-forward, if-i-am-not-progressing-i-am-failing, being-stuck-means-i-am-broken, my-value-is-my-momentum, i-must-always-be-progressing) and building balanced beliefs (stasis is a phase, one can take a small step, not moving is not failing, stuckness is human not broken, worth is being not momentum, and stasis is allowed); (3) ACT values-based tiny-action — the Hayes (1999) approach: the meaningful life is the one lived in valued-action that includes the tiny-step, not the one lived in the constant-progress for worth; acting from one's values includes the willingness to take one small step in the pursuit, and the tiny-action is the chosen-movement, not the momentum-demand. Yes, CBT for fear of being stuck works — the cognitive distortions are well-established in the CBT literature, the ACT values-based tiny-action approach has strong empirical support for paralysis and avoidance patterns, and the momentum research confirms that stasis is a phase that the tiny-action breaks.
