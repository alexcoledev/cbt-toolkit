What is Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Alzheimer's Disease?

Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Alzheimer's Disease (DSM-5 294.0x) is a diagnosis in the Neurocognitive Disorders category, used when: (1) there is evidence of modest cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition) — based on both concern from the individual, a knowledgeable informant, or the clinician, AND objective evidence of decline; (2) the cognitive deficits do NOT interfere with independence in everyday activities (IADLs are preserved), though greater effort, compensatory strategies, or assistance may be needed — this is the 'mild' threshold, distinguishing it from Major NCD; (3) the deficits do not occur exclusively during delirium; (4) the deficits are not better explained by another mental or medical disorder; (5) there is evidence of Alzheimer pathology (amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, hippocampal atrophy on MRI, amyloid positivity on PET imaging or CSF biomarkers, or genetic evidence such as autosomal dominant Alzheimer mutation). The distinctive clinical features include: episodic memory decline as the hallmark early feature (difficulty learning and retaining new information, while other cognitive domains are relatively preserved early on), preserved independence in IADLs (the person can still manage medications, finances, appointments, and complex tasks, though with greater effort or compensatory strategies), evidence of Alzheimer pathology (hippocampal atrophy, amyloid biomarkers), an uncertain progression trajectory (while progression to Major NCD is common, some individuals remain stable or even improve, and the rate of progression varies widely), and a window for early intervention (vascular risk factor control, cognitive engagement, physical exercise, social engagement, and monitoring may slow progression and maximize function during the mild stage). Mild NCD due to Alzheimer's sits on the Alzheimer pathology continuum between normal aging and Major NCD due to Alzheimer's, and was previously called mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to Alzheimer's. The cognitive changes are more pronounced than normal age-related cognitive change but do not meet the major threshold.

The "The-It-Is-Just-Normal-Aging" Distortion (age-attribution / aging-as-explanation)

Distortion: "I am just getting older. Everyone forgets things as they age. The memory lapses, the word-finding problems, the slower thinking — that is just normal for my age. My father was the same at this age, and he was fine. There is no point seeing a doctor about normal aging. Everyone's memory gets worse, and that is just life. It is not a medical condition, it is just getting old."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Normal-Aging. The age-attribution (it is just getting older) is the distortion: WHILE NORMAL AGING PRODUCES MILD COGNITIVE SLOWING, the cognitive decline in mild neurocognitive disorder due to Alzheimer's is DISTINCT FROM AND MORE PRONOUNCED THAN normal age-related cognitive change — the episodic memory decline (difficulty learning and retaining new information) exceeds age-related memory change, represents a decline from the individual's previous level of performance, and is accompanied by evidence of Alzheimer pathology (hippocampal atrophy, amyloid biomarkers) that is not present in normal aging. The aging-as-explanation is the distortion: attributing the cognitive changes to normal aging drives missed evaluation — the neuropsychological testing that could confirm the decline is beyond normal aging is never done, the biomarkers that could identify Alzheimer pathology are never checked, the baseline cognitive assessment that could track progression is never established, and the early intervention window is missed. The normal-for-my-age is the distortion: treating the cognitive changes as expected aging drives missed early intervention — the vascular risk factor control (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia) that could slow progression is never initiated, the cognitive engagement and physical exercise that may protect function are never prioritized, the monitoring that could detect progression to Major NCD is never established, and the compensatory strategies that could maximize function are never built. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does neuropsychological testing show — decline beyond normal aging or age-appropriate change, what does the MRI show — hippocampal atrophy or normal volume for age, what do biomarkers show if available, what is the functional impact — are the changes more than occasional lapses, what does the informed informant report — a noticeable decline or age-typical change), challenging the age-attribution, and building engagement with the evaluation, monitoring, and early intervention that could slow progression and maximize function during the mild stage.

What happens in a CBT session for mild neurocognitive disorder due to Alzheimer's disease?
