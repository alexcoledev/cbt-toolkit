Fear of being replaced — the intense anxiety that someone else will fill one's place and that the filling will make one redundant — is one of the most common and quietly exhausting emotional patterns in modern competitive life. It is the emotional root that drives competitive anxiety (constant comparison and the inability to celebrate others' success), possessiveness in relationships (holding partners tightly so no one else can occupy their attention), overworking to be indispensable (taking on everything, refusing to delegate, building one's entire worth on being the only one who can do the work), and resentment when someone new joins one's role or space (the hostility toward a new colleague, a new partner's friend, a new team member, because their presence feels like the beginning of one's replacement). It is distinct from fear of abandonment (which is about a person leaving — the fear that a specific relationship will end and one will be alone) and from fear of failure (which is about one's own inadequacy — the fear that one will not measure up) — fear of being replaced is about someone else filling one's place making one redundant: the person may still be present, but the dread is that someone else can do what one does, occupy the role one occupies, fill the space one fills, and that the fillability will prove one was never uniquely needed. It is maintained by distinctive cognitive distortions that general competition-anxiety tools, abandonment tools, and self-esteem tools each miss: the if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant redundancy-equation distortion where another's success is treated as one's own obsolescence, the i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter indispensability-requirement distortion where mattering is made to require being irreplaceable so a replaceable person is a worthless person, the their-presence-erases-mine presence-erasure distortion where someone else occupying the same space is treated as one's own erasure, the if-i-am-replaced-i-am-worthless replacement-worthlessness distortion where being replaced in a role is equated with being worthless as a person, the i-must-guard-my-role-against-all-comers role-guarding distortion where one must defend one's position against every potential replacement (which prevents collaboration and growth), and the being-replaced-means-i-was-never-needed retroactive-nullification distortion where being replaced is treated as retroactive proof that one's contribution was never real. CBT with self-compassion (Neff 2003), ACT values-based unique contribution (Hayes 1999), and self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) is evidence-based for this pattern and addresses the distortions at their root.

Fear of being replaced is the emotional root of three downstream patterns. The i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter distortion makes being replaceable feel like being worthless, producing overworking to be indispensable (the person takes on everything, refuses to delegate, builds their entire worth on being the only one who can do the work, and the overworking consumes the life and prevents the relationships and rest that would actually sustain the person). The if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant distortion makes others' success feel like one's own obsolescence, producing competitive anxiety (the person cannot celebrate others' achievements, monitors colleagues' progress constantly, and experiences others' competence as a threat rather than as a contribution to the shared work). And the their-presence-erases-mine distortion makes someone else occupying the same space feel like one's own erasure, producing possessiveness in relationships and resentment toward new team members (the person holds partners tightly so no one else can occupy their attention, and greets new colleagues with hostility because their presence feels like the beginning of one's replacement). Fear of being replaced is the upstream cause; frantic overworking, chronic competitive anxiety, and strained relationships are the downstream effects. The key insight for fear of being replaced: your worth is in who you are and what you uniquely contribute, not in occupying an irreplaceable slot — someone else filling a role does not retroactively erase your contribution or your value, because the contribution was the values you enacted and the person you were, not the slot you held. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) provides the deeper frame: the competence need is satisfied by one's own development (intrinsic), not by one's ranking against others (comparative), and the comparative definition never satisfies the need because there is always someone who can do some part of what one does. ACT (Hayes 1999) provides the motivation: the meaningful contribution is the values one enacts, not the role one holds, and the values can be enacted in any role. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) provides the safety net: when the fear of replacement fires, one meets it with kindness rather than with the self-attack that the fear is a personal failing.

The Fear of Being Replaced Cycle (How It Develops)

The fear of being replaced cycle is the self-reinforcing loop that produces the frantic overworking, the chronic competitive anxiety, and the strained relationships that come from concluding one is replaceable:

A situation arises where one's replaceability is salient (a new colleague is hired, a partner makes a new friend, a sibling outperforms one, a task one thought was uniquely one's is given to someone else) → if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant fires (if they can do what I do, I am redundant, so I must ensure no one can do what I do) → overworking to be indispensable or competitive anxiety or possessiveness (take on everything so only one can do it, monitor and outperform colleagues, hold relationships tightly so no one else can fill one's place) → short-term safety achieved (the activity feels like it is securing one's irreplaceability, and the fear of replacement subsides temporarily) → the irreplaceability feels fragile (someone else is always developing, the role is always evolving, the relationships are always shifting, and the fear returns) → the person redoubles the role-guarding (more overworking, more competitive monitoring, more possessiveness, and the activity consumes more of the life) → the i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter distortion fires (if I am not indispensable, I do not matter, so I must make myself indispensable, which requires even more role-guarding) → the person stops contributing from values and starts contributing from fear (the work becomes about being the only one who can do it rather than about the values the work enacts, the relationships become about being irreplaceable rather than about connection, and the contribution that would have been meaningful is never made because the person was too busy securing their slot to enact their values) → repeat with more desperation and less genuine contribution

The cycle is driven by the short-term safety that role-guarding provides (overworking, competitive monitoring, and possessiveness all feel like they are securing one's irreplaceability, and the feeling reduces the fear temporarily) and maintained by the long-term cost of the role-guarding pattern (the work is consumed by securing the slot rather than enacting the values, the relationships are strained by the possessiveness, the competitive anxiety prevents the collaboration that would actually produce better work, and the contribution that would have been worth making is never made because the person was too busy guaranteeing their irreplaceability). The tragic irony of fear of being replaced is that the protection strategy (overworking to be indispensable, competitive monitoring, possessiveness to prevent replacement) produces the very outcome it was meant to prevent (the person spends their energy securing their slot rather than making the unique contribution that would have made them genuinely valuable, and the slot they secure is a slot held by fear rather than a slot filled by contribution). The CBT intervention breaks the cycle at two points: (1) uniqueness-worth decoupling — when the i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter distortion ties one's worth to being irreplaceable, the first step is recognizing that one's worth is in who one is (one's character, one's values, one's relationships, one's lived experience), not in the slot one occupies ('My worth is who I am; the slot can be filled, but the person who fills it is a different person with a different contribution, and the filling does not erase me'); (2) ACT values-based unique contribution — when the if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant distortion fires, one challenges it (another's success is their achievement, not my obsolescence; the work is bigger than any one person, and others' competence contributes to the shared work rather than competing with mine) and replaces the role-guarding with values-based contribution in the present (the contribution that matters is the values one enacts, not the slot one holds) met with self-compassion (Neff 2003) if the fear of replacement fires while the restructuring proceeds.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions & Their Reframes

1. The If-They-Succeed-I-Am-Redundant (redundancy-equation)

Distortion: "If they succeed — if a colleague does the work well, if a peer achieves what I achieved, if someone else can do what I do — I am redundant. Their success is my obsolescence. The fact that someone else can fill my role proves I was never uniquely needed."
Reframe: "Another's success is their achievement, not my obsolescence — and the redundancy-equation distortion converts a fact about someone else (they are competent) into a verdict about oneself (I am redundant). The if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant distortion takes the fact that others can do some of what one does (which is true of every person in every role — no one is the only person who can do anything) and treats it as evidence that one is redundant. But another's competence is about them, not about one: a colleague who does the work well is contributing to the shared work, not replacing one. A peer who achieves what one achieved is reaching a milestone one also reached, not erasing one's having-reached-it. Someone else who can do what one does is sharing the capacity, not proving one's capacity was never unique. The redundancy-equation distortion makes one's value contingent on being the only one who can do the work, and the contingency is impossible to satisfy (because there is always someone who can do some part of what one does, and the comparative ground is always shifting). The reframe: another's success is their achievement, and their achievement does not make one redundant — the work is bigger than any one person, and others' competence contributes to the shared work rather than competing with one's contribution. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: when competence is defined intrinsically (am I developing my own capacities, am I growing, am I contributing from my own strengths), another's success is irrelevant to one's competence satisfaction; when competence is defined comparatively (am I better than others, am I the only one who can do this), another's success is always a threat. The intrinsic definition is the reframe. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) makes the reframe survivable: the person who meets another's success with kindness (toward the other and toward oneself) can celebrate the achievement rather than experiencing it as a threat."

2. The I-Must-Be-Indispensable-to-Matter (indispensability-requirement)

Distortion: "To matter, I must be indispensable. A replaceable person is a worthless person. If someone else can do what I do — if my role can be filled, if my absence would be covered, if my contribution is not unique — then my life did not count."
Reframe: "My worth is in who I am, not in being irreplaceable — and the indispensability-requirement distortion converts one specific form of mattering (being irreplaceable) into the only form of mattering. The i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter distortion takes a partial truth (being irreplaceable is one way to matter) and universalizes it (being irreplaceable is the only way to matter, so a replaceable person is a worthless person). But mattering takes many forms: the parent who raised a kind child mattered to that child, regardless of whether another parent could have raised a kind child too. The friend who was present in a hard time mattered to the friend, regardless of whether another friend could have been present too. The worker who did their work with care mattered to the people who relied on the work, regardless of whether another worker could have done the work with care too. The person who simply lived with integrity, kindness, and presence mattered — to the people they encountered, to the world they inhabited, to themselves. None of these forms of mattering require being irreplaceable, and all of them are real. The indispensability-requirement distortion devalues these forms of mattering by making them contingent on irreplaceability, and the devaluation drives the overworking that consumes the life. The reframe: one's worth is in who one is (one's character, one's values, one's relationships, one's lived experience), not in the slot one occupies — one matters in the person one is and the values one enacts, and the mattering is real regardless of whether someone else could fill the role. ACT (Hayes 1999) is the core tool: the meaningful contribution is the values one enacts, not the role one holds, and the values can be enacted in any role — so being replaceable in a role does not erase one's contribution, because the contribution was the values, not the slot. The person who can hold worth-as-who-I-am can contribute from values rather than securing from fear, and the contribution is the mattering — the values enacted are the worth that counts, even if the role is shared."

3. The Their-Presence-Erases-Mine (presence-erasure)

Distortion: "If they are present — if a new colleague joins the team, if a partner makes a new friend, if someone else enters the space I occupied — I am erased. Their presence takes up the room that was mine. Being the only one in my role is the only proof I mattered."
Reframe: "Their presence is their addition, not my erasure — and the presence-erasure distortion converts someone else's presence into one's own absence. The their-presence-erases-mine distortion takes the fact that someone else is now present (a new colleague, a new friend, a new team member) and treats their presence as the erasure of one's own presence. But another's presence is about them, not about one: a new colleague who joins the team is adding their contribution to the shared work, not removing one's contribution. A partner's new friend is expanding the partner's social world, not contracting one's place in it. A new team member is sharing the work, not erasing the person who was there first. One's presence in the role, in the relationship, in the space is a fact that another's presence does not erase — the time one was present, the contribution one made, the relationships one built are all real and are not undone by someone else arriving. The presence-erasure distortion makes one's existence contingent on being the only one, and the contingency drives the possessiveness (holding relationships tightly so no one else can fill one's place) and the resentment toward new team members (greeting them with hostility because their presence feels like the beginning of one's replacement) that prevent the collaboration and the new connections that would actually enrich one's life. The reframe: another's presence is their addition, not one's erasure — one was present in the time one was present, and the presence is a fact that their arrival does not change. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the core tool: the person who meets another's presence with kindness can welcome the addition rather than experiencing it as a threat, and the welcoming is the capacity that allows one to share space without collapsing. ACT (Hayes 1999) reinforces this: the values one enacts in the shared space are the contribution that matters, and the contribution is made in the present, not guaranteed by being the only one."

4. The If-I-Am-Replaced-I-Am-Worthless (replacement-worthlessness)

Distortion: "If I am replaced — if someone else takes over my role, if my position is filled, if I am no longer the one who does what I did — I am worthless. Being replaced is the proof that I never mattered, that my contribution was never real, that I was always just a placeholder."
Reframe: "Being replaced in a role is not being worthless as a person — and the replacement-worthlessness distortion conflates the role with the person. The if-i-am-replaced-i-am-worthless distortion takes the fact that one's role can be filled (which is true of every role — roles are slots, and slots are designed to be fillable) and treats the filling as evidence that one is worthless as a person. But a role is not a person: the role one occupied is a slot in a system, and the slot can be filled by different people at different times. The person who filled the role — one — is not the role. One is the person who brought one's character, one's values, one's relationships, one's lived experience to the role, and the bringing is real regardless of whether someone else now brings their own character, values, relationships, and experience to the same role. The replacement-worthlessness distortion equates the role with the person, and the equation makes every role-change a personal annihilation. The reframe: being replaced in a role is a fact about the role (the slot was filled by someone else), not a verdict about the person (one is worthless) — the person who occupied the role is still the person they were, with the character, values, and relationships they had, and the role-change does not erase any of it. ACT (Hayes 1999) is the core tool: one's contribution is the values one enacted while one was in the role, and the values enacted are a fact about the past that the role-change does not undo. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) reinforces this: the competence one developed, the autonomy one exercised, the relatedness one built are all one's own, and they do not disappear when the role changes — they are carried forward into whatever one does next. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) makes the role-change survivable: the person who meets being replaced with kindness can grieve the role (which is a real loss) without concluding they are worthless (which is the distortion)."

5. The I-Must-Guard-My-Role-Against-All-Comers (role-guarding)

Distortion: "I must guard my role against all comers. Every potential replacement is a threat. I must not share knowledge, must not delegate, must not collaborate, must not mentor — because making others capable of doing my work is making myself replaceable. My safety is in being the only one who can do what I do."
Reframe: "I can collaborate rather than guard my role — and the role-guarding distortion converts one specific safety strategy (being the only one who can do the work) into the only safety strategy. The i-must-guard-my-role-against-all-comers distortion takes a partial truth (being the only one who can do the work is one form of job security) and universalizes it (being the only one is the only form of safety, so one must guard against all comers). But role-guarding is a strategy that destroys the very thing it is meant to protect: the person who refuses to share knowledge, delegate, or collaborate becomes a bottleneck (the work cannot proceed without them, which is the point, but the bottleneck makes the work worse, which makes the person less valuable, not more). The person who refuses to mentor ensures that no one can grow into the role, which means the team does not develop, which means the work the team does is limited, which means the person's own contribution is limited by the team's limitations. The role-guarding distortion makes collaboration feel like self-destruction, and the feeling prevents the collaboration that would actually produce better work, develop the team, and make the person more valuable (as a contributor and a leader, not just as a solo performer). The reframe: one can collaborate rather than guard one's role — sharing knowledge, delegating, and mentoring develop the team and the work, and the development makes everyone more valuable, including oneself. ACT (Hayes 1999) is the core tool: the meaningful contribution is the values one enacts, and the values include generosity, collaboration, and the development of others — enacting these values is the contribution that matters, and the contribution is made larger by sharing, not smaller. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) reinforces this: the relatedness need is satisfied by genuine collaboration, not by isolation; the competence need is satisfied by developing others (which is a higher-order competence), not by hoarding capacity. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) makes the sharing survivable: the person who meets the fear of sharing with kindness can share anyway, and the sharing is the contribution that the fear was meant to prevent."

6. The Being-Replaced-Means-I-Was-Never-Needed (retroactive-nullification)

Distortion: "If I can be replaced, it means I was never needed. The fact that someone else can do what I did proves my contribution was never real, never unique, never necessary. Being replaced retroactively nullifies everything I gave to the role."
Reframe: "Being replaced means the role was shared, not that I was never needed — and the retroactive-nullification distortion converts a fact about the present (someone else can do the work now) into a verdict about the past (one's contribution was never real). The being-replaced-means-i-was-never-needed distortion takes the fact that one's role can be filled by someone else and treats it as retroactive proof that one's contribution was never necessary. But the present fillability of a role does not erase the past contribution: the work one did was done, the people one helped were helped, the relationships one built were built, the values one enacted were enacted — all in the time one was in the role, and all real regardless of whether someone else now fills the role. The retroactive-nullification distortion confuses the present capacity of the role (it can be filled) with the past value of one's contribution (it was never real), but the two are not the same: the contribution was real in the moment it was made, and the reality is a fact about the past that the present fillability does not undo. The reframe: being replaced means the role was shared (someone else can now do the work), not that one was never needed (one's contribution was never real) — the contribution one made in the time one was in the role is a fact that the role-change does not retroactively erase. ACT (Hayes 1999) is the core tool: the values one enacted in the role are the contribution that matters, and the values enacted are a fact about the past that cannot be nullified by a present role-change. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) reinforces this: the competence one developed, the autonomy one exercised, the relatedness one built are all real and are carried forward — they are not erased by the role-change. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) makes the reframe survivable: the person who meets the retroactive-nullification thought with kindness can hold the reality of their past contribution ('I gave what I gave, and the giving was real') rather than letting the present role-change erase it."

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Fear of Being Replaced

Component 1 — Assessment & Psychoeducation: Replaceability auditing (identify the situations where one's replaceability is most salient, the overworking, competitive anxiety, and possessiveness patterns one uses, the roles one is guarding, the relationships one is holding too tightly). Psychoeducation on the fear of being replaced cycle (replaceability salient → redundancy-equation distortion → overwork or compete or cling → short-term safety → irreplaceability feels fragile → redouble role-guarding → indispensability-requirement distortion → stop contributing from values and start contributing from fear → repeat), the self-determination theory research (Deci & Ryan 2000: the competence need is satisfied intrinsically by one's own development, not comparatively by one's ranking against others, and the comparative definition never satisfies the need), and the key insight that fear of being replaced is the emotional root of frantic overworking, chronic competitive anxiety, and strained relationships (the i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter distortion makes being replaceable feel like being worthless, the if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant distortion makes others' success feel like one's own obsolescence). Introduce the ACT values-based unique contribution approach (Hayes 1999), the self-determination theory intrinsic-competence practice (Deci & Ryan 2000), and the self-compassion practice (Neff 2003).

Component 2 — Cognitive Restructuring & Skill Building: Identify the 6 cognitive distortions (if-they-succeed-i-am-redundant, i-must-be-indispensable-to-matter, their-presence-erases-mine, if-i-am-replaced-i-am-worthless, i-must-guard-my-role-against-all-comers, being-replaced-means-i-was-never-needed) and build balanced beliefs. Practice uniqueness-worth decoupling ('My worth is who I am; the slot can be filled, but the person who fills it is a different person with a different contribution, and the filling does not erase me') to break the indispensability-requirement distortion. Apply the values-based-unique-contribution reframe (the meaningful contribution is the values one enacts, not the role one holds, and the values can be enacted in any role). Practice intrinsic-competence definition (Deci & Ryan 2000: competence is one's own development, not one's ranking against others; 'Am I growing, am I developing my capacities, am I contributing from my strengths' rather than 'Am I better than others, am I the only one who can do this'). Rehearse self-compassion responses (Neff 2003) for replacement scenarios ('Someone else can do what I do, and my contribution is still real; the role is shared, not erased, and I am still the person I am'). Distinguish the role-guarding one can release from the values-based contribution one can choose (the first is avoidance of replacement, the second is contribution alongside the possibility of replacement).

Component 3 — Behavioral Practice & Maintenance: Practice one values-based contribution per day that is intentionally collaborative (sharing knowledge, delegating, mentoring, celebrating a colleague's success, welcoming a new team member), starting with low-stakes (a small knowledge share, a brief delegation) and escalating to higher-stakes (mentoring someone into one's role, celebrating a peer's achievement that one might have wanted for oneself). Track the outcome (the value enacted, one's own fear during it, the replacement-anxiety that fired, the distortion that maintained the fear). Process the discomfort of the collaboration (the fear that fires is the i-must-guard-my-role-against-all-comers distortion, not a signal the collaboration was wrong). Apply self-compassion (Neff 2003) when the fear persists (kindness to the self, not evidence of weakness). Use ACT values-based contribution (Hayes 1999) to maintain the practice through the fear ('the value I enact in the present is the contribution that matters, and the contribution is made in the enacting, not in being the only one who can do the work'). Use self-determination theory intrinsic-competence (Deci & Ryan 2000) to frame the development as one's own growth, not as a comparison to others. Generalize to higher-stakes values-based collaboration as the capacity strengthens and the role-guarding pattern weakens.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fear of being replaced the same as fear of failure?

Fear of being replaced and fear of failure are related but not identical. Fear of failure is the anxiety about one's own inadequacy — the fear that one will not measure up, that one's performance will fall short, that one will not be good enough. Fear of being replaced is the anxiety that someone else will fill one's place — the fear that another person will do what one does, occupy the role one occupies, and that the fillability will make one redundant. The overlap is substantial: failure can trigger fear of being replaced (if I fail, someone else will succeed and take my place), and fear of being replaced can intensify fear of failure (if I am replaceable, I must not fail, because failure will confirm my replaceability). But the primary distortion differs: fear of failure's distortion is about one's own performance (will I measure up), fear of being replaced's distortion is about another's presence (will someone else fill my place). A person can fear failure without dominant replacement feelings (dreading personal inadequacy more than being superseded), and a person can fear being replaced without dominant failure feelings (dreading being superseded more than personal inadequacy). In practice, the two patterns often co-occur in competitive environments, and CBT for one will often address the other. The self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000), ACT values-based unique contribution (Hayes 1999), and self-compassion (Neff 2003) tools on this page serve both patterns: they build the capacity to define competence intrinsically (fear of failure) and the capacity to hold worth independent of the slot (fear of being replaced), because both capacities come from the same root — a worth base that is in who one is rather than in one's performance or one's irreplaceability.

How is fear of being replaced different from fear of abandonment?

Fear of being replaced and fear of abandonment are related but distinct. Fear of abandonment is the anxiety about a person leaving — the fear that a specific relationship will end and one will be alone, that a particular person who one depends on will withdraw their presence, love, or attention. Fear of being replaced is the anxiety about someone else filling one's place — the fear that another person will occupy the role one occupies, do what one does, and that the fillability will make one redundant. The overlap is substantial: abandonment can trigger fear of being replaced (if this person leaves, someone else will take my place in their life), and fear of being replaced can intensify fear of abandonment (if I am replaceable, I will be abandoned for someone better). But the primary distortion differs: fear of abandonment's distortion is about a specific relationship (will this person stay), fear of being replaced's distortion is about one's role or place (will someone else fill it). A person can fear abandonment without dominant replacement feelings (dreading the specific loss more than being superseded), and a person can fear being replaced without dominant abandonment feelings (dreading being superseded in a role more than losing a specific relationship). The key distinction: abandonment is about who will stay with me, being replaced is about whether my place can be filled. CBT for fear of abandonment builds attachment security (the capacity to be alone without collapsing); CBT for fear of being replaced builds uniqueness-worth decoupling (the capacity to have one's place filled without collapsing). Both capacities are reinforced by self-compassion (Neff 2003), which builds the internal base that makes both aloneness and replaceability less devastating.

Why does self-determination theory help with fear of being replaced?

Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 2000) helps with fear of being replaced because it provides the deeper frame for understanding the competence need. Deci & Ryan's core insight: humans have three basic psychological needs — autonomy (the sense of volition in one's actions), competence (the sense of effectiveness in one's interactions), and relatedness (the sense of connection with others). The competence need is the one most relevant to fear of being replaced. When competence is defined comparatively (am I better than others, am I the only one who can do this, am I irreplaceable), the need is never satisfied — because there is always someone who can do some part of what one does, and the comparative ground is always shifting as others develop. The comparative definition produces the chronic competitive anxiety, the constant monitoring of colleagues' progress, and the inability to celebrate others' success that characterize fear of being replaced. When competence is defined intrinsically (am I developing my own capacities, am I growing, am I contributing from my own strengths, am I mastering the skills that matter to me), the need is satisfied by one's own development regardless of others — another's success is irrelevant to one's competence satisfaction, because one's competence is measured against one's own past, not against others' present. Self-determination theory helps in three ways: (1) it reveals the mechanism — the competitive anxiety is driven by the comparative competence definition, and understanding the mechanism allows the person to shift to the intrinsic definition; (2) it provides the alternative — the intrinsic competence definition satisfies the need without requiring irreplaceability, and the satisfaction reduces the drive to guard one's role; (3) it connects to relatedness — the relatedness need is satisfied by genuine collaboration (which role-guarding prevents), and the satisfaction of relatedness reduces the fear of replacement (because the connection is real and is not contingent on being the only one). Combined with ACT (Hayes 1999), which provides the values-based contribution that is the alternative to the role-guarding, and self-compassion (Neff 2003), which makes the fear survivable, self-determination theory makes fear of being replaced approachable — the combination that reduces the fear at its root.
