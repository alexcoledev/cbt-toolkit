What is Pyromania?

Pyromania (DSM-5 312.33) is an impulse-control disorder characterized by deliberate and purposeful fire-setting on more than one occasion. The fire-setting is impulsive (not planned for profit, political expression, or vengeance), driven by tension or affective arousal before the act and producing fascination with, attraction to, or gratification from fire and its situational contexts (watching the fire, the fire trucks, the aftermath). DSM-5 diagnostic criteria: (1) Deliberate and purposeful fire-setting on more than one occasion. (2) Tension or affective arousal before the act. (3) Fascination with, interest in, curiosity about, or attraction to fire and its situational contexts and consequences (watching fires, fire trucks, fire settings, aftermath). (4) Pleasure, gratification, or relief when setting fires or when witnessing or participating in their aftermath. (5) The fire-setting is not done for monetary gain, as an expression of political ideology, to conceal criminal activity, to express anger or vengeance, to improve living circumstances, or in response to a delusion or hallucination. (6) The fire-setting is not better explained by conduct disorder, a manic episode, or antisocial personality disorder. Key features: the fire-setting is impulsive (not for profit or purpose), the tension-relief cycle is the core mechanism (tension builds → fire-setting provides relief/gratification → relief is short-lived → tension builds again), the fascination with fire is a diagnostic feature, and the behavior produces significant shame, guilt, and distress. Typical triggers: stress, negative emotions (sadness, anxiety, anger, boredom), fascination opportunities (access to fire, matches, accelerants), exposure to fire-related stimuli. Comorbidity: high rates of mood disorders (major depression ~40-60%), anxiety disorders (~30-50%), substance use disorders (~30-50%, especially alcohol), and antisocial personality disorder (~20-40%). Course: chronic without treatment, often beginning in childhood or adolescence. CBT with covert sensitization produces ~50-70% reduction in fire-setting episodes in research trials.

Why Pyromania Needs Specific CBT

General impulse-control tools say "just resist the urge." General anxiety tools address fear but miss the gratification and fascination components. ODD/CD tools address defiance and rule violations but miss the tension-relief cycle. IED tools address aggressive impulses but miss the fire-specific distortions. Kleptomania tools address stealing impulses but miss the fascination and the unique danger of fire. Ordinary arson interventions address criminal behavior but miss the psychiatric impulse-control mechanism. None of these fit pyromania: you have recurrent fire-setting with tension before and relief/gratification after, the cognitive distortions about the urge being unstoppable, about being able to control the fire, about fire being the only relief, about being drawn to fire, about one more fire not mattering, and about fire-setting being empowering are the primary drivers of the behavior, the urge-fatalism produces the fire-setting by treating the impulse as unstoppable, the control-illusion produces the fire-setting by treating escalation risk as negligible, the relief-narrowing prevents alternative coping by treating fire-setting as the only option, the fascination-fatalism prevents resistance by treating the draw as destiny, the incremental-minimization prevents commitment to change by treating each episode as negligible, and the power-gratification reduces shame by treating fire-setting as empowering. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT can target directly:

The "The Urge To Set Fire Is Too Strong To Resist" Urge-Fatalism Distortion

Distortion: "The tension is unbearable. I cannot fight it. The impulse is in control. The urge is too strong. When the tension builds I have to act. I have no choice when the urge hits."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of the urge feeling overwhelming from the urge-fatalism and examining the evidence rather than treating the impulse as unstoppable. The urge-fatalism distortion (the tension is unbearable, I cannot fight it, the impulse is in control) treats the fire-setting urge as an unstoppable force. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where the urge is strong but NOT involuntary (I retain agency throughout), neurobiological research shows the urge follows a predictable trajectory (trigger → tension buildup → urge → decision point → action or coping → aftermath) with multiple decision points where intervention is possible, the 'too strong' experience reflects underdeveloped urge-surfing skills (the urge feels unmanageable because the skills have not been built, not because the urge is actually unmanageable), research shows people with pyromania CAN resist the urge in some contexts (they rarely set fires when consequences are immediate or when family is present) which proves the capacity exists, the urge-fatalism prevents the urge-surfing and distress tolerance skill-building that would reduce fire-setting (if the urge is too strong, there is no point trying), CBT and covert sensitization have demonstrated efficacy for pyromania (~50-70% reduction in fire-setting episodes) which would be impossible if the urges were truly unmanageable, the urge-fatalism is often reinforced by the relief of attributing the fire-setting to an external force (it feels better to say 'the urge took over' than 'I chose to set fire'), and the urge-fatalism is a learned cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target and restructure. CBT helps by separating the real experience of the urge feeling overwhelming (the urge IS strong and the tension IS real) from the urge-fatalism (therefore I cannot resist, therefore fire-setting is the only option), building urge-surfing skills (riding the urge without acting, noticing it peaks and passes in 20-30 minutes), and teaching distress tolerance that proves the urge is survivable."

The "I Can Control The Fire" Control-Illusion Distortion

Distortion: "I set small fires. I am careful. It will not spread. I watch it closely. I have done this many times without incident. I know what I am doing. The fire is contained."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of having set contained fires from the control-illusion and examining the evidence rather than treating escalation risk as negligible. The control-illusion distortion (I set small fires, I am careful, it will not spread, I watch it closely) treats the fire as manageable and the risk of escalation as negligible. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where fire is by nature unpredictable and uncontrollable (wind shifts, hidden fuel sources, accelerant behavior, structural factors can cause rapid escalation regardless of intent), research shows ~40-60% of fires set by people with pyromania cause unintended damage (the base rate of escalation is far higher than the control-illusion assumes), the consequences of escalation are catastrophic (property destruction, injury, death, criminal prosecution including felony arson charges, civil liability, lifelong sex-offender registry in some jurisdictions) and far exceed whatever momentary relief the fire-setting provided, the control-illusion is reinforced by the fires that did NOT escalate (the distortion feels true because many small fires do not result in immediate consequences, but this is gambler's fallacy — past containment does not reduce future risk), modern fire investigation is highly sophisticated (the risk of detection is increasing, not decreasing, with DNA evidence, surveillance cameras, and fire-debris analysis), the control-illusion prevents the risk-awareness and impulse control that would reduce fire-setting (if I can control it, there is no reason to resist), and the control-illusion is a cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target. CBT helps by separating the real experience of having set contained fires (the past IS real) from the control-illusion (therefore I will always control it, therefore the risk is zero), examining the actual probability and consequences of escalation, and using covert sensitization to make the consequences vivid and salient in the moment of the urge."

The "Fire Is The Only Way To Relieve The Tension" Relief-Narrowing Distortion

Distortion: "Nothing else relieves the tension. Fire is the only option. Other activities do not work. The tension only goes away if I set a fire. I have tried other things and they do not help."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of fire-setting providing relief from the relief-narrowing and examining the evidence rather than treating fire-setting as the sole tension-relief mechanism. The relief-narrowing distortion (nothing else relieves the tension, fire is the only option, other activities do not work) treats fire-setting as the sole available tension-relief mechanism. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where the tension-relief cycle is the core maintaining mechanism (tension builds → fire-setting provides relief → relief is short-lived → tension builds again), the relief from fire-setting is temporary and paradoxically increases overall tension (the relief lasts minutes to hours, but the guilt, shame, fear of detection, and reinforcement of the cycle produce more tension over time), research shows multiple alternative tension-relief mechanisms are effective for pyromania urges (urge-surfing, deep breathing, exercise, social support, competing response training, mindfulness), the relief-narrowing is often reinforced by the fact that fire-setting DOES provide immediate relief (the distortion feels true in the moment because the relief is real and fast), but the relief is not unique to fire-setting (the same relief can be achieved through urge-surfing, which studies show produces comparable tension reduction without the consequences), the relief-narrowing prevents the exploration and skill-building of alternative coping that would reduce fire-setting (if fire is the only option, there is no reason to try alternatives), and the relief-narrowing is a cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target. CBT helps by separating the real experience of fire-setting providing relief (the relief IS real) from the relief-narrowing (therefore it is the ONLY option), building a menu of alternative tension-relief activities, and practicing urge-surfing and competing responses that prove the tension is survivable without fire-setting."

The "I Am Drawn To Fire" Fascination-Fatalism Distortion

Distortion: "Fire fascinates me. I cannot look away. The pull is irresistible. I am drawn to fire. Watching fire is the only thing that calms me. The fascination means I must act. I am a fire person."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of the fascination feeling compelling from the fascination-fatalism and examining the evidence rather than treating the draw to fire as destiny. The fascination-fatalism distortion (fire fascinates me, I cannot look away, the pull is irresistible, I am drawn to fire) treats the fascination with fire as an unstoppable destiny. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where fascination with fire is a diagnostic feature but NOT a mandate to act (the person retains agency throughout — fascination is a feeling, fire-setting is a choice), neurobiological research shows the fascination follows a predictable trajectory (exposure → curiosity → fascination → urge → decision point → action or coping → aftermath) with multiple decision points where intervention is possible, the 'irresistible pull' experience reflects underdeveloped alternative-engagement skills (the pull feels irresistible because alternative sources of fascination and engagement have not been built, not because the pull is actually irresistible), research shows people with pyromania CAN resist the fascination in some contexts (they rarely act on the fascination when consequences are immediate or when family is present) which proves the capacity exists, the fascination-fatalism prevents the urge-surfing and alternative-engagement skill-building that would reduce fire-setting (if the pull is irresistible, there is no point trying), CBT and covert sensitization have demonstrated efficacy for pyromania (~50-70% reduction in fire-setting episodes) which would be impossible if the fascination were truly irresistible, the fascination-fatalism is often reinforced by the relief of attributing the fire-setting to an intrinsic identity (it feels better to say 'I am a fire person' than 'I chose to set fire'), and the fascination-fatalism is a cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target. CBT helps by separating the real experience of the fascination feeling compelling (the fascination IS real and the draw IS strong) from the fascination-fatalism (therefore I cannot resist, therefore fire-setting is the only option), building urge-surfing skills (riding the fascination without acting, noticing it peaks and passes), and teaching distress tolerance and alternative engagement that prove the fascination is survivable."

The "One More Fire Won't Matter" Incremental-Minimization Distortion

Distortion: "One more fire is nothing. I can stop anytime. This is the last time. I have already done it so many times that one more does not matter. I will quit after this. The damage is already done."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of a single episode being small from the incremental-minimization and examining the evidence rather than treating each episode as negligible. The incremental-minimization distortion (one more fire is nothing, I can stop anytime, this is the last time) treats each individual episode as negligible and promises future cessation. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where the recurrent nature means each episode reinforces the neural pathway and the behavioral pattern (each 'one more fire' strengthens the habit and makes the next urge stronger), the 'I can stop anytime' belief is contradicted by the recurrent failure to stop (the person has likely told themselves this many times and not stopped, which is the definition of impaired control), the 'this is the last time' promise is the most predictive sign that it will NOT be the last time (research shows this promise is associated with continued fire-setting, not cessation), the incremental-minimization prevents the commitment and lifestyle change that would actually reduce fire-setting (if one more does not matter, there is no urgency to change), each episode carries real risk (escalation, injury, death, arrest, prosecution, consequences that cannot be undone) — the risk does not diminish with repetition, it accumulates, the incremental-minimization is often reinforced by the relief each episode provides (the distortion feels true because the immediate consequence of any single episode is usually small), and the incremental-minimization is a cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target. CBT helps by separating the real experience of a single episode being small (any one episode IS a small event) from the incremental-minimization (therefore the pattern does not matter, therefore I can stop anytime), examining the cumulative risk and the reinforcement dynamics, and building commitment to cessation with lapse prevention planning."

The "Setting Fire Makes Me Feel Power" Power-Gratification Distortion

Distortion: "Fire gives me power. Watching it burn feels good. I am in control when I set the fire. The fire does what I want. Setting fires makes me feel alive. I am powerful when I create destruction."
Reframe: "I am separating the real experience of feeling a moment of agency in ignition from the power-gratification and examining the evidence rather than treating fire-setting as empowering. The power-gratification distortion (fire gives me power, watching it burn feels good, I am in control when I set the fire) treats fire-setting as a source of power and gratification. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 impulse-control disorder (312.33) where the fire-setting is by definition an impulse-control failure, not an act of power (the person is driven by the urge, not in control of it — the power-gratification is the mind's attempt to reframe the loss of control as control), research shows the power-gratification is a post-hoc rationalization that serves to reduce the shame and guilt (the distortion makes the fire-setting feel powerful rather than impulsive), the 'I am in control' rationalization is contradicted by the recurrent failure to resist the urge (if the person were in control, they could stop — the power-gratification masks the loss of agency), the 'fire does what I want' rationalization ignores that fire is by nature unpredictable and uncontrollable (the fire is in control, not the person — the power-gratification is an illusion), the power-gratification prevents the honest appraisal and impulse control that would reduce fire-setting (if fire-setting makes me powerful, there is no reason to resist), the power-gratification is often reinforced by the momentary sense of control that fire-setting provides (the distortion feels true because the act of ignition IS a moment of agency, but it is agency used to surrender agency to the fire), and the power-gratification is a cognitive pattern that CBT can directly target. CBT helps by separating the real experience of feeling a moment of agency in ignition (the moment IS real and the desire for control IS legitimate) from the power-gratification (therefore fire-setting is power, therefore I am in control), examining the evidence about whether fire-setting is actually power or is a surrender to impulse, and building genuine power through mastery, achievement, and constructive control."

These reframes are not toxic positivity, denial of the real distress of the fire-setting cycle, or minimizing the genuine challenges that pyromania creates for individuals, families, and communities. They are evidence-based cognitive restructuring — the core CBT techniques, adapted for the pyromania context. The tools on this page walk you through generating your own reframes using your actual situation, your actual fire-setting experiences, and your actual trigger-urge-response patterns, not generic affirmations.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Pyromania

CBT for pyromania combines three evidence-based components:

  1. Cognitive restructuring — identify and challenge the urge-fatalism (the urge to set fire is too strong to resist), the control-illusion (I can control the fire), the relief-narrowing (fire is the only way to relieve the tension), the fascination-fatalism (I am drawn to fire), the incremental-minimization (one more fire won't matter), and the power-gratification (setting fire makes me feel power). Use urge logs (situation, tension level 0-10, urge strength 0-10, thoughts, action taken, relief level 0-10, guilt level 0-10) to map the tension-relief cycle. Use Socratic questioning (what is the evidence that the urge is unmanageable? what would you tell a friend? is there another way to relieve the tension? what are the actual probabilities of escalation?). Build reframes that separate the real challenges from the distortions. Identify and challenge the post-hoc rationalizations that reduce guilt (power-gratification, minimization, fascination-fatalism) separately from the pre-hoc distortions that produce the fire-setting (urge-fatalism, relief-narrowing, control-illusion).
  2. Covert sensitization — an evidence-based CBT technique specifically effective for pyromania and other impulse-control disorders. The technique: (a) Identify high-risk situations — the specific locations, times, emotional states, and fire-access opportunities that trigger the fire-setting urge. (b) Vividly imagine the negative consequences — while feeling the urge, imagine the full sequence of negative outcomes: the fire escalating beyond control, property destruction, the sound of sirens, injury to self or others, the possibility of death, arrest, handcuffs, the shame of a public arrest, the phone call to family, the felony arson charge, the criminal record, the sex-offender registry, the loss of employment, the loss of relationships, the civil lawsuit. (c) Make the imagery multisensory and vivid — not just 'I might get caught' but the specific sights (flames spreading, smoke, destruction), sounds (sirens, alarms, shouting), feelings (heat, fear, shame), and social consequences of escalation and detection. (d) Practice repeatedly — the aversive imagery is practiced in session and as homework, paired with the urge, so that the urge itself begins to elicit the aversive association. (e) Pair with alternative behavior — after the aversive imagery, immediately practice an alternative coping response (urge-surfing, leaving the area, deep breathing, calling a support person). (f) Graduated exposure — begin with imagining low-risk urges and progress to high-risk urges. Over weeks to months, covert sensitization builds an aversive association with the fire-setting urge that competes with the gratification, making the urge easier to resist.
  3. Stimulus control + alternative behavior + comorbid treatment — change the environment and build alternative coping. The technique: (a) Avoid high-risk situations — identify the locations, times, emotional states, and fire-access opportunities that trigger the urge and avoid them when possible (remove matches and lighters, avoid fire-related media when emotionally triggered, avoid locations with fire access). (b) Competing response training — when the urge arises, immediately engage in a physically incompatible response (clench fists, cross arms, leave the area, hold phone) for 60 seconds until the urge peaks and passes. (c) Urge-surfing — ride the urge without acting, noticing that it peaks at 7-8/10 and passes within 20-30 minutes, building the evidence that the urge is survivable. (d) Alternative tension-relief — build a menu of alternative coping (deep breathing, exercise, social support, mindfulness, creative activity, sensory grounding) and practice using them for the tension that triggers the urge. (e) Alternative engagement — address the fascination that the fascination-fatalism says fire satisfies, through genuine engagement, constructive interests, mastery activities, and treatment of comorbid conditions. (f) Comorbid treatment — if mood disorder is comorbid (~40-60%), pursue depression treatment (CBT, SSRI); if anxiety is comorbid (~30-50%), pursue anxiety treatment (CBT, SSRI if severe); if substance use is comorbid (~30-50%), pursue substance treatment; if antisocial personality disorder is comorbid (~20-40%), pursue appropriate treatment. (g) Medication — for severe cases (frequent fire-setting, legal consequences, significant impairment), SSRIs, mood stabilizers, or antipsychotics in some cases may complement CBT. (h) Support groups — consider impulse-control support groups for ongoing support and accountability.

This 3-component protocol addresses the cognitive distortions (component 1), the gratification-aversion balance (component 2), and the environmental triggers and alternative coping (component 3). The components work together: cognitive restructuring reduces the distortions that drive the fire-setting, covert sensitization builds aversion that competes with the gratification, and stimulus control + alternative behavior change the environment and build the coping capacity. For severe cases (frequent fire-setting, legal consequences, significant impairment), medication (SSRIs, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics) may complement CBT.

Key Distinctions from Related Impulse-Control & Conduct Disorders

Pyromania vs. Ordinary Arson / Criminal Fire-Setting

Pyromania = impulsive, tension before + relief/gratification after, fascination with fire, not for profit or purpose, accompanied by shame and distress. Ordinary arson = planned, premeditated, for monetary gain, political expression, concealment of crime, vengeance, or insurance fraud, no tension-relief cycle, no distress about the act (only about getting caught). The key distinction: if there is a tension-relief cycle and the fire-setting is not for profit or purpose, it is pyromania; if the fire-setting is planned and for a purpose, it is ordinary arson. Pyromania is a psychiatric disorder; ordinary arson is criminal behavior. The two can co-occur but have different treatment implications. DSM-5 explicitly excludes pyromania diagnosis if the fire-setting is for monetary gain, political expression, concealment of crime, or vengeance.

Pyromania vs. Conduct Disorder (CD)

Pyromania = impulsive fire-setting driven by tension-relief cycle, fascination with fire, accompanied by shame and distress. CD (DSM-5 312.81) = persistent pattern of aggression, deceit, theft, and serious rule violations, often instrumental (goal-directed), without the tension-relief cycle and without distress about the behavior. The key distinction: if the fire-setting is impulsive with tension before and relief after, and there is fascination with fire, it is pyromania; if the fire-setting is part of a broader pattern of instrumental aggression, deceit, and rule violations, it is CD. CD is diagnosed in children/adolescents; pyromania can be diagnosed at any age. DSM-5 excludes pyromania diagnosis if the fire-setting is better explained by CD.

Pyromania vs. Intermittent Explosive Disorder (IED)

Pyromania = recurrent fire-setting, tension before + relief/gratification after, fascination with fire. IED (DSM-5 312.84) = discrete episodes of impulsive aggression (verbal or physical) out of proportion to provocation, tension before + relief after. Both are impulse-control disorders with a tension-relief cycle, but the impulse target differs: pyromania = fire-setting, IED = aggression. The key distinction: if the impulse is to set fire, it is pyromania; if the impulse is to aggress, it is IED. Both respond to CBT but with different techniques (covert sensitization for pyromania, aggression management for IED).

Pyromania vs. Kleptomania

Pyromania = recurrent fire-setting, tension before + relief/gratification after, fascination with fire. Kleptomania (DSM-5 312.32) = recurrent failure to resist stealing impulses, tension before + relief/gratification after, items not needed. Both are impulse-control disorders with a tension-relief cycle, but the impulse target differs: pyromania = fire-setting, kleptomania = stealing. The key distinction: if the impulse is to set fire, it is pyromania; if the impulse is to steal, it is kleptomania. Both are rare and both respond to CBT with covert sensitization.

Pyromania vs. Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder)

Pyromania = recurrent fire-setting, tension before + relief/gratification after, fascination with fire. Trichotillomania (DSM-5 312.311) = recurrent hair pulling with tension before and relief/gratification after, noticeable hair loss. Both are impulse-control disorders with a tension-relief cycle, but the impulse target differs: pyromania = fire-setting, trichotillomania = hair pulling. The key distinction: if the impulse is to set fire, it is pyromania; if the impulse is to pull hair, it is trichotillomania. Trichotillomania is classified as an obsessive-compulsive related disorder and has stronger evidence for habit reversal training (HRT); pyromania has stronger evidence for covert sensitization.

Pyromania vs. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Pyromania = ego-syntonic in the moment (gratifying, fascination-satisfying), tension before + gratification after, the person does not want to have the urge but the act itself provides pleasure. OCD = ego-dystonic (the obsessions and compulsions are unwanted and produce distress, not gratification), anxiety before + relief after but no gratification. The key distinction: if the act provides gratification or pleasure (not just relief from anxiety), it is pyromania; if the act provides only relief from anxiety and is experienced as unwanted, it is OCD. Both respond to CBT but with different techniques (covert sensitization for pyromania, exposure and response prevention for OCD).

When to See a Professional

These tools are self-help based on CBT principles, useful for the cognitive distortions that drive fire-setting impulses and for the covert sensitization and stimulus control techniques that complement professional treatment, not a substitute for a psychologist, a psychiatrist, or therapy with a trained clinician. If you are experiencing recurrent fire-setting impulses, especially impulses that have led to arrest, legal consequences, property damage, injury, significant shame or distress, impairment in work or relationships, or co-occurring mood, anxiety, substance, or antisocial disorders, please consult a healthcare provider or a mental health professional for proper diagnosis and treatment. Pyromania is a recognized condition (DSM-5 312.33) that often benefits from CBT with a trained therapist (cognitive restructuring, covert sensitization, stimulus control), evaluation for comorbid disorders, and sometimes medication (SSRIs, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics in some cases) for severe cases. CBT is most effective as a complement to professional treatment, especially for the cognitive distortions that maintain the fire-setting cycle and the covert sensitization that builds aversion to the urge. If you are experiencing severe distress, thoughts of self-harm, or any condition affecting safety, please contact a licensed mental health professional or a crisis line immediately. Frequently Asked Questions

Can CBT help with pyromania?

Yes. CBT for pyromania addresses the cognitive distortions that drive recurrent fire-setting impulses: urge-fatalism (the urge is too strong), control-illusion (I can control the fire), relief-narrowing (fire is the only way), fascination-fatalism (I am drawn to fire), incremental-minimization (one more fire won't matter), and power-gratification (setting fire makes me feel power). CBT helps through cognitive restructuring, covert sensitization, and stimulus control + alternative behavior. CBT produces ~50-70% reduction in fire-setting episodes in research trials, especially when covert sensitization is included.

I feel like the urge to set fire is too strong to resist. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a core urge-fatalism distortion. The distortion is the urge-fatalism (the tension is unbearable, I cannot fight it) that treats the fire-setting urge as unstoppable. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where the urge is strong but NOT involuntary, the 'too strong' experience reflects underdeveloped urge-surfing skills (not actually unmanageable urges), and people with pyromania CAN resist the urge in some contexts (proving the capacity exists). CBT helps by separating the real experience of the urge feeling overwhelming from the urge-fatalism, building urge-surfing skills (riding the urge without acting, noticing it peaks and passes in 20-30 minutes), and teaching distress tolerance. The urge is real; the conclusion that it is unmanageable is the distortion.

I feel like I can control the fire. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a common control-illusion distortion. The distortion is the control-illusion (I set small fires, I am careful, it will not spread) that treats fire as manageable. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where fire is by nature unpredictable and uncontrollable, research shows ~40-60% of fires set by people with pyromania cause unintended damage, and modern fire investigation is highly sophisticated. CBT helps by separating the real experience of having set contained fires from the control-illusion, examining the actual probability and consequences of escalation, and using covert sensitization to make the consequences vivid. The past containment is real; the conclusion that you will always control it is the distortion.

I feel like fire is the only way to relieve the tension. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a core relief-narrowing distortion. The distortion is the relief-narrowing (nothing else relieves the tension, fire is the only option) that treats fire-setting as the sole tension-relief mechanism. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where the relief from fire-setting is temporary and paradoxically increases overall tension, and research shows multiple alternative tension-relief mechanisms are effective (urge-surfing, deep breathing, exercise, social support, competing responses). CBT helps by separating the real experience of fire-setting providing relief from the relief-narrowing, building a menu of alternative tension-relief activities, and practicing urge-surfing. The relief is real; the conclusion that it is the only option is the distortion.

I feel like I am drawn to fire and cannot look away. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a core fascination-fatalism distortion. The distortion is the fascination-fatalism (fire fascinates me, I cannot look away, the pull is irresistible) that treats the fascination with fire as destiny. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where fascination is a diagnostic feature but NOT a mandate to act, the 'irresistible pull' reflects underdeveloped alternative-engagement skills, and people with pyromania CAN resist the fascination in some contexts. CBT helps by separating the real experience of the fascination feeling compelling from the fascination-fatalism, building urge-surfing skills, and teaching alternative engagement. The fascination is real; the conclusion that it is irresistible is the distortion.

I feel like one more fire won't matter. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a common incremental-minimization distortion that maintains the cycle. The distortion is the incremental-minimization (one more fire is nothing, I can stop anytime, this is the last time) that treats each episode as negligible. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where each episode reinforces the neural pathway, the 'I can stop anytime' belief is contradicted by recurrent failure to stop, and the 'this is the last time' promise is predictive of continued fire-setting. CBT helps by separating the real experience of a single episode being small from the incremental-minimization, examining the cumulative risk and reinforcement dynamics, and building commitment to cessation. Any one episode is small; the conclusion that the pattern does not matter is the distortion.

I feel like setting fire makes me feel powerful. Can CBT help?

Yes, and this is a common power-gratification distortion. The distortion is the power-gratification (fire gives me power, watching it burn feels good, I am in control) that treats fire-setting as empowering. The reality is that pyromania is a recognized DSM-5 condition (312.33) where the fire-setting is an impulse-control failure not an act of power, the power-gratification is a post-hoc rationalization that reduces shame, and fire is by nature uncontrollable (the fire is in control, not the person). CBT helps by separating the real experience of feeling a moment of agency in ignition from the power-gratification, examining whether fire-setting is actually power or is a surrender to impulse, and building genuine power through mastery and achievement. The moment of agency is real; the conclusion that fire-setting is power is the distortion.

What is covert sensitization and how does it work for pyromania?

Covert sensitization is an evidence-based CBT technique specifically effective for pyromania. The technique: (1) Identify high-risk situations — the specific locations, times, and emotional states that trigger the fire-setting urge. (2) Vividly imagine the negative consequences — while feeling the urge, imagine the full sequence: the fire escalating, property destruction, sirens, injury, arrest, the felony arson charge, the criminal record. (3) Make the imagery multisensory and vivid. (4) Practice repeatedly — the aversive imagery is paired with the urge so the urge itself begins to elicit the aversive association. (5) Pair with alternative behavior — after the aversive imagery, practice an alternative coping response. (6) Graduated exposure — begin with low-risk urges and progress to high-risk. Over weeks to months, covert sensitization builds an aversive association that competes with the gratification, making the urge easier to resist.
