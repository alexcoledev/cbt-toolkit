Dissociation is the disconnection between thoughts, identity, memory, and surroundings — a trauma response where the mind detaches from an overwhelming experience that it cannot process directly. Depersonalization is the feeling of being detached from oneself or one's body: watching yourself from outside, feeling your body is not yours, feeling like a robot or an automaton. Derealization is the feeling that the external world is unreal, dream-like, foggy, distant, or made of cardboard. Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder (DPDR) affects ~1-2% of the population; ~30-50% of trauma survivors experience dissociation at some point. The harm is in the cycle: the trauma triggers the dissociation, the dissociation produces the I-Am-Not-Real and the World-Is-Not-Real, the I-Am-Not-Real produces the I-Am-Losing-My-Mind (fear of psychosis), the I-Am-Losing-My-Mind produces anxiety that deepens the dissociation, the I-Will-Disappear and the I-Am-Watching-Myself and the My-Body-Is-Not-Mine reinforce the detachment, and the whole pattern confirms that the self is gone. CBT is evidence-based for dissociation (CBT-TR, trauma-focused CBT, NICE-recommended for trauma responses). CBT works by identifying the cognitive distortions layered on the dissociation, examining the evidence (the dissociation is a protective response, not a permanent breakdown; the fear of psychosis is evidence of intact reality testing), practicing grounding skills (5-4-3-2-1 sensory reorientation, body scan, present-moment anchoring), and rebuilding the connection to self, body, and present moment. What is Depersonalization & Dissociation?

In psychiatry, depersonalization is a dissociative phenomenon characterized by a subjective feeling of detachment from oneself, manifesting as a sense of disconnection from one's thoughts, emotions, sensations, or actions, and often accompanied by a feeling of observing oneself from an external perspective. Those affected often feel as though they are observing the world from a distance, as if separated by a barrier "behind glass". They maintain insight into the subjective nature of their experience, recognizing that it pertains to their own perception rather than altering objective reality. This distinction between subjective experience and objective reality distinguishes depersonalization from delusions, where individuals firmly believe in false perceptions as genuine truths. Depersonalization is also distinct from derealization, which involves a sense of detachment from the external world rather than from oneself.

Why Dissociation Needs Specific CBT

General PTSD tools say "you had a trauma, let's process the intrusions." General C-PTSD tools say "you had repeated trauma, let's rebuild the self-organization." General anxiety tools say "you worry, let's examine the worry." General psychosis tools say "you have lost touch with reality, let's restore reality testing with medication." None of them address the specifics: the I-Am-Not-Real detaches the self from the body in a way general PTSD tools cannot address because the detachment is the primary experience not a trauma intrusion, the World-Is-Not-Real makes the surroundings feel unreal in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the unreality is a dissociative symptom not a worry, the I-Am-Losing-My-Mind misinterprets dissociation as psychosis in a way general psychosis tools cannot address because the reality testing is intact (the fear of psychosis is the proof you are not psychotic), the I-Will-Disappear catastrophizes the detachment as annihilation in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the annihilation is about the self dissolving not about a future threat, the I-Am-Watching-Myself traps the self in observer-mode in a way general identity tools cannot address because the observer is a dissociated part not a general identity question, and the My-Body-Is-Not-Mine severs ownership of the body in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the severing is a trauma response not a body-image concern. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT for dissociation can target directly:

The "I-Am-Not-Real" Distortion (Depersonalization / Dissociation-As-Safety)

Distortion: "I am not real. I am watching myself from outside, and the watching is the one thing that means I am not here, and the not-here is the one thing that means I am a ghost, and the ghost is the one thing that means I am permanently gone, and the permanently-gone is the one thing that means I will never return, and the never-return is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the I-Am-Not-Real. The I-Am-Not-Real (I am watching myself from outside) was a depersonalization and a dissociation-as-safety response: the mind detached the self from the body to survive an overwhelming experience, and because the detachment feels like evidence of non-existence, the inference is that the self is permanently gone. The depersonalization (I am not real, I am a ghost) is the distortion: the detachment is real, but the conclusion that the self is permanently gone is the misinterpretation of a protective response as a permanent state — the dissociation is the mind's way of surviving, not the mind's way of ending. The dissociation-as-safety (the mind detached to protect) is the reframe: the depersonalization is a trauma response that can be healed, not a permanent condition. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the self is permanently gone versus the evidence that the detachment is a state that comes and goes, what would a neutral observer say about a person who dissociates versus what they would say about the person's existence, what is the base rate of dissociation that resolves with grounding and trauma processing), challenging the depersonalization (the detachment is real, but the conclusion that the self is gone is the distortion), and practicing the grounding — the 5-4-3-2-1 sensory reorientation, the body scan, the present-moment anchoring, so the mind learns that the self returns and the detachment was the trauma's defense, not the self's end. The detachment is real; the conclusion that the self is permanently gone is the distortion.

The "World-Is-Not-Real" Distortion (Derealization / Dissociation-As-Trauma-Response)

Distortion: "The world is not real. Everything looks foggy and distant, and the foggy is the one thing that means the world is a dream, and the dream is the one thing that means nothing matters, and the nothing-matters is the one thing that means I am alone, and the alone is the one thing that means the isolation is permanent, and the permanent is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the World-Is-Not-Real. The World-Is-Not-Real (everything looks foggy and distant) was a derealization and a dissociation-as-trauma-response: the mind detached the perception of the external world to survive an overwhelming experience, and because the detachment feels like evidence of objective unreality, the inference is that the world is a dream. The derealization (the world is not real) is the distortion: the foggy perception is real, but the conclusion that the world is objectively unreal is the misinterpretation of a perceptual detachment as a fact about reality — the world is real; the perception of it is altered. The dissociation-as-trauma-response (the mind detached the perception to protect) is the reframe: the derealization is a trauma response that can be healed, not a revelation about the nature of reality. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the world is objectively unreal versus the evidence that the perception is altered, what would a neutral observer say about a person who experiences derealization versus what they would say about the world's reality, what is the base rate of derealization that resolves with grounding and trauma processing), challenging the derealization (the foggy perception is real, but the conclusion that the world is a dream is the distortion), and practicing the grounding — the sensory reorientation (touch a surface, name 5 things you can see, feel the temperature), so the mind learns that the world returns and the fog was the trauma's defense, not a property of the world. The foggy perception is real; the conclusion that the world is objectively unreal is the distortion.

The "I-Am-Losing-My-Mind" Distortion (Psychosis-Fear / Misinterpretation)

Distortion: "I am losing my mind. The dissociation is the proof, and the proof is the one thing that means I am going crazy, and the going-crazy is the one thing that means I will be institutionalized, and the institutionalized is the one thing that means I am permanently insane, and the permanently-insane is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the I-Am-Losing-My-Mind. The I-Am-Losing-My-Mind (the dissociation is the proof I am going crazy) was a psychosis-fear and a misinterpretation: the mind interprets the dissociative detachment as evidence of psychosis, and because the detachment is frightening, the inference is that it is the beginning of a psychotic break. The psychosis-fear (I am going crazy) is the distortion: the dissociation is real, but the conclusion that it means psychosis is the misinterpretation of a trauma response as a psychotic symptom — and critically, the very fact that you are frightened of going crazy is evidence that you are not. Psychotic disorders typically lack insight: the person believes the unreality is objective fact, not a symptom. The fear of psychosis (I am terrified I am losing my mind) is the proof of intact reality testing. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the dissociation is psychosis versus the evidence that it is a trauma response, what would a clinician say about a person who is frightened of going crazy versus what they would say about the person's reality testing, what is the base rate of dissociation that is trauma-related versus psychotic), challenging the misinterpretation (the dissociation is real, but the conclusion that it is psychosis is the distortion — the fear of psychosis is the proof you are not psychotic), and practicing the grounding and the education — learning that dissociation is a known, named, treatable trauma response, so the mind learns that the detachment is the trauma's defense, not the mind's collapse. The dissociation is real; the conclusion that it means psychosis is the distortion — and the fear of psychosis is the proof of intact reality testing.

If you are in crisis

Dissociation, especially when the detachment is intense, the derealization is consuming, or the I-Am-Losing-My-Mind is producing thoughts of self-harm, can coexist with severe distress, despair, or suicidal ideation. The dissociation is a trauma response; the conclusion that it means permanent breakdown is the distortion. If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, please reach out now:

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US): call or text 988
  • Samaritans (UK/ROI): call 116 123
  • NAMI HelpLine (US): 1-800-950-6264 or text "HelpLine" to 62640
  • ADAA (international directory): adaa.org/finding-help
  • ISSTD (International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation): isstd.org — hosts trauma-and-dissociation-specific resources and a provider directory

These tools are for the cognitive distortions that dissociation maintains. They are not a substitute for a trauma-trained therapist, a dissociation-informed clinician, or emergency help for a crisis that is escalating. Trauma-focused treatment for dissociation (stabilization, grounding, trauma processing, integration) is best done with a trained clinician — especially if the dissociation is severe, frequent, or accompanied by identity fragmentation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is dissociation the same as psychosis?

No. In dissociation, the person retains reality testing — they know the detachment or unreality is a feeling, not a fact (I feel like the world is unreal, but I know it is real). In psychosis, reality testing is impaired — the person believes the unreal experience is objectively true. The I-Am-Losing-My-Mind distortion in dissociation is the fear of psychosis, not psychosis itself: the very fact that you are frightened of going crazy is evidence that you are not. Psychotic disorders typically lack this insight. CBT for dissociation targets the misinterpretation of dissociation as psychosis; CBT for psychosis (with a psychiatrist) targets the impaired reality testing. Use these tools if you feel detached but know it is a feeling; seek a psychiatrist if you believe the unreality is objective fact.

Is dissociation the same as PTSD or C-PTSD?

No. Dissociation is a symptom that can appear in PTSD, C-PTSD, and as a standalone dissociative disorder. PTSD includes dissociation as one symptom among intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions, and hyperarousal — but the primary problem is the single-incident trauma. C-PTSD includes dissociation alongside the three self-organization disturbances — but the primary problem is the repeated/prolonged trauma. Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder (DPDR) is a standalone dissociative disorder where the dissociation is the primary problem. The I-Am-Not-Real, the World-Is-Not-Real, the I-Am-Watching-Myself are dissociation-specific patterns that general PTSD and C-PTSD tools miss because they target the trauma or self-organization, not the detachment itself. Use the PTSD tools if the primary problem is a single trauma; use the C-PTSD tools if the primary problem is repeated trauma; use these tools if the primary problem is the dissociation itself.

What is grounding and how does it help dissociation?

Grounding is the evidence-based first-line skill for dissociation. It works by reorienting the mind to the present moment through the five senses, which interrupts the dissociative detachment and signals to the brain that the overwhelming experience is over and it is safe to return to the body and the present. The 5-4-3-2-1 technique: name 5 things you can see, 4 things you can touch, 3 things you can hear, 2 things you can smell, 1 thing you can taste. Body scan: feel your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair, the temperature of the air on your skin. Present-moment anchoring: state the current date, location, and one fact about your present situation. Grounding is not a cure — it is the stabilization phase that makes the cognitive work possible. Until the mind can return to the present, examining the evidence for the I-Am-Not-Real feels like examining evidence from inside a dream. Grounding is the foundation; the CBT tools on this page are the cognitive restructuring that prevents the dissociation from returning.
