CBT vs Other Therapies: Which Is Right for You?

An honest, evidence-based comparison of the six most common therapy approaches — with a decision guide to help you choose.

Comparison guide · evidence over loyalty

If you're researching therapy options, you've probably found passionate advocates for each approach. This page cuts through the loyalty and compares CBT, psychodynamic therapy, ACT, DBT, medication, and humanistic therapy on what actually matters: what the evidence says, what each is best for, and how to decide.

No therapy is universally best. The right question isn't "which is better" but "which is better for your situation." Here's what the research says.

Each Approach Explained

A brief, honest summary of what each therapy does, what it's best for, and where it falls short.

CBT

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

The most researched form of psychotherapy. CBT identifies and changes distorted thought patterns and maladaptive behaviors. It's structured, present-focused, and skill-oriented — you learn techniques you can use independently after therapy ends.

How it works: Identify automatic thoughts → evaluate evidence → reframe distortions → behavioral experiments to test beliefs → practice new patterns.

Limitations: Can feel rigid or manualized. Less effective for complex personality disorders without adaptation. Present-focus may miss deep relational patterns.

Best for: Anxiety, depression, OCD, PTSD, insomnia, panic, phobias, health anxiety. Try our free CBT tools →
Psychodynamic

Psychodynamic / Psychoanalytic Therapy

Explores how unconscious patterns and early relationships shape current behavior and emotions. The therapeutic relationship itself is a key instrument — it reveals relational patterns as they happen in the room.

How it works: Free association → explore recurring themes → connect present patterns to past relationships → work through in the therapeutic relationship.

Limitations: Less structured (harder to know if it's working). Takes longer. Smaller evidence base for specific conditions. Less accessible and often more expensive.

Best for: Recurring relationship problems, identity issues, complex grief, when you want to understand origins of patterns rather than just manage symptoms.
ACT

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

A "third-wave" CBT variant. Instead of changing thought content (like CBT), ACT changes your relationship with thoughts — accepting them as mental events while committing to values-based action. Uses mindfulness heavily.

How it works: Accept difficult thoughts/feelings → defuse from them (they're not "you") → contact present moment → identify values → committed action.

Limitations: Newer (smaller evidence base than CBT). "Acceptance" can be misunderstood as giving up. Less structured for specific symptom reduction.

Best for: Chronic pain, when fighting thoughts makes things worse, values clarification, when you want flexibility rather than symptom elimination.
DBT

Dialectical Behavior Therapy

A CBT variant developed specifically for borderline personality disorder. Combines CBT's cognitive techniques with mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness skills. Delivered in both individual therapy and skills groups.

How it works: Weekly skills group + individual therapy → learn 4 skill modules → practice between sessions → phone coaching for crisis moments.

Limitations: Time-intensive (typically 6-12 months). Requires both group and individual therapy. Less flexible than standard CBT.

Best for: Borderline personality disorder, self-harm, intense emotional dysregulation, suicidal ideation, substance use with emotional dysregulation.
Medication

Psychiatric Medication (SSRIs, SNRIs, etc.)

Medication alters neurochemistry to reduce symptoms. Not a therapy in the traditional sense (no skills learned), but often the fastest way to reduce symptom severity. Works best alongside therapy for moderate-to-severe conditions.

How it works: Adjust serotonin/norepinephrine/dopamine levels → symptom reduction over 2-6 weeks → adjust dose based on response and side effects.

Limitations: Symptoms often return when stopped. Side effects (weight, sleep, sexual, emotional numbing). Trial-and-error to find the right medication. Does not teach coping skills.

Best for: Severe depression/anxiety (as first-line or adjunct), when symptoms are too severe to engage with therapy, bipolar disorder, psychosis. Consult a psychiatrist.
Humanistic

Humanistic / Person-Centered Therapy

Focuses on self-actualization, personal growth, and the therapeutic relationship. The therapist provides unconditional positive regard, empathy, and genuineness. Less directive than CBT — you lead, the therapist supports.

How it works: Therapist creates a warm, non-judgmental space → you explore at your own pace → self-understanding emerges → growth happens through the relationship.

Limitations: Less structured (harder to measure progress). Smaller evidence base for specific conditions. May be too unstructured for conditions needing active intervention (OCD, PTSD).

Best for: Self-exploration, life transitions, existential concerns, when you need to feel heard and understood, mild-moderate issues where the relationship is the main healing factor.

The honest truth

No therapy wins on every dimension. CBT has the strongest evidence for the most common conditions, but it's not universally best. The best therapy is the one that fits your situation, preferences, and the quality of the therapist delivering it. A skilled therapist matters more than the modality label.

Decision Guide: Which Therapy Is Right for Me?

Follow these steps. This is a starting point, not a diagnosis — a mental health professional can give you a personalized recommendation.

1
What are you experiencing? Anxiety, depression, OCD, PTSD, panic, phobias, insomnia, or health anxiety → CBT is first-line (strongest evidence, recommended by APA/NICE/WHO). Free CBT tools →
2
Intense emotions, self-harm, or relationship instability? → DBT is the gold standard for borderline personality and emotional dysregulation.
3
Fighting thoughts makes things worse? Chronic pain, or you're exhausted from trying to change thoughts → ACT teaches acceptance + values-based action instead.
4
Recurring relationship patterns or wanting to understand origins? → Psychodynamic explores how early relationships shape current patterns.
5
Symptoms are severe (can't get out of bed, can't function) → medication first to reduce symptoms enough to engage with therapy, then add CBT.
6
Want to feel heard and explore at your own pace? → humanistic / person-centered therapy provides a non-directive, empathetic space.
7
Not sure? → Start with CBT (most evidence, most accessible, teaches lasting skills). Many therapists integrate approaches and will adjust based on your response.

Can You Combine Approaches?

Yes — and many therapists do. Common integrations:

  • CBT + medication: For moderate-to-severe depression or anxiety, this combination often outperforms either alone.
  • CBT + psychodynamic: Start with CBT for symptom relief, add psychodynamic exploration for underlying patterns.
  • CBT + ACT: Use CBT to challenge specific distortions, ACT for broader acceptance of what can't be changed.
  • DBT skills + individual therapy: DBT is already an integration (CBT + mindfulness + skills training).

The best therapists are flexible — they use the approach that fits each client, not a single dogma.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is CBT better than psychodynamic therapy?

It depends on what you need. For anxiety, depression, OCD, and PTSD, CBT is as effective or more effective and is first-line per major guidelines. For complex interpersonal patterns or understanding recurring relationship problems, psychodynamic may be more appropriate. Many people benefit from elements of both.

What is the difference between CBT and ACT?

CBT changes the content of thoughts — replacing distorted thoughts with realistic ones. ACT changes your relationship with thoughts — accepting them as mental events while committing to values-based action. ACT is a third-wave therapy built on CBT foundations. Both are evidence-based.

Should I choose CBT or medication?

They aren't either-or. For moderate to severe depression or anxiety, CBT plus medication often outperforms either alone. For mild conditions, CBT alone is often sufficient. CBT teaches skills that persist after treatment; medication works only while you take it.

How is DBT different from CBT?

DBT is a CBT variant for borderline personality disorder. It adds mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness skills. If you struggle with intense emotions or self-harm, DBT may be more appropriate than standard CBT.

Which therapy is right for me?

Start with what you're experiencing. Anxiety/depression/OCD/PTSD → CBT. Intense emotions/self-harm → DBT. Wanting to accept rather than change thoughts → ACT. Recurring relationship patterns → psychodynamic. Severe symptoms → medication + therapy. A good therapist will help you choose.

Can I do CBT on my own without a therapist?

Self-help CBT using structured tools can be effective for mild to moderate issues. Our free CBT toolkit provides 36 interactive tools. However, self-help is not a substitute for professional therapy for moderate to severe symptoms.
