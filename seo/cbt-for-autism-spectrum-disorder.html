What is Autism Spectrum Disorder?

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD, DSM-5 299.00) is a neurodevelopmental condition diagnosed when there are persistent differences in: (1) Social communication and social interaction — differences in social-emotional reciprocity (back-and-forth social interaction), nonverbal communication (eye contact, facial expressions, body language), and developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships. (2) Restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities — stereotyped or repetitive motor movements (stimming), insistence on sameness and routines, highly restricted fixated interests, and hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input. These differences are present from early developmental periods, cause clinically significant impairment, and are not better explained by intellectual disability alone. Key features include: sensory differences (hyper- or hypo-sensitivity to sound, light, touch, taste, smell, pain, temperature), executive function differences (planning, flexibility, task switching), special interests (deep, focused interests that are strengths), stimming (self-regulatory repetitive movements), masking (suppressing autistic traits to appear neurotypical, which causes burnout), alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing emotions, ~50% of autistic adults), co-occurring conditions (anxiety ~40%, depression ~30%, ADHD ~30-40%), and neurobiological basis (differences in brain connectivity, synaptic pruning, and neural development). CBT for adults with ASD is evidence-based for co-occurring anxiety and depression, with neurodiversity-affirming adaptations.

How CBT Works for Adults with Autism

CBT for adults with autism follows a neurodiversity-affirming approach that adapts traditional CBT for autistic cognitive styles while respecting autistic identity:

  1. Neurodiversity-affirming stance — The therapist recognizes autism as natural neurological variation, not a disease to cure. The goal is reducing distress from co-occurring conditions and cognitive distortions, not making the person less autistic. Autistic identity is respected and valued.
  2. Adaptation for autistic cognitive style — Use concrete and literal language (less metaphor, more direct), incorporate special interests as therapeutic tools and motivators, recognize and accommodate sensory needs in sessions, use visual supports and written materials, allow stimming and self-regulation behaviors, and be explicit about social expectations in the therapeutic relationship.
  3. Cognitive restructuring of the 6 distortions — Target the The-I-Am-Just-Broken, The-I-Should-Be-Able-To-Just-Be-Normal, The-It-Is-Just-A-Behavior-Problem, The-I-Will-Never-Have-Real-Relationships, The-If-I-Could-Just-Try-Harder-To-Fit-In, and The-My-Differences-Mean-I-Cannot-Succeed with evidence examination adapted for concrete thinking, cognitive reframing, and behavioral experiments that reduce shame, reduce masking, build diagnosis acceptance, build connection hope, shift from masking to accommodation, and build strengths-based identity.
  4. Co-occurring condition treatment — Address anxiety (~40% of autistic adults) and depression (~30%) with CBT adapted for autistic cognitive styles. Include sensory regulation strategies, routine-building, and predictable structures that reduce anxiety.
  5. Masking reduction and burnout prevention — Address the masking burnout that drives much autistic distress. Build self-advocacy skills, reduce unnecessary masking, create sensory-friendly environments, and develop authentic social strategies that work with autistic social styles.
  6. Sensory regulation — Build skills for managing sensory overload: identifying sensory triggers, developing regulation strategies (stimming, sensory tools, environment modification), creating sensory-friendly spaces, and advocating for sensory accommodations.
  7. Strengths-based approach — Identify and leverage autistic strengths (pattern recognition, deep focus, detail orientation, special interest expertise, honesty, loyalty) rather than focusing exclusively on deficits. Build goals around the autistic person's values and strengths.

The evidence base supports adapted CBT for co-occurring conditions in adults with ASD (Spain et al., 2015; Weiss & Lunsky, 2011; Spek et al., 2013). The neurodiversity-affirming approach is supported by both research and the autistic community, and produces better outcomes than approaches that pathologize autistic traits.

The 6 Distortions — With Reframes

The-I-Am-Just-Broken: "I'm broken. Normal people don't struggle with this. Something is fundamentally wrong with me. I'm defective."
Reframe: "Neurological difference is not defectiveness. Autism is natural variation in human neurology, recognized by the neurodiversity framework. Different does not mean broken. The distress I experience comes from co-occurring conditions (anxiety, depression) and from environments that don't accommodate my needs, not from being autistic. Many autistic people live fulfilling lives with appropriate support and accommodation. Equating my neurology with brokenness drives shame, which worsens mental health and blocks the support that would help. I am a neurodivergent person with strengths and challenges, not a broken neurotypical person."
The-I-Should-Be-Able-To-Just-Be-Normal: "I should just be able to act normal. Everyone masks. Asking for accommodation is weakness. I just need to try harder to fit in."
Reframe: "Masking causes burnout, mental health decline, and loss of identity — it is not a sustainable or healthy long-term strategy. Accommodation is a legitimate need, not weakness (like glasses for vision or a wheelchair ramp for mobility). Neurotypical social norms are not universal or superior; autistic social styles are valid. Self-advocacy is strength. The goal is not to appear neurotypical but to thrive as autistic with appropriate support. Reducing masking and increasing accommodation improves mental health and functioning. I can live authentically and seek the support I need."
The-It-Is-Just-A-Behavior-Problem: "Autism isn't real. It's just bad behavior and poor social skills that need to be disciplined out. I just need to try harder."
Reframe: "Autism has robust neurobiological evidence: differences in brain connectivity, synaptic pruning, and neural development, documented across decades of research. It is recognized by the American Psychiatric Association, American Medical Association, NIMH, and every major medical organization. The behavioral framing misses the underlying neurology — autistic behaviors are expressions of neurological differences, not willful misbehavior. Invalidation of the diagnosis delays support and accommodation and worsens outcomes. Accepting the diagnosis is the first step to building the supports and strategies that actually work with my neurology."
The-I-Will-Never-Have-Real-Relationships: "I'll never have real friends or relationships. I'm too different. No one could accept me as I am."
Reframe: "Autistic people build meaningful relationships — different social styles are valid, not inferior. Many autistic people have deep, loyal friendships and partnerships, often built around shared interests rather than neurotypical social patterns. Shared-interest communities (special interest groups, online communities, neurodivergent spaces) are natural relationship-building environments. The all-or-nothing framing that I will never have relationships is a distortion that blocks the connection that is possible. Relationships may look different from neurotypical ones, but different does not mean less real or less meaningful."
The-If-I-Could-Just-Try-Harder-To-Fit-In: "If I just tried harder to fit in, I wouldn't have these problems. Masking more is the answer. Accommodation is giving up."
Reframe: "Masking more increases burnout, mental health decline, and loss of identity — it is the problem, not the solution. Accommodation reduces distress and improves functioning; it is intelligent, not giving up. Self-advocacy is effective and empowering. Working with my neurology (sensory regulation, routine, special interests, authentic social styles) is healthier than working against it (masking, forcing neurotypical patterns). The energy spent masking could be spent thriving. Building authentic engagement with appropriate support is more sustainable and fulfilling than exhausting myself to appear neurotypical."
The-My-Differences-Mean-I-Cannot-Succeed: "Being autistic means I can't succeed in work or life. My differences are all deficits. I'm permanently limited."
Reframe: "Autistic strengths include pattern recognition, deep focus, detail orientation, special interest expertise, honesty, loyalty, and innovative thinking. Many autistic adults succeed in careers that leverage these strengths, especially with appropriate accommodation. Differences include both strengths and challenges; the deficit-only framing that treats all differences as deficits is a distortion. With accommodation (sensory-friendly environments, clear communication, routine, flexibility), autistic people thrive in many fields. My neurology includes strengths I can leverage, and my challenges can be accommodated. I am not permanently limited; I am differently equipped."

Frequently Asked Questions

Is CBT evidence-based for adults with autism?

Yes. CBT is an evidence-based treatment for co-occurring conditions in adults with autism spectrum disorder (DSM-5 299.00), particularly anxiety (affecting ~40% of autistic adults) and depression (~30%). The evidence base includes Spain et al. (2015), who adapted CBT for autistic adults with co-occurring anxiety; Weiss & Lunsky (2011), who developed CBT for autistic adults with co-occurring mental health conditions; and Spek et al. (2013), who showed CBT effectiveness for anxiety and depression in adults with ASD. Key adaptations include using concrete language, incorporating special interests, accommodating sensory needs, using visual supports, and modifying the pace and structure of sessions. Neurodiversity-affirming CBT — which respects autistic identity and does not try to make the person less autistic — produces better outcomes and is supported by both research and the autistic community.

What is masking and why is it harmful?

Masking (also called camouflaging) is the practice of suppressing or hiding autistic traits to appear neurotypical. This includes: suppressing stimming, forcing eye contact, scripting social interactions, hiding sensory distress, mimicking neurotypical social behavior, and suppressing special interests. Masking is harmful because: (a) it causes severe burnout — the cognitive and emotional cost of constant suppression leads to exhaustion, meltdowns, and mental health decline; (b) it delays or prevents diagnosis — masked autistic people are often missed in childhood and diagnosed late, missing early support; (c) it increases mental health problems — masking is associated with higher rates of anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation; (d) it erodes identity — constant suppression of one's natural way of being leads to loss of self-knowledge and authenticity. CBT for autistic adults addresses masking by building self-advocacy, reducing unnecessary masking, developing authentic social strategies, and creating environments where masking is less necessary.

What are autistic strengths?

Autism involves differences that include both challenges and strengths. Well-documented autistic strengths include: pattern recognition (identifying patterns and connections that others miss), deep focus and concentration (sustained attention on areas of interest), detail orientation (noticing details that others overlook), special interest expertise (deep knowledge in focused areas), honesty and directness (valuing truth over social convenience), loyalty (deep commitment to people and values), innovative and divergent thinking (approaching problems from unique angles), strong sense of justice (commitment to fairness and ethical principles), reliability and consistency (following through on commitments), and visual and spatial strengths (thinking in pictures, strong spatial reasoning). The deficit-only framing that treats all autistic differences as deficits is a cognitive distortion. Effective support leverages autistic strengths while accommodating challenges, rather than focusing exclusively on deficits. Many fields (technology, research, arts, quality assurance, data analysis) benefit from autistic cognitive styles.
