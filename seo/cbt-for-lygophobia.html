Lygophobia is an intense irrational fear of darkness or the dark. It is a specific phobia (DSM-5 300.29). It frequently overlaps with nyctophobia (fear of the dark), scotophobia (fear of darkness), achluophobia (fear of darkness), and phasmophobia (fear of ghosts). It becomes clinically significant when it leads to avoidance, needing lights on at all times, or severe distress at nightfall. The avoidance is what CBT targets — and the key insight is that lygophobia is maintained by cognitive distortions plus avoidance reinforcement.

Cognitive Distortions → Reframes

The three most common distortion-reframe patterns:

Distortion (Dark-Catastrophizing): "If it is dark, something dangerous is hiding. The darkness means I cannot see threats, so I am vulnerable to attack. Being in the dark means something will hurt me."
Reframe: "The belief that darkness means danger is the catastrophizing distortion that lygophobia has created. The reality is that darkness is a natural state, the same room that is safe in light is safe in dark, and visibility does not determine safety. The evidence-based response is cognitive restructuring. I can reframe: darkness is neutral; my room is the same safe space; I am not more vulnerable in the dark."
Distortion (Hypervigilance / Scanning): "I must constantly listen for sounds and scan the shadows in the dark. If I stop being vigilant, something will catch me off guard. Every sound in the dark is a threat."
Reframe: "The belief that you must constantly scan the dark for threats is the hypervigilance distortion that lygophobia has created. The reality is that hypervigilance maintains anxiety, sounds at night are normal (house settling, wind, appliances), and constant scanning prevents rest. The evidence-based response is attentional retraining. I can reframe: sounds are normal; scanning maintains fear; I can rest without monitoring."
Distortion (Safety Behavior / Avoidance): "If I keep lights on at all times and avoid dark rooms, I avoid the danger that darkness brings. Needing light protects me from what the dark hides."
Reframe: "The belief that keeping lights on keeps you safe is the safety behavior and avoidance-reinforcement distortion that lygophobia has created. The reality is that light does not provide physical safety, avoidance reinforces fear, and chronic light dependence prevents restful sleep and limits freedom. The evidence-based response is graded exposure with relaxation. I can reframe: light does not equal safety; avoidance maintains fear; gradual dark exposure reduces anxiety over time."

Frequently Asked Questions

What is lygophobia and how common is it?

Lygophobia is an intense irrational fear of darkness or the dark. It is a specific phobia (DSM-5 300.29), closely related to nyctophobia (fear of the dark), scotophobia (fear of darkness), and achluophobia (fear of darkness). Specific phobias affect approximately 7-9% of the population. CBT is highly effective with 60-80% reduction in phobic anxiety.

How is lygophobia different from normal caution in the dark?

Lygophobia centers specifically on an irrational fear of darkness, not normal caution in low-light environments. The key insight is that lygophobia is maintained by catastrophizing (the dark hides danger), hypervigilance (scanning for threats), and avoidance reinforcement (needing lights on). CBT with cognitive restructuring and graded exposure directly targets these distortions.

What does CBT for lygophobia involve and does it work?

CBT for lygophobia involves cognitive restructuring, graded exposure, and relaxation. The core components: (1) cognitive restructuring challenging the catastrophizing and hypervigilance; (2) graded exposure (dim lights, short darkness, sit in dark with support, sleep without nightlight); (3) relaxation for calm. CBT is evidence-based with 60-80% reduction in phobic anxiety.
