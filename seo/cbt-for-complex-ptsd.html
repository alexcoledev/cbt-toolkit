6C-PTSD-specific tools

Complex PTSD (C-PTSD) results from repeated or prolonged trauma that is interpersonal and difficult or impossible to escape — childhood abuse, domestic violence, captivity, human trafficking, chronic neglect, prolonged bullying. C-PTSD includes the four PTSD symptom clusters (intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions, hyperarousal) PLUS three disturbances in self-organization: affective dysregulation (emotions feel dangerous and uncontrollable), negative self-concept (the belief that the self is fundamentally diminished or broken), and disturbed relationships (difficulty trusting and maintaining connections). The harm is in the cycle: the repeated trauma erodes the self-concept and relational capacity, the eroded self-concept produces the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken and the I-Deserved-It, the eroded relational capacity produces the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted, the affective dysregulation produces the My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous, the anticipatory hyperarousal produces the It-Will-Happen-Again, and the whole pattern reinforces the Trauma-Defines-Me. CBT is evidence-based for C-PTSD (phase-based treatment, CBT-IT, NICE-recommended for trauma). CBT works by identifying the cognitive distortions, examining the evidence (the trauma was real but the conclusion that the self is broken is the distortion), practicing affect-regulation skills, and rebuilding the self-concept and relational capacity that the prolonged trauma eroded. C-PTSD is distinct from PTSD (single-incident trauma not repeated/prolonged), depression (mood disorder not trauma-derived self-organization disturbance), anxiety (worry not trauma intrusions), general trauma, and all occupation/identity/transition pages. All tools run in your browser. No signup, no backend, no data sent anywhere. Privacy-first by design — which matters especially for C-PTSD, where the shame about the trauma and the distrust of others can make reaching for help feel dangerous.

Why C-PTSD Needs Specific CBT

General PTSD tools say "you had a trauma, let's process the intrusions." General depression tools say "you feel worthless, let's examine the worthlessness." General anxiety tools say "you worry, let's examine the worry." General trauma tools say "you were hurt, let's process the hurt." None of them address the specifics: the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken collapses the self into the repeated trauma in a way general depression tools cannot address because the brokenness is trauma-derived not mood-derived, the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted overgeneralizes the betrayal to all relationships in a way general trust-issue tools cannot address because the overgeneralization is from interpersonal trauma not from a single disappointment, the My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous catastrophizes the affect in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the emotion-fear is from affective dysregulation not from general worry, the Trauma-Defines-Me fuses the identity with the wound in a way general identity tools cannot address because the fusion is between the self and repeated abuse not between the self and a general role, the I-Deserved-It internalizes the blame for the perpetrator's actions in a way general self-criticism tools cannot address because the blame is for trauma not for a general mistake, and the It-Will-Happen-Again anticipates re-traumatization in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the anticipation is trauma-driven not worry-driven. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT for C-PTSD can target directly:

The "I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken" Distortion (Negative-Self-Concept / Personalization)

Distortion: "I am fundamentally broken. The trauma happened to me, and the happening is the one thing that means I am damaged, and the damaged is the one thing that means I am worthless, and the worthless is the one thing that means I cannot be whole, and the cannot-be-whole is the one thing that means the brokenness is permanent, and the permanent is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken. The I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken (I am damaged) was a negative-self-concept and a personalization: the mind treats the trauma as evidence of a flaw in the self, and because the trauma was repeated and interpersonal, the inference is that the self invited or deserved it. The negative-self-concept (I am diminished, damaged, broken) is the distortion: the trauma was real, the harm was real, but the conclusion that the self is broken is the internalization of the perpetrator's message — not an objective property of the self. The personalization (the trauma happened to me, therefore I am the cause) is the trap: the logic makes the victim the author of the violation, which means the violation was deserved, which means the self is worthless, which means the brokenness is permanent, which means the life shrinks to the wound. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the self is broken versus the evidence that the self was harmed, what would a neutral observer say about a person who was repeatedly hurt versus what they would say about the person's worth, what is the base rate of trauma survivors who rebuild full and valued lives), challenging the negative-self-concept (the trauma is real, but the conclusion that the self is broken is the distortion), and practicing the self-rebuilding — the identification of values, capacities, and relationships that exist alongside the wound, so the mind learns that the self is larger than the trauma. The trauma is real; the conclusion that the self is broken is the distortion.

The "No-One-Can-Be-Trusted" Distortion (Relational-Disturbance / Overgeneralization)

Distortion: "No one can be trusted. The person who was supposed to protect me harmed me, and the harming is the one thing that means all people harm, and the all-people-harm is the one thing that means connection is dangerous, and the dangerous is the one thing that means I must be alone, and the alone is the one thing that means the isolation is safety, and the isolation is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted. The No-One-Can-Be-Trusted (all people harm) was a relational-disturbance and an overgeneralization: the mind treats the betrayal by the perpetrator as evidence of all people's nature, and because the betrayal was by someone trusted, the inference is that trust itself is dangerous. The relational-disturbance (connection is dangerous) is the distortion: the perpetrator's betrayal is real, but the conclusion that all people betray is the overgeneralization from one person to all people — a survival mechanism that protected during the trauma but harms after. The overgeneralization (one person harmed me, therefore all people will harm me) is the trap: the logic makes the betrayal the rule, which means trust is impossible, which means connection is impossible, which means the isolation continues, which means the wound cannot heal. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that all people betray versus the evidence that some people are safe, what would a neutral observer say about the base rate of trustworthy people, what is the evidence from the person's own life of people who were kind), challenging the relational overgeneralization (the betrayal is real, but the conclusion that all people betray is the distortion), and practicing the relational rebuilding — the gradual identification of safe people and the gradual extension of trust, so the mind learns that connection is possible and the isolation was the trauma's legacy not the self's destiny. The betrayal is real; the conclusion that all people betray is the distortion.

The "My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous" Distortion (Affective-Dysregulation / Catastrophizing)

Distortion: "My emotions are dangerous. The feeling is the flood, and the flood is the one thing that means I will lose control, and the losing control is the one thing that means I will be destroyed, and the destroyed is the one thing that means I must suppress, and the suppress is the one thing that means the pressure builds, and the pressure is the trap."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous. The My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous (the feeling is the flood) was an affective-dysregulation and a catastrophizing: the mind treats the emotion as a force that will overwhelm, and because the emotion is intense (as it is in C-PTSD where the affective system was repeatedly activated), the inference is that the emotion will destroy. The affective-dysregulation (the emotion is uncontrollable) is the distortion: the emotion is intense, but the conclusion that it will destroy is the catastrophizing — the emotion is a signal, not a verdict, and the signal can be regulated. The catastrophizing (if I feel, I will lose control, and if I lose control, I will be destroyed) is the trap: the logic makes the feeling the flood, which means suppression is necessary, which means the emotion is never processed, which means the pressure builds, which means the eventual eruption confirms the fear. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the emotion will destroy versus the evidence that the emotion will peak and pass, what would a neutral observer say about the capacity to tolerate intense emotion, what is the base rate of people who feel intense emotion and are not destroyed), challenging the affective catastrophizing (the intensity is real, but the conclusion that the emotion will destroy is the distortion), and practicing the affect-regulation — the identification of the emotion as a signal, the grounding skills that tolerate the intensity, and the gradual exposure to the feeling, so the mind learns that the emotion passes and the self survives. The intensity is real; the conclusion that the emotion will destroy is the distortion.

If you are in crisis

C-PTSD, especially when the trauma intrusions are intense, the affective dysregulation is consuming, or the negative self-concept is producing thoughts of self-harm, can coexist with severe distress, despair, or suicidal ideation. The trauma is real; the conclusion that the self is broken is the distortion. If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, please reach out now:

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US): call or text 988
  • Samaritans (UK/ROI): call 116 123
  • NAMI HelpLine (US): 1-800-950-6264 or text "HelpLine" to 62640
  • ADAA (international directory): adaa.org/finding-help
  • ISSTD (International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation): isstd.org — hosts trauma-specific resources and provider directory
  • RAINN (sexual assault): 1-800-656-4673 or rainn.org — for trauma from sexual violence

Frequently Asked Questions

Is C-PTSD the same as PTSD?

No. PTSD results from a single-incident trauma (a car accident, a single assault, a natural disaster). C-PTSD results from repeated or prolonged trauma that is interpersonal and difficult to escape (childhood abuse, domestic violence, captivity, chronic neglect). PTSD has four symptom clusters: intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions, hyperarousal. C-PTSD has those four PLUS three disturbances in self-organization: affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbed relationships. CBT for PTSD targets the single-trauma intrusions; CBT for C-PTSD targets the single-trauma intrusions AND the self-organization disturbances — the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken, the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted, the My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous. Use the PTSD tools if the trauma was a single incident; use these tools if the trauma was repeated or prolonged and the self-concept and relationships are affected.

Is C-PTSD the same as depression?

No. Depression is a mood disorder with persistent low mood, anhedonia, and worthlessness — but the worthlessness is not trauma-derived and there are no trauma intrusions or hyperarousal. C-PTSD includes trauma intrusions (flashbacks, nightmares), hyperarousal (startle, hypervigilance), AND the three self-organization disturbances that come from repeated interpersonal trauma. The I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken in C-PTSD is specifically the internalization of the perpetrator's message — not a general depressive worthlessness. CBT for depression targets the mood; CBT for C-PTSD targets the trauma intrusions and the self-organization disturbances. Use the depression tools if the primary problem is mood; use these tools if the primary problem is repeated trauma and its effect on the self and relationships.

What is phase-based treatment for C-PTSD?

Phase-based treatment is the evidence-based model for C-PTSD. Phase 1 is stabilization: building safety, affect-regulation skills, and the therapeutic alliance. Phase 2 is trauma processing: identifying and processing the trauma memories and the cognitive distortions (the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken, the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted, the I-Deserved-It) using CBT techniques. Phase 3 is integration: rebuilding the self-concept, relational capacity, and life meaning that the prolonged trauma eroded. The cognitive tools on this page — identifying the I-Am-Fundamentally-Broken, the No-One-Can-Be-Trusted, the My-Emotions-Are-Dangerous — are the foundation that makes all three phases work: until the distortions are identified and examined, stabilization feels like suppression, processing feels like re-traumatization, and integration feels like pretending. Phase-based treatment is best done with a trauma-trained therapist, but the cognitive tools here are the starting point.
