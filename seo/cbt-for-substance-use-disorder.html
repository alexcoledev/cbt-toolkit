What are Substance Use Disorders?

Substance Use Disorders (DSM-5 substance-related and addictive disorders) are a cluster of conditions involving the recurrent use of one or more substances despite significant substance-related problems. The DSM-5 criteria include: impaired control (using more or longer than intended, persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down, great deal of time spent obtaining/using/recovering, craving), social impairment (failure to fulfill major role obligations, continued use despite social/interpersonal problems, reduction or abandonment of important activities), risky use (recurrent use in hazardous situations, continued use despite knowing it causes physical/psychological problems), and pharmacological criteria (tolerance, withdrawal). Severity specifiers: mild (2-3 criteria), moderate (4-5 criteria), severe (6+ criteria). The substances include alcohol, cannabis, hallucinogens (phencyclidine and others), inhalants, opioids (including heroin, fentanyl, prescription pain relievers), sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics, stimulants (including cocaine, amphetamine, methamphetamine), tobacco, and other/unknown substances. Gambling Disorder is the only recognized non-substance (behavioral) addiction in DSM-5, involving persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behavior. Key features include craving (intense desire or urge), tolerance (need for increased amounts for desired effect), withdrawal (negative symptoms when use is reduced or stopped), neurobiological basis (differences in reward circuitry, dopamine signaling, prefrontal cortex function), high co-occurrence (depression, anxiety, PTSD, ADHD, personality disorders), functional consequences (health problems, relationship damage, occupational impairment, legal problems, financial devastation), and response to treatment (CBT, medication-assisted treatment, mutual support groups, motivational interviewing). CBT for substance use disorders is evidence-based and targets the cognitive distortions that maintain addiction.

How CBT Works for Substance Use Disorders

CBT for substance use disorders follows an evidence-based approach that integrates cognitive restructuring, behavioral skills, and motivational enhancement:

  1. Motivational enhancement — Address ambivalence about change using motivational interviewing principles (Miller & Rollnick): express empathy, develop discrepancy between current behavior and values, roll with resistance, support self-efficacy. Meet the individual where they are in the stages of change (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance).
  2. Cognitive restructuring of the 6 distortions — Target the The-I-Can-Stop-Anytime, The-It-Is-Not-That-Bad, The-Everyone-Does-It, The-I-Will-Always-Need-This, The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything, and The-No-One-Can-Know with evidence examination, cognitive reframing, and behavioral experiments that build honest self-assessment, harm recognition, recovery hope, lapse-to-learning reframing, and support-seeking.
  3. Trigger and craving management — Identify high-risk situations (internal triggers like emotions, external triggers like people/places/things), develop coping skills for cravings (urge surfing, distraction, delay tactics, alternative activities), and build a personalized trigger management plan.
  4. Relapse prevention (Marlatt & Gordon) — Teach that lapses are normal, predictable, and learnable, not failures. Develop a relapse prevention plan that identifies warning signs, high-risk situations, coping responses, and support contacts. Reframe the abstinence violation effect (The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything) so a lapse becomes a learning opportunity, not a reason to abandon recovery.
  5. Coping skills training — Build alternative coping strategies for the emotions and situations that substance use was addressing: stress management, emotion regulation, problem-solving, communication skills, and pleasant activity scheduling. The goal is building a life where substances are less needed.
  6. Co-occurring condition treatment — Address co-occurring depression, anxiety, PTSD, ADHD, and personality disorders with CBT. Integrated treatment of both the substance use disorder and co-occurring conditions produces better outcomes than sequential or parallel treatment.
  7. Values-based recovery — Connect recovery to the individual's values and life goals. Build motivation around what recovery enables (relationships, health, work, meaning) rather than only what it avoids. Identify the gap between current behavior and valued direction, and build commitment to valued action.

The evidence base strongly supports CBT for substance use disorders. Carroll and Kiluk (2017) demonstrated CBT efficacy across multiple substance types. Magill et al. (2014) meta-analyzed CBT for alcohol and drug use disorders. Marlatt and Gordon's relapse prevention model is foundational. CBT is effective as standalone treatment and in combination with medication-assisted treatment (MAT), mutual support groups (AA, NA, SMART Recovery), and other therapeutic approaches.

The 6 Distortions — With Reframes

The-I-Can-Stop-Anytime: "I can stop anytime. I'm choosing to use. I just don't want to stop right now. If it were really a problem, I'd quit. I have it under control."
Reframe: "The evidence of impaired control is in my history: I've tried to cut down and couldn't, I've used more or longer than I intended, I've spent significant time obtaining and recovering from the substance, I experience craving, and I've developed tolerance and withdrawal. These are the DSM-5 criteria for impaired control, not signs of free choice. The The-I-Can-Stop-Anytime distortion overestimates my control by focusing on the intention to quit someday while ignoring the repeated unsuccessful attempts. Craving, tolerance, and withdrawal are neurobiological processes that override willpower — they are not character defects, they are the addiction operating. Honest control awareness — recognizing where my control is impaired — is the first step toward recovery, not a defeat. I can examine the evidence objectively: how many times have I tried to cut down, how many times have I used more than intended, how much of my life revolves around the substance. The answer is not a judgment of my worth; it is information about what I'm dealing with and what kind of help I need."
The-It-Is-Not-That-Bad: "It's not that bad. Other people have real problems. My use isn't affecting anyone. The consequences aren't really from the substance. I can handle it."
Reframe: "The harm from substance use is often invisible to the person using, precisely because the cognitive distortions minimize it. The objective evidence includes health impacts (liver, cardiovascular, neurological, overdose risk), relationship damage (broken trust, conflict, neglect), occupational impairment (missed work, poor performance, job loss), legal problems (DUI, possession, behaviors while intoxicated), and financial costs. The The-It-Is-Not-That-Bad distortion downplays these by comparing to imagined worse cases, attributing consequences to other causes, and focusing on the absence of catastrophic outcomes while ignoring accumulating harm. Family members, friends, and colleagues often see the harm before I do — their concerns are data, not attacks. Honest harm assessment means looking at the full picture: not just whether my life has collapsed, but whether the substance is costing me things I value, whether I'm living below my potential, whether I'm numbing emotions that need attention. The substance doesn't have to destroy my life to be a problem — it just has to be costing me more than I're gaining."
The-Everyone-Does-It: "Everyone drinks. Everyone uses. My use is normal compared to my friends. If they're fine, I'm fine. This is just what people do."
Reframe: "Normalizing my use by comparing to others is a cognitive distortion that blocks self-evaluation. The comparison is selective: I compare to the heaviest users in my social circle, not to population norms or to my own values. The DSM-5 criteria for substance use disorder are individual — they depend on my impaired control, my social impairment, my risky use, my pharmacological criteria — not on how my use compares to others. Two people can use the same amount and one has a disorder and the other does not, because the diagnosis is about the consequences and impaired control, not the quantity. The The-Everyone-Does-It distortion also ignores that my social circle may not be representative: if my friends also have substance use disorders, comparing to them normalizes the disorder, not health. Population data shows that most people use substances moderately or not at all, and that substance use disorders affect a minority — my comparison group is skewed. The healthier question is not 'am I worse than my friends?' but 'is my use causing problems in my life, and does it meet the criteria for a disorder?' That question has an answer I can find through honest self-assessment, not social comparison."
The-I-Will-Always-Need-This: "I'll always need this. I can't cope without it. Recovery is impossible for me. This is just who I am now. The dependence is permanent."
Reframe: "Dependence is not permanent — the brain changes that underlie addiction are reversible through neuroplasticity, and effective treatments exist. Medication-assisted treatment (MAT) for opioid use disorder (methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone) is evidence-based and life-saving. CBT builds the coping skills that replace the function the substance was serving. Millions of people have achieved sustained recovery from substance use disorders — recovery is not rare, it is the expected outcome of effective treatment. The The-I-Will-Always-Need-This distortion treats the current state as permanent, ignoring that the brain heals, that coping skills can be learned, that cravings diminish with time and abstinence, and that life without the substance becomes more rewarding as recovery builds. The hopelessness this distortion generates blocks engagement with the very treatments that would prove it wrong. I cannot know that recovery is impossible for me until I have genuinely engaged with evidence-based treatment — and the evidence says recovery is possible. The feeling of permanence is a symptom of the disorder, not a fact about my future."
The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything: "I slipped once, so I've failed. The recovery is over. I might as well go all the way now. All that progress is gone."
Reframe: "A single lapse (a one-time use) is NOT a full relapse (a return to previous patterns of use), and it does not erase progress. Marlatt's relapse prevention model teaches that lapses are normal, predictable, and learnable. The The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything distortion (the abstinence violation effect) is one of the most harmful cognitive distortions in recovery because it turns a manageable lapse into a full relapse: the thought 'I've already failed, so I might as well keep going' removes the motivation to stop, which transforms a one-time event into a return to regular use. The progress I made is not erased by a lapse — the coping skills I learned, the triggers I identified, the support I built are all still there. A lapse is information: what trigger was unanticipated, what coping skill was missing, what high-risk situation did I enter unprepared, what plan needs adjustment. The healthier response is to stop the lapse immediately, examine what happened without self-attack, adjust the recovery plan, and reach for support. Recovery is a process with ups and downs, not a binary state of perfect abstinence or total failure. The goal is preventing a lapse from becoming a relapse, and that requires dropping the all-or-nothing framing that says one slip means it's over."
The-No-One-Can-Know: "No one can know about this. I have to hide it. If people found out, they'd reject me. I'm alone in this. I have to deal with it by myself."
Reframe: "Addiction is a medical condition, not a moral failing — the American Medical Association, American Psychiatric Association, and Surgeon General all classify substance use disorders as treatable medical conditions with neurobiological bases. The stigma that treats addiction as a character defect is the distortion, not the condition. The The-No-One-Can-Know distortion hides the struggle due to anticipated stigma, drives isolation, and blocks the support that enables recovery. Isolation worsens substance use disorders — recovery is far more successful with social support, professional treatment, and mutual support groups (AA, NA, SMART Recovery). Disclosure, when safe and strategic, builds understanding and connection: people who understand addiction respond with compassion, not rejection. SAMHSA's National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential, and available 24/7 — the confidentiality means I can seek help without anyone knowing. The people worth having in my life will not reject me for having a medical condition. The shame that demands secrecy is a symptom of the disorder and of societal stigma, not a truth about my worth or my relationships. I can break the isolation by reaching for confidential help first, then building a support network as I strengthen recovery."

Frequently Asked Questions

Is CBT evidence-based for substance use disorders?

Yes. CBT is one of the most evidence-based treatments for substance use disorders (DSM-5 substance-related and addictive disorders), with efficacy demonstrated across alcohol, cannabis, opioid, stimulant, and tobacco use disorders, as well as gambling disorder. Carroll and Kiluk (2017) demonstrated CBT efficacy across multiple substance types. Magill et al. (2014) meta-analyzed CBT for alcohol and drug use disorders with positive results. Marlatt and Gordon's relapse prevention model is foundational and widely validated. CBT targets the cognitive distortions (The-I-Can-Stop-Anytime, The-It-Is-Not-That-Bad, The-Everyone-Does-It, The-I-Will-Always-Need-This, The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything, The-No-One-Can-Know) that maintain addiction, builds coping skills, manages cravings, and prevents relapse. CBT is effective as standalone treatment and in combination with medication-assisted treatment (MAT), mutual support groups, and motivational interviewing. Integrated treatment of co-occurring mental health conditions produces better outcomes.

What is the abstinence violation effect?

The abstinence violation effect is the The-One-Slip-Ruins-Everything cognitive distortion: the belief that a single lapse (one-time use) means total failure and justifies abandoning recovery. This is one of the most harmful distortions in substance use recovery because it transforms a manageable lapse into a full relapse. Marlatt's relapse prevention model directly addresses this by teaching that lapses are normal, predictable, and learnable events — not failures. The goal is to prevent a lapse from becoming a full relapse by examining the trigger, the high-risk situation, the coping response, and adjusting the recovery plan. The healthier response to a lapse is to stop immediately, examine what happened without self-attack, adjust the plan, and reach for support. Recovery is a process with ups and downs, not a binary state.

How does CBT relate to medication-assisted treatment (MAT)?

CBT and medication-assisted treatment (MAT) work best together and address different aspects of substance use disorders. MAT (methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone for opioids; naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram for alcohol) addresses the neurobiological aspects: reducing cravings, blocking euphoric effects, managing withdrawal, and stabilizing brain chemistry. CBT addresses the cognitive and behavioral aspects: restructuring the 6 distortions, building coping skills, managing triggers, preventing relapse, and treating co-occurring conditions. MAT makes it possible to engage in CBT by reducing the physiological drive that can overwhelm cognitive strategies, and CBT builds the skills and recovery framework that support long-term success. The combination of MAT plus CBT produces better outcomes than either alone. MAT is evidence-based and life-saving, particularly for opioid use disorder — the stigma against MAT is not supported by the evidence.
