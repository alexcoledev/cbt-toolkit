Free CBT tools built for the cognitive patterns that monophobia (fear of being alone) creates and maintains.

6 Monophobia tools · CBT evidence-based · 100% free · no signup

Monophobia (fear of being alone) — the intense irrational fear of solitude or being by oneself — produces distinctive cognitive distortions that general anxiety tools, separation anxiety tools, and abandonment tools each miss: the being-alone-means-i-am-abandoned where solitude is equated with permanent abandonment, the i-cannot-survive-by-myself overgeneralization where self-sufficiency is denied, the something-will-happen-and-no-one-will-help catastrophizing where emergencies are predicted with no rescue, the alone-means-unlovable labeling where solitude is equated with being unlovable, the i-must-always-have-company safety-behavior where company is always sought, and the avoiding-solitude-protects-me avoidance-reinforcement where solitude is never tolerated. These 6 free CBT tools are built around the cognitive distortions and behavioral patterns that monophobia actually creates — with examples, scenarios, and reframes that match the lived experience of a person who cannot bear an empty room, who calls someone the moment they are alone, who fills every hour with company, who believes solitude means being unloved, who has organized their life around never being by themselves.

Monophobia is uncommon but impactful, with high comorbidity with separation anxiety disorder, abandonment fear, agoraphobia, and PTSD. It becomes clinically significant when it is: (1) persistent and leads to avoidance of solitude, constant company-seeking, or inability to be home alone; (2) driven by a fear of being alone in general, not just separation from a specific person; (3) the person calls someone whenever alone, fills their schedule to avoid solitary time, or sleeps with company/TV/phone; (4) safety behaviors include constant texting, keeping someone on call, avoiding going home alone, or never traveling alone; (5) manifests as anticipatory anxiety before solitary periods, distress during solitude, and relief when company arrives. The driver is the fear of solitude and its meaning (abandonment, danger, unworthiness), the trigger is any situation where the person is or might be alone, and the content is the belief that being alone is dangerous and proves one is unloved. It is distinct from separation anxiety disorder because the focus is solitude in general, not separation from a specific attachment figure. It is distinct from agoraphobia because the fear is of being alone, not of being trapped. It is distinct from social anxiety because the person wants company, not social approval. The dependency on constant company is what CBT targets.

Cognitive Distortions → Reframes

The three most common distortion-reframe patterns in monophobia:

Distortion (Catastrophizing / Abandonment-Fear): "If I am alone, it means I have been abandoned. Being alone proves no one cares about me. If people cared, someone would be here. Solitude means I am unlovable and forgotten. I cannot survive by myself — I need someone present to be safe. If something happens, no one will help me. I must always have company or I am in danger. The silence is proof that I am unwanted."
Reframe: "The belief that being alone means you are abandoned and unlovable — that is the catastrophizing and abandonment-fear distortion that monophobia has created. The reality is that being alone is a normal human experience that everyone has, and it does not mean you are unlovable or forgotten: people choose solitude for rest, reflection, and independence. The 'if people cared, someone would be here' is the mind-reading that assumes others' absence means indifference, when people have their own lives, schedules, and needs. The 'I cannot survive by myself' is the overgeneralization that denies your coping abilities, when you have survived every moment of your life including alone ones. The 'alone means unlovable' is the labeling that equates a temporary state with a permanent identity trait. The evidence-based response is cognitive restructuring (separate solitude from abandonment) and graded exposure (practice being alone, learn it is tolerable). I can reframe: being alone is normal; solitude does not mean unlovable; people have their own lives; I can survive by myself; alone is a state, not an identity."
Distortion (Probability-Overestimation / Harm-While-Alone): "If I am alone, something terrible will happen. I will have a medical emergency and no one will find me. I will be attacked and no one will help. I will lose consciousness and die alone. The risk of being alone is unacceptable. I must have someone present at all times to call for help if needed. Being alone is dangerous and I cannot tolerate the risk."
Reframe: "The belief that being alone means something terrible will happen with no rescue — that is the probability-overestimation distortion that monophobia has created. The reality is that the vast majority of time spent alone is completely safe: millions of people live alone and are fine. The 'I will have a medical emergency' is the catastrophizing that overestimates the probability of sudden catastrophic events, when medical emergencies are rare and people living alone have phones, neighbors, and emergency services. The 'I will be attacked' is the probability-overestimation that conflates solitude with danger, when most violence is committed by acquaintances, not during solitude. The 'I will lose consciousness and die alone' is the catastrophizing that predicts the worst outcome from a low-probability event. The evidence-based response is cognitive restructuring (examine actual risk probabilities) and graded exposure (practice short periods alone, build confidence). I can reframe: alone is usually safe; emergencies are rare; I have a phone and emergency services; millions live alone safely; the risk is low and manageable."
Distortion (Safety-Behavior / Company-Seeking-Reinforcement): "I must always have someone with me. I should call someone whenever I am alone. I should avoid going home if no one is there. I should fill my schedule with social events so I am never solitary. I should sleep with the phone or TV on for company. I should ask friends to stay over. If I feel alone, I must reach out immediately. These precautions are necessary because being alone is unbearable."
Reframe: "The belief that you must always have company and avoid solitude at all costs — that is the safety-behavior distortion that monophobia has created. The reality is that constant company-seeking prevents disconfirmation of the fear and reinforces the belief that being alone is dangerous. The 'I should call someone whenever I am alone' is the safety-behavior that provides temporary relief but prevents learning that solitude is tolerable. The 'I should avoid going home if no one is there' is the avoidance that shrinks your life and maintains the phobia. The 'fill my schedule so I am never solitary' is the avoidance that prevents development of self-reliance skills. The 'sleep with the phone or TV on' is the safety-behavior that prevents learning to self-soothe. The evidence-based response is graded exposure (practice being alone for increasing periods, drop safety behaviors) and self-reliance skills training. I can reframe: company-seeking reinforces fear; avoidance prevents learning; I can develop self-reliance; solitude is tolerable; the goal is independence, not constant company."

Frequently Asked Questions

What is monophobia and how common is it?

Monophobia is an intense, irrational fear of being alone or solitary. The person fears solitude itself — not just separation from a specific person, but being alone in general. It is estimated to affect roughly 3-5% of the population, with higher rates among those with separation anxiety disorder, abandonment wounds, or trauma history. Many people experience mild discomfort when alone; monophobia is the severe form where the fear of solitude drives significant avoidance, constant company-seeking, and life restriction. It often begins in childhood or after an abandonment/loss event, and can persist into adulthood if untreated. The condition is maintained by safety behaviors (always having company, calling someone, filling the schedule) that prevent learning that being alone is tolerable. CBT breaks this cycle by targeting the cognitive distortions and safety behaviors that maintain it.

How is monophobia different from related conditions?

Monophobia is distinct from separation anxiety disorder because it is solitude-specific: the fear is of being alone in general, not of being separated from a particular attachment figure. It is distinct from autophobia (fear of oneself) because the fear is of solitude, not of the self. It is distinct from social anxiety disorder because the fear is of solitude, not of social evaluation — the person wants company, not approval. It is distinct from agoraphobia because the fear is of being alone, not of being in places where escape is difficult (though the two often co-occur). Monophobia often co-occurs with separation anxiety, abandonment fear, agoraphobia, and PTSD. The key insight is that the fear of being alone creates a dependency on constant company, which prevents development of self-reliance and reinforces the belief that solitude is dangerous.

What does CBT for Monophobia involve and does it work?

CBT for Monophobia involves cognitive restructuring, graded exposure, and self-reliance skills training. The core components: (1) cognitive restructuring challenging the being-alone-means-i-am-abandoned (catastrophizing: solitude equals abandonment), the i-cannot-survive-by-myself (overgeneralization: self-sufficiency is denied), the something-will-happen-and-no-one-will-help (probability-overestimation: emergencies are predicted), the alone-means-unlovable (labeling: solitude equals worthlessness), the i-must-always-have-company (safety-behavior: company prevents danger), and the avoiding-solitude-protects-me (avoidance-reinforcement: solitude is never tolerated); (2) graded exposure to being alone for increasing periods while dropping safety behaviors like calling someone, filling the schedule, or sleeping with the TV on; (3) self-reliance skills training including self-soothing, independent problem-solving, and building confidence in coping alone. CBT is evidence-based with significant reduction in fear and avoidance in controlled studies.
