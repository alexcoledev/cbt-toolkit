Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) is an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder characterized by prominent obsessions or compulsions that are the direct physiological consequence of substance intoxication or withdrawal, or medication use. The OCD-spectrum symptoms (obsessions, compulsions, body-focused repetitive behaviors, hoarding, ordering, checking) are caused by the pharmacological effects of a substance or medication — not by a primary mental disorder and not during delirium. Common substances of abuse that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms include cocaine and amphetamines/stimulants (intoxication produces obsessions, compulsions, and repetitive behaviors through dopaminergic and noradrenergic activation — checking, ordering, skin picking, repetitive grooming), caffeine (high-dose intoxication can produce intrusive thoughts and checking), cannabis (intoxication can produce obsessions and compulsions in vulnerable individuals), and alcohol (withdrawal can produce transient intrusive thoughts). Common medications that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms include corticosteroids (prednisone produces intrusive thoughts, obsessions, and compulsive behaviors), dopaminergic medications for Parkinson's disease (levodopa, dopamine agonists produce compulsive gambling, shopping, sexual behavior, and hoarding — dopamine dysregulation syndrome), stimulant medications for ADHD (methylphenidate, amphetamines can produce or exacerbate obsessions and compulsions), and antipsychotics (some produce akathisia and repetitive behaviors). Specifiers include with obsessive-compulsive symptoms, with compulsions, and with intrusive thoughts. The PRIMARY treatment is addressing the substance use or adjusting the medication (stopping cocaine, tapering the stimulant, switching medications, adjusting the dopaminergic dose), with CBT (including ERP for residual compulsions) as an adjunct for residual symptoms, adherence, and relapse prevention.

What is Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder?

Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) is an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder where the obsessions or compulsions are the direct physiological consequence of a substance (intoxication or withdrawal) or a medication. The DSM-5 criteria require: (a) prominent obsessions or compulsions; (b) there is evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the disturbance is the direct physiological consequence of a substance (intoxication or withdrawal) or a medication; (c) the disturbance is not better explained by a non-substance-induced OCD-spectrum disorder (e.g., OCD 300.3, Body Dysmorphic Disorder, Hoarding Disorder, where the symptoms are not attributable to a substance or medication); (d) the disturbance does not occur exclusively during delirium; (e) the disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. The critical feature is the causal substance/medication link: the obsessions/compulsions are not coincidental with substance use or medication use but are PRODUCED BY the substance or medication through a known pharmacological mechanism. For example, in cocaine intoxication, the drug blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake, producing massive dopaminergic activation that can manifest as obsessions, checking compulsions, and repetitive behaviors — the OCD-spectrum symptoms are the pharmacological consequence of the cocaine. In prednisone therapy, the synthetic glucocorticoid produces intrusive thoughts, obsessions, and compulsive behaviors through effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and limbic system — the OCD-spectrum symptoms are the pharmacological consequence of the medication. In dopaminergic medication for Parkinson's disease, the dopamine agonist produces compulsive gambling, shopping, or sexual behavior through dopamine dysregulation in the reward circuit — the compulsions are the pharmacological consequence of the medication. The symptoms must not occur exclusively during delirium and must cause clinically significant distress or impairment.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder

CBT for Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the substance-dismissal and substance-fatalism distortions that block identification of the causative agent and treatment engagement, the monocausal-reductionism and treatment-hopelessness distortions that dismiss CBT and block psychological treatment, the dichotomous-attribution and brokenness-fusion distortions that block integrated care and recovery identity. CBT with substance-psychological integration is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment that addresses residual obsessions/compulsions, adherence, and relapse prevention alongside substance use treatment or medication management.

1. Psychoeducation, Substance-OCD Link Education, Substance/Medication Management & Symptom Monitoring

The foundation of CBT for Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder is understanding the substance-OCD link, identifying the cognitive distortions that maintain the obsessions/compulsions and interfere with substance cessation or medication adherence, ensuring the causative substance or medication is being addressed, and learning symptom monitoring skills. Psychoeducation: learn the DSM-5 criteria (prominent obsessions/compulsions, direct physiological consequence of substance intoxication/withdrawal or medication, not better explained by a non-substance-induced OCD-spectrum disorder, not during delirium, clinically significant impairment), the common substances that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms (cocaine/amphetamine intoxication, caffeine intoxication, cannabis intoxication, alcohol withdrawal), the common medications that produce OCD-spectrum symptoms (corticosteroids, dopaminergic medications for Parkinson's, stimulant medications for ADHD, antipsychotics), the specifiers (with obsessive-compulsive symptoms, with compulsions, with intrusive thoughts), the treatment sequence (substance/medication addressed FIRST, CBT as adjunct), and the good prognosis with integrated care. Substance-OCD link education is the cornerstone: many people with substance/medication-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms have never understood that their obsessions and compulsions are the DIRECT pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication — the It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion frames the symptoms as primary OCD, the My-OCD-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological distortion splits OCD dichotomously, and the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion dismisses the psychological treatment that addresses residual symptoms. CBT helps the person understand the specific mechanism by which the substance or medication produces OCD-spectrum symptoms (e.g., cocaine blocks catecholamine reuptake producing dopaminergic activation that drives checking and ordering; prednisone affects the HPA axis and limbic system producing intrusive thoughts; dopamine agonists dysregulate the reward circuit producing compulsive gambling/shopping/sexual behavior), recognize the symptom pattern (correlation with substance use or medication status, pharmacological mechanism, response to addressing the causative agent), and pursue the substance use assessment or medication review that the symptoms warrant. Symptom monitoring: track obsessions, compulsions, substance use, medication adherence, and the temporal relationship between substance/medication changes and symptom changes — this data is the evidence base for the substance-OCD link and for treatment planning.

2. Cognitive Restructuring of Substance-Dismissal, Substance-Fatalism, Monocausal-Reductionism, Treatment-Hopelessness, Dichotomous-Attribution & Brokenness-Fusion Distortions + ERP for Residual Compulsions

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain the condition. Keep an OCD-substance belief log: each day, write down the distressing thoughts (it is just the substance, my OCD is permanent because my use is, stopping the substance is enough, the OCD will never improve, my OCD is all chemical or all psychological, I am broken at the root), identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the obsessions/compulsions are the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication requiring identification of the causative agent — the symptoms have a pharmacological mechanism, they correlate with substance use or medication status, and they require addressing the causative agent; CBT reduces residual OCD-spectrum symptoms even when substance use recovery is ongoing — the nervous system sensitization and learned OCD patterns respond to CBT even when the substance use recovery continues; residual obsessions/compulsions and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment — the symptoms that persist after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted, the distortions that interfere with substance cessation or medication adherence, and the relapse symptoms when substance use resumes all require CBT; CBT produces meaningful improvement in substance/medication-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms — cognitive restructuring reduces symptom amplification, ERP reduces residual compulsions, and adherence improves outcomes; obsessions/compulsions are produced by both pharmacological and psychological factors requiring integrated care — the substance or medication produces the pharmacological OCD-spectrum symptoms, the psychological interpretation amplifies them, and both must be addressed; the substance use is a medical condition and the medication need is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person — the substance use is treatable, the medication need is manageable, and the identity is separate from the substance use or medication need), and develop the reframe. Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) for residual compulsions that persist after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted: build an exposure hierarchy of the obsession triggers, practice exposure while refraining from the compulsion, and learn that the anxiety habituates and the feared outcome does not occur. Substance-psychological integration: recognize that the OCD-spectrum symptoms have both a pharmacological component (the substance or medication produces the symptoms) and a psychological component (the interpretation and behavioral response amplify and maintain them), and that integrated care addressing both produces better outcomes than either alone.

3. Behavioral Regulation, Substance/Medication Management, ERP Practice & Relapse Prevention

Behavioral regulation: establish regular sleep (sleep deprivation amplifies obsessions and worsens substance cravings), regular meals (irregular eating destabilizes blood sugar and triggers anxiety and cravings), regular activity (moderate exercise reduces sympathetic activation and supports recovery), and substance reduction (caffeine, alcohol, stimulants all interact with OCD-spectrum symptoms and substance use recovery). Substance/medication management: if the OCD-spectrum symptoms are substance-induced, engage substance use treatment (detoxification, medication-assisted treatment, counseling, support groups, relapse prevention planning); if the symptoms are medication-induced, work with the prescribing clinician to adjust the dose, switch to an alternative medication, or add an adjunctive anti-obsessional — never stop a prescribed medication without medical supervision. Medication adherence tracking (pill diary, medication reminder system, adherence barriers identified and addressed), and medical communication skills (tracking symptoms to report to the medical team, preparing questions for appointments, advocating for integrated care). ERP practice for residual compulsions: between-session exposure homework, SUDS tracking, response prevention practice, and habituation monitoring. Relapse prevention plan: a written plan that includes the early warning signs of OCD-spectrum symptoms returning (increased obsession intensity, increased compulsion frequency, increased substance craving, increased medication non-adherence, increased catastrophizing about symptoms), the action steps for each warning sign (return to CBT skills, contact clinician, resume ERP, adjust substance use treatment, review medication with prescriber), the triggers for relapse (substance use resumption, medication changes, stress, sleep disruption, major life transitions), and a treatment contact plan. The relapse prevention plan is written during treatment, reviewed regularly, and updated as recovery progresses — it is a living document that prepares the person for the predictable challenges of long-term recovery from substance/medication-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder

Each distortion maintains the condition by blocking identification of the causative agent, dismissing CBT, blocking integrated care, or preventing recovery identity. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. It-Is-Just-The-Substance (substance-dismissal / pharmacology-etiology-dismissal): "It is just my OCD. The substance is not the problem. The medication is not causing this. I have always had obsessions, the substance just makes them worse." The person dismisses the substance/medication etiology, framing the OCD-spectrum symptoms as primary and blocking the investigation that would identify the causative agent.
Reframe: "The obsessions/compulsions are the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication requiring identification of the causative agent. The It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion is a substance-dismissal: the person has dismissed the substance/medication etiology, treating the OCD-spectrum symptoms as purely psychological when they are the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication. But the symptoms in this disorder have a known pharmacological mechanism — cocaine produces obsessions and checking through catecholamine reuptake blockade and dopaminergic activation, prednisone produces intrusive thoughts through HPA axis and limbic system effects, dopamine agonists produce compulsive gambling through reward circuit dysregulation, stimulant medications produce compulsions through dopaminergic activation. The symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status (symptoms worsen during intoxication or withdrawal, improve when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted), they have a pharmacological mechanism (not just psychological), and they require addressing the causative agent (stopping the substance or adjusting the medication reduces the OCD-spectrum symptoms). The distortion is dangerous because it blocks the identification of the causative agent — the person attributes the symptoms to primary OCD and never identifies the cocaine, the prednisone, the dopamine agonist, or the stimulant medication that is producing the obsessions and compulsions. The antidote is substance-OCD link education: understand the specific mechanism by which substances and medications produce OCD-spectrum symptoms, recognize that the symptom pattern indicates substance/medication etiology, and pursue the substance use assessment or medication review that the symptoms warrant."
2. My-OCD-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is (substance-fatalism / chronicity-transfer): "My substance use is ongoing, so my OCD is ongoing. Nothing will help the obsessions because the use is permanent. I am stuck with these compulsions forever because the use is forever." The person fatalizes the OCD-spectrum symptoms as permanent by transferring the chronicity of the substance use to the symptoms, blocking CBT engagement.
Reframe: "CBT reduces residual OCD-spectrum symptoms even when substance use recovery is ongoing. The My-OCD-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is distortion is a substance-fatalism: the person has transferred the chronicity of the substance use to the OCD-spectrum symptoms, treating them as equally permanent. But even when the substance use recovery is ongoing (recovery is a process, not an event), the OCD-spectrum symptoms are NOT equally permanent — the symptoms have two components: (1) the direct pharmacological symptoms from the substance (which resolve as the substance is cleared and the nervous system normalizes), and (2) the residual symptoms from nervous system sensitization and learned OCD patterns (which are reduced by CBT and ERP). The residual symptoms respond to CBT even when the substance use recovery continues — cognitive restructuring reduces the catastrophizing that amplifies symptoms, ERP reduces residual compulsions, and exposure to recovery triggers reduces the OCD-spectrum symptoms about them. The distortion is harmful because it blocks CBT engagement — if the OCD is permanent, treatment is futile. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful OCD-spectrum symptom reduction in substance use recovery. The antidote is recognizing that the OCD-spectrum symptoms and the substance use are not equally chronic — the substance use recovery may be ongoing, but the symptom component is treatable."
3. Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough (monocausal-reductionism / psychological-treatment-dismissal): "I just need to stop using. Therapy is not for me. Stopping will solve everything. Once I stop the substance, the OCD will go away on its own." The person dismisses CBT as unnecessary, treating substance cessation as sufficient and blocking the psychological treatment that addresses residual symptoms and adherence.
Reframe: "Residual obsessions/compulsions and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment. The Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion is a monocausal-reductionism: the person has reduced the OCD-spectrum symptoms to a single cause (the substance) and dismissed the psychological treatment, treating substance cessation as sufficient. But three problems require CBT even after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted: (1) residual OCD-spectrum symptoms persist — the nervous system has been sensitized, the obsessions/compulsions have become learned responses, and the symptom interpretation patterns amplify residual symptoms; (2) adherence-interfering distortions — the It-Is-Just-The-Substance blocks identification of the causative agent, the I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root produces avoidance of treatment settings, and these distortions interfere with the ongoing substance use recovery or medication management; (3) relapse symptoms — when substance use resumes or the medication changes, the OCD-spectrum symptoms flare with it, and without CBT and ERP skills the person catastrophizes the flare, the symptoms amplify, and the recovery is slower. Integrated care (substance use treatment + CBT) outperforms substance-only care for OCD-spectrum symptoms — the CBT addresses the psychological factors that substance use treatment cannot reach. The antidote is recognizing that substance cessation is necessary but not sufficient — the OCD-spectrum symptoms have both pharmacological and psychological components, and both require treatment."
4. The-OCD-Will-Never-Improve (treatment-hopelessness / treatment-resistance-fatalism): "Nothing works for my OCD. I have tried everything. My case is different. The obsessions will never get better because they are caused by a substance." The person fatalizes the OCD-spectrum symptoms as treatment-resistant, using the substance etiology as evidence that psychological treatment cannot help.
Reframe: "CBT produces meaningful improvement in substance/medication-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms. The The-OCD-Will-Never-Improve distortion is a treatment-hopelessness: the person has fatalized the OCD-spectrum symptoms as treatment-resistant, using the substance etiology as evidence that CBT cannot help. But CBT produces meaningful improvement through four mechanisms: (1) cognitive restructuring reduces the symptom amplification that the catastrophizing produces — even when the substance causes the pharmacological symptoms, the person's interpretation of the symptoms amplifies and prolongs them, and restructuring reduces the amplification; (2) ERP reduces residual compulsions that persist after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted — exposure and response prevention breaks the compulsion cycle; (3) substance use recovery and medication adherence improve — the distortions that interfere with cessation or adherence are restructured, and better recovery/adherence reduces the pharmacological symptoms; (4) relapse prevention skills reduce the impact of substance use resumption or medication changes. The distortion is a self-fulfilling prophecy — if the OCD will never improve, the person does not engage with CBT, and the symptoms do not improve. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful OCD-spectrum symptom reduction in substance/medication-induced OCD. The antidote is recognizing that the substance etiology does not make the symptoms treatment-resistant — the psychological components respond to psychological treatment."
5. My-OCD-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological (dichotomous-attribution / pharmacological-psychological-split): "My OCD is either chemical or mental, not both. Either it is the substance or it is me. I need to figure out which is which and treat them separately." The person splits OCD dichotomously into all-chemical or all-psychological, blocking the integrated care that addresses both.
Reframe: "Obsessions/compulsions are produced by both pharmacological and psychological factors requiring integrated care. The My-OCD-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological distortion is a dichotomous-attribution: the person has split OCD-spectrum symptoms into mutually exclusive categories (chemical OR psychological), blocking the recognition that the symptoms are produced by BOTH. But the OCD-spectrum symptoms in Substance/Medication-Induced OCD are inherently both — the substance or medication produces the pharmacological symptoms (obsessions, compulsions, repetitive behaviors), AND the psychological interpretation amplifies them (catastrophizing about the obsessions increases their severity and duration). The obsession is chemical (the cocaine produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation 'this obsession means I am dangerous' amplifies the anxiety about it). The compulsion is chemical (the dopaminergic medication produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation 'I must perform this compulsion' amplifies the drive). The dichotomous split is harmful because it blocks integrated care — if OCD is all-chemical, only the substance use treatment or medication adjustment addresses it; if all-psychological, only the CBT clinician addresses it. But the symptoms require BOTH — the substance use treatment or medication adjustment addresses the pharmacological symptoms, and the CBT clinician addresses the psychological interpretation that amplifies them. Integrated care outperforms either alone. The antidote is recognizing that OCD-spectrum symptoms are produced by both factors simultaneously."
6. I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root (brokenness-fusion / substance-use-as-identity): "I am defective. I am an addict. I am broken at the root. The substance use means I am a broken person, not a person with a substance use problem." The person fuses the substance use or medication need with personal brokenness, treating a medical condition as evidence of fundamental defectiveness.
Reframe: "The substance use is a medical condition and the medication need is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person. The I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root distortion is a brokenness-fusion: the person has fused the substance use or medication need with their identity, treating the condition as evidence of fundamental brokenness rather than a medical condition that happened to a person. But substance use is a medical condition — addiction is a brain disorder involving neuroadaptation, not a character defect; medication-induced OCD-spectrum symptoms are a pharmacological side effect, not a personal failing; the need for medication is a medical event, not a brokenness. The person is a person who has a substance use condition or who needs a medication, not a broken person. The condition is treatable (substance use treatment manages the condition, CBT manages the OCD-spectrum symptoms, medication adjustment manages the side effect), the person is not defective (having a substance use condition or needing a medication is a human experience, not a defect), and the identity is separate from the condition. The distortion is harmful because it blocks recovery — if the person is broken at the root, treatment is futile, treatment settings are avoided, and the identity collapses into the condition. But the evidence shows that people with substance use conditions and medication needs recover, manage, and live full lives — the condition is a medical event, not an identity. The antidote is recognizing that the condition is a medical event that happened to a person, that the person is not the condition, and that treatment addresses the condition while the person remains a whole person."

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder requires coordinated care from both a substance use specialist or prescribing clinician (for the substance use or medication management) and a CBT-trained clinician (for the OCD-spectrum symptoms), especially when the obsessions/compulsions are severe or the substance use is complex. Seek help if: the OCD-spectrum symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status (worsen during intoxication or withdrawal, improve when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted — this is the hallmark of substance/medication etiology); you are using a substance (cocaine, amphetamines, caffeine, cannabis, alcohol) and the substance use is producing obsessions or compulsions that cause significant distress or impairment; you are taking a medication (corticosteroid, dopaminergic medication, stimulant medication, antipsychotic) and the medication is producing OCD-spectrum symptoms — do not stop the medication without consulting the prescribing clinician; you have OCD-spectrum symptoms and have NOT had a substance use assessment or medication review (the It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion may be blocking the investigation that would identify a causative agent); the obsessions/compulsions are producing significant distress or interfering with daily functioning; the cognitive distortions are interfering with substance cessation or medication adherence; you have stopped the substance or adjusted the medication but residual OCD-spectrum symptoms persist (the residual symptoms require CBT and ERP — the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion may be blocking the psychological treatment); the OCD-spectrum symptoms are producing secondary problems (depression, relationship strain, occupational impairment); or you have thoughts of self-harm or suicide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is Substance/Medication-Induced OCD different from primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder?

Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) and primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) both produce prominent obsessions and compulsions, but the etiology differs fundamentally. In Substance/Medication-Induced OCD, the obsessions/compulsions are the DIRECT PHARMACOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCE of a substance (intoxication or withdrawal) or a medication — the substance (e.g., cocaine) is PRODUCING the OCD-spectrum symptoms through a known pharmacological mechanism (catecholamine reuptake blockade producing dopaminergic activation that drives checking and ordering). The symptoms are a pharmacological consequence of the substance use. In primary OCD, the obsessions and compulsions are not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance or medication. The treatments differ: Substance/Medication-Induced OCD requires addressing the substance use or adjusting the medication first with CBT (including ERP) as adjunct; primary OCD is treated primarily with CBT with ERP and/or SSRIs without substance cessation as the primary intervention.

What substances and medications most commonly cause OCD-spectrum symptoms?

The substances and medications that most commonly produce obsessions or compulsions (Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder, DSM-5 292.9) fall into two categories. Substances of abuse: cocaine and amphetamines/stimulants (intoxication produces obsessions, compulsions, and repetitive behaviors through dopaminergic and noradrenergic activation — checking, ordering, skin picking, repetitive grooming), caffeine (high-dose intoxication can produce intrusive thoughts and checking behaviors), cannabis (intoxication can produce obsessions and compulsions, especially in vulnerable individuals or high-THC strains), alcohol (withdrawal can produce transient intrusive thoughts and compulsive checking). Medications: corticosteroids (prednisone, dexamethasone produce intrusive thoughts, obsessions, and compulsive behaviors — 'steroid psychosis' can include OCD-spectrum symptoms), dopaminergic medications for Parkinson's disease (levodopa, dopamine agonists produce compulsive behaviors including compulsive gambling, shopping, sexual behavior, and hoarding — dopamine dysregulation syndrome), stimulant medications for ADHD (methylphenidate, amphetamines can produce or exacerbate obsessions and compulsions, especially at high doses or in vulnerable individuals), antipsychotics (some produce akathisia and repetitive behaviors that can be mistaken for compulsions), anticholinergics, and thyroid preparations (excessive dose can produce anxiety with intrusive thoughts). The key diagnostic feature is that the OCD-spectrum symptoms correlate with the substance use or medication and have a known pharmacological mechanism.

If the OCD-spectrum symptoms are caused by a substance or medication, do I still need CBT?

Yes, CBT is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment for Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder, even though the substance or medication is the primary cause. Three reasons. First, residual OCD-spectrum symptoms often PERSIST after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted — the nervous system has been sensitized, the obsessions/compulsions have become learned responses, and the symptom interpretation patterns amplify residual symptoms. CBT and ERP extinguish the residual through cognitive restructuring and exposure. Second, the cognitive distortions interfere with SUBSTANCE CESSATION OR MEDICATION ADHERENCE — the person does not engage with substance use treatment, does not take medication consistently, does not attend follow-up appointments. CBT corrects these distortions, improving cessation/adherence and outcomes. Third, substance use RESUMES and medications CHANGE, and the OCD-spectrum symptoms flare with each change. CBT provides relapse prevention skills that reduce the symptom duration and intensity of each flare. Integrated care (substance use treatment/medication management + CBT) outperforms substance-only or medication-only care for OCD-spectrum symptoms.

Can I have both Substance/Medication-Induced OCD AND primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder?

Yes. A person can have both Substance/Medication-Induced Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) AND primary Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3). For example, a person with cocaine use may have OCD-spectrum symptoms directly caused by the cocaine intoxication (the 292.9 diagnosis) AND additional obsessions and compulsions that are not attributable to the cocaine (the primary OCD diagnosis). The DSM-5 allows both diagnoses when each set of symptoms meets full criteria and is not better explained by the other. The treatment addresses both: the substance use is treated (reducing the pharmacologically-caused symptoms), the primary OCD is treated with CBT with ERP and/or SSRIs (reducing the primary OCD), and the integrated plan coordinates the substance use treatment and psychological treatment.

What if I need the medication that is causing my OCD-spectrum symptoms?

If a prescribed medication (e.g., dopaminergic medication for Parkinson's disease, corticosteroid for an autoimmune condition, stimulant medication for ADHD) is producing OCD-spectrum symptoms, you face a treatment dilemma: the medication is medically necessary but the obsessions/compulsions are intolerable. Do not stop the medication on your own — work with your prescribing clinician to find the best balance. Options include: (1) dose adjustment — the lowest effective dose may produce fewer OCD-spectrum side effects; (2) alternative medication — a different medication in the same class may produce fewer symptoms; (3) adjunctive anti-obsessional — a medication to counteract the symptoms (e.g., an SRI for residual compulsions); (4) timing adjustment; (5) CBT and ERP for the residual symptoms — even with optimal medication management, some obsessions/compulsions may persist, and CBT addresses the residual through cognitive restructuring, ERP, and relapse prevention. The My-OCD-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is distortion may tell you the symptoms are permanent because the medication is necessary, but the symptoms are treatable even when the medication is ongoing — CBT reduces the residual symptom component.
