Managing On-Call Panic: Overcoming Catastrophizing in Production Outages

Being on-call can be incredibly stressful, especially when an alert triggers immediate panic and thoughts of worst-case cascade failures. This tendency to catastrophize can make a challenging situation feel unbearable, clouding your judgment. CBT provides tools to ground yourself and think clearly during an incident.

The Cognitive Distortions Behind This

CBT names the specific thinking patterns that fuel this anxiety. Here are the top 3 you are likely using:

Catastrophizing — This small error will definitely bring down the entire system, customers will churn, and I'll be fired immediately.
Fortune Telling — Even if I fix this, another, worse outage is just around the corner, and I won't be able to handle it.
Emotional Reasoning — I feel completely overwhelmed by this alert, so it must mean the outage is truly catastrophic and beyond my control.

The 7-Step Thought Record

Walk through these 7 steps. It takes 5 minutes. The magic is in steps 4 and 5 — forcing yourself to find evidence against the anxious thought.

1. Situation
It's 2 AM, and my pager goes off with an alert about increased latency on a critical API endpoint. My heart immediately starts pounding.
2. Emotions
I feel intense anxiety (10/10), panic (9/10), and a sense of dread (8/10).
3. Automatic Thoughts
This is it, the big one. The whole system is going to crash. I won't be able to fix it, and it's all my fault. We'll lose millions, and I'll be blamed.
4. Evidence For
The alert is for a critical service. High latency can indicate deeper problems. I've seen major outages before where small issues cascaded.
5. Evidence Against
Most alerts are minor issues that are quickly resolved. We have robust monitoring and rollback procedures. I've handled alerts successfully in the past. I'm part of a team, and I can escalate if needed. The alert is 'increased latency,' not 'system down.'
6. Alternative/Balanced Thought
An alert is a signal to investigate, not a confirmed disaster. While challenging, I have the skills and resources to diagnose and resolve issues, or escalate if necessary. I'll focus on the immediate next steps.
7. Outcome Re-evaluation
My anxiety has dropped to 6/10. I still feel the urgency, but I can now focus on checking logs and runbooks instead of spiraling into worst-case scenarios.

The Balanced Thought

After completing the thought record, your balanced reframe might look like:

An alert signals an issue that needs investigation, not necessarily a catastrophic failure. I have processes, tools, and a team to help diagnose and resolve it. My focus should be on the immediate, actionable steps.

Why This Works

This is not "just think positive." A thought record forces balanced evidence gathering. Your brain naturally collects evidence that confirms your fears (confirmation bias). Writing down evidence against the thought does not lie to you — it surfaces the evidence you were filtering out. That is the intervention.
