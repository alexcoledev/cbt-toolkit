What is Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.83) is a Depressive Disorder characterized by a prominent mood disturbance that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition. The mood disturbance may involve depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure, and must cause significant distress or impairment. The diagnosis requires evidence that the disturbance is the direct physiological consequence of the medical condition — not merely a psychological reaction to being ill (which would be an adjustment disorder), and not a primary mental disorder (which would be MDD). Common medical causes and their approximate depression prevalence: hypothyroidism (depression in ~15-30%, due to slowed metabolism affecting neurotransmitter synthesis), Parkinson's disease (~25-40%, due to degeneration of serotonergic and noradrenergic neurons alongside dopaminergic), multiple sclerosis (~20-50%, due to demyelination affecting mood-regulating circuits), stroke (~30-50%, especially left frontal and left basal ganglia strokes), traumatic brain injury (~25-50%, depending on injury location and severity), cancer (~20-40%, especially pancreatic, lung, and brain cancers), chronic pain (variable, often 30-50%), autoimmune disorders like lupus (~15-40%, due to inflammation and cytokine effects), HIV/AIDS (~20-40%, due to direct CNS effects and psychosocial factors), epilepsy (~20-30%, especially temporal lobe), vitamin B12 deficiency (variable, due to impaired neurotransmitter synthesis), Cushing's syndrome (~50-70%, due to hypercortisolism), and anemia (variable, due to reduced oxygen delivery to the brain). The key distinction from MDD is the presence of a clear medical etiology — in Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition, treating the underlying medical condition is essential and can resolve or reduce the depression; from adjustment disorder is that the depression is physiologically caused, not a psychological reaction; from substance/medication-induced depressive disorder is that a medical condition (not a substance) is the cause. CBT is evidence-based, adapted for the medical context — CBT reduces the cognitive distortions that maintain the depression, rebuilds behavioral engagement that respects medical limitations, and reconstructs an identity that includes but is not defined by the condition, in coordination with the medical team treating the underlying condition.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

CBT for Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.83) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the cognitive distortions that deepen the depression, the behavioral withdrawal that the condition and depression co-produce, and the identity fusion and care-fragmentation that block integrated recovery.

1. Cognitive Restructuring

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain the depression. Keep a medical-depression belief log: each day, write down the distressing thoughts (my medical condition defines me, the depression is just the illness, nothing can help both, my body betrayed me, it is not real depression, everything is about the condition), identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the condition is one aspect of a larger self, the depression is real and treatable even when medically caused, integrated care is the evidence-based standard, the body is the site of both the condition and its care and is not the enemy, medically-caused depression deserves mental health treatment, life exists alongside the illness), and generate a reframe. Use Socratic questioning: Was I only my diagnosis before the diagnosis? Do I have interests, relationships, values, and capacities that are not the condition? If the depression is medically caused, does that make the suffering less real? Does treating the medical condition automatically treat the depression, or do both need attention? Is my body the enemy, or is my body the organism that is both experiencing the condition and capable of receiving care? Is there anything in my life that is not about the condition — a relationship, a value, an interest, a memory? Psychoeducation about the mechanism (the medical condition directly alters brain function through inflammation, neurotransmitter disruption, structural damage, or hormonal effects — the depression is a real physiological consequence, not a character failure or a purely psychological reaction), the integrated care standard (treating the medical condition AND the depression together produces better outcomes than either alone), and the effectiveness of CBT for medically-caused depression — the goal is to replace identity fusion, invalidation, and care-fragmentation with accurate understanding and integrated engagement.

2. Medical-Integrated Behavioral Activation

Rebuild the behavioral engagement that the medical condition and the depression have co-eroded. Both the condition and the depression produce withdrawal — the condition limits what the body can do, the depression limits what the person wants to do, and the two reinforce each other in a self-perpetuating cycle. Behavioral activation adapted for the medical context breaks the cycle. Activity monitoring with medical context: for one week, track every activity and rate it for pleasure (0-10), mastery/accomplishment (0-10), and energy cost (0-10) — this reveals which activities still produce positive affect without exceeding current energy limits. Activity scheduling within medical constraints: systematically reintroduce activities that previously produced pleasure or mastery, adapted for current medical limitations — do not wait for the condition to resolve or motivation to return before acting. Graded task assignment respecting energy: break overwhelming tasks into small, achievable steps that respect current energy and medical limitations — the spoon theory framework (limited daily energy units) helps pace engagement without the boom-bust cycle that worsens both conditions. Values clarification across health states: identify what matters to the person (connection, contribution, creativity, growth) and find values-consistent activities that are accessible within current medical constraints — values are stable even when health and mood are not. Contrast scheduling: compare activities that worsen mood or symptoms (isolation, rumination, overexertion, substance use) with activities that improve mood within constraints (adapted social contact, paced physical activity, meaningful engagement) and shift the balance. The behavioral component is the engine of recovery — the cognitive restructuring reduces the distortions, but behavioral activation rebuilds the life the condition and depression have narrowed.

3. Identity Reconstruction & Integrated Care

Develop self-as-context: the My-Medical-Condition-Defines-Me distortion fuses the diagnosis with the self. CBT rebuilds a stable self-as-context: "I am a person who has a medical condition; I am not the condition. The condition is something I have, not something I am." Values-based identity stable across health states: identify values that are stable across health and illness (kindness, integrity, curiosity, connection, contribution, courage) and act on them regardless of current health status — the values are who the person is; the health and mood are what the person is experiencing. Pre-condition memory: recall and document who the person was before the condition — interests, relationships, capacities, personality traits — the condition amnesia (the sense that one has always been defined by the illness) is a symptom, not a truth. Integrated care plan: develop a written plan that coordinates medical treatment (treating the underlying condition with the medical team) and mental health treatment (CBT for the depression, possibly medication) — the Nothing-Can-Help-Both distortion fragments care; the antidote is a plan that treats both together, with each clinician aware of the other's role. Body reconciliation: the My-Body-Betrayed-Me distortion alienates the person from their body. CBT rebuilds a working relationship with the body: "My body is experiencing a condition, and my body is also the organism that receives treatment, rests, and heals. The body is not the enemy; it is the site of both the condition and its care." Comorbid treatment: if there is anxiety, grief, PTSD, or an adjustment reaction to the diagnosis (common comorbidities), treat the comorbid condition alongside the depression — comorbidity is the rule, not the exception, in medically-caused depression. The identity and integrated care component is where the cognitive and behavioral gains are sustained — a stable self, a reconciled body, and an integrated care plan are the foundation of long-term recovery alongside the medical condition.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

Each distortion maintains the depression. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. My-Medical-Condition-Defines-Me (identity-fusion / self-as-disease): "I am my diagnosis, this condition is who I am now, everything else about me is gone." The person fuses the medical condition with the self, blocking identity reconstruction and engagement with non-illness life.
Reframe: "I am a person who has a medical condition. The condition is one aspect of my life, not the whole of who I am. I had interests, relationships, values, and capacities before the diagnosis, and I still have them — the condition does not erase the self. The My-Medical-Condition-Defines-Me is a symptom that fuses the condition with the self; the self is larger than any one condition."
2. The-Depression-Is-Just-The-Illness (causal-dismissal / over-attribution-to-medical): "The depression is just a symptom of my illness, it's not real depression, it doesn't need its own treatment, just treat the illness." The person dismisses the depression as merely a medical symptom, blocking the mental health treatment that would reduce suffering.
Reframe: "The depression is real even though it is medically caused. It produces real suffering, real cognitive distortions, real behavioral withdrawal, and real impairment. CBT is effective for medically-caused depression — it reduces the distortions, rebuilds engagement, and reconstructs identity. Treating the medical condition is necessary but may not be sufficient; the depression deserves its own evidence-based treatment. The The-Depression-Is-Just-The-Illness blocks the help that works."
3. Nothing-Can-Help-Both (treatment-fatalism / either-or): "Treating the medical condition won't help the depression, treating the depression won't help the medical condition, I have to choose, they are separate competing priorities." The person blocks integrated care with a false either-or, fragmenting treatment.
Reframe: "Integrated care — treating the medical condition AND the depression together — is the evidence-based standard and produces better outcomes than either alone. The two are not competing priorities; they are complementary. Treating the depression improves medical outcomes (better adherence, better self-care, better quality of life); treating the medical condition can reduce the depression. The Nothing-Can-Help-Both is a distortion that fragments care; the antidote is a coordinated plan with both the medical team and a CBT-trained clinician."
4. My-Body-Betrayed-Me (body-as-enemy / somatic-alienation): "My body has turned against me, my body is the enemy, I can't trust anything about my physical self." The person alienates themselves from their own body, blocking medical engagement, self-care, and body-based treatment.
Reframe: "My body is experiencing a condition, and my body is also the organism that receives treatment, rests, and heals. The body is not the enemy; it is the site of both the condition and its care. Alienating from the body blocks the self-care (rest, nutrition, paced activity, medication adherence) that improves both the condition and the depression. The My-Body-Betrayed-Me is a distortion; the antidote is a working relationship with the body as the organism that is both suffering and capable of care."
5. It-Is-Not-Real-Depression (invalidation / medical-vs-mental hierarchy): "Because it's caused by my medical condition, it's not real depression, it doesn't count, I shouldn't be getting mental health treatment, real depression is only primary." The person invalidates medically-caused depression through a false hierarchy, blocking CBT.
Reframe: "Medically-caused depression is real depression. The DSM-5 recognizes Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (293.83) as a valid diagnosis. The suffering is real, the cognitive distortions are real, the behavioral withdrawal is real, and the response to CBT is real. There is no hierarchy where primary depression counts and medically-caused depression does not. The It-Is-Not-Real-Depression is a distortion that blocks evidence-based treatment; the antidote is recognizing medically-caused depression as real and deserving of mental health care."
6. Everything-Is-About-The-Condition (condition-centrality / overgeneralization): "Everything in my life is now about the condition, nothing exists outside of it, there is no life apart from the illness." The person overgeneralizes the condition to the whole of life, blocking engagement with non-illness life and values.
Reframe: "The condition is one part of life, not the whole of life. Relationships, values, interests, memories, and meaning exist alongside the condition — some may be adapted, but they are not erased. The Everything-Is-About-The-Condition is a distortion that narrows life to the illness; the antidote is actively engaging with non-illness life — maintaining relationships, pursuing accessible interests, acting on values, and building a life that includes the condition but is not reduced to it."

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Seek help from a CBT-trained clinician in coordination with your medical team if: the depressive symptoms are severe enough to disrupt self-care, relationships, or medical adherence; there are thoughts of self-harm or suicide (seek immediate help — call a crisis line or go to an emergency department); the depression has persisted and is not improving with medical treatment alone; the depression is interfering with treatment of the underlying medical condition (non-adherence, missed appointments, self-care failure); you have been told the depression is just a symptom and does not need its own treatment (this is the The-Depression-Is-Just-The-Illness distortion — medically-caused depression responds to CBT); you feel your body is the enemy and cannot engage with medical care (this is the My-Body-Betrayed-Me distortion — body reconciliation is part of treatment); there is a comorbid condition (anxiety, grief, PTSD, adjustment reaction to the diagnosis) that is also present; or integrated care (medical + mental health) has not been tried. A psychiatrist can evaluate medication (SSRIs are often appropriate alongside medical treatment); a CBT-trained clinician can provide the cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, and identity reconstruction adapted for the medical context. Medically-caused depression is real, treatable, and deserving of its own care alongside the medical condition.

Frequently Asked Questions

What medical conditions can cause Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition?

Many medical conditions can directly cause depression through pathophysiological mechanisms. Common causes include: endocrine disorders — hypothyroidism (slowed metabolism affects neurotransmitter synthesis), Cushing's syndrome (hypercortisolism, ~50-70% develop depression), Addison's disease; neurological disorders — Parkinson's disease (~25-40%, degeneration of serotonergic and noradrenergic neurons), multiple sclerosis (~20-50%, demyelination of mood circuits), epilepsy (~20-30%, especially temporal lobe), stroke (~30-50%, especially left frontal and left basal ganglia), traumatic brain injury (~25-50%); cancer — especially pancreatic, lung, and brain cancers (~20-40%, through cytokine effects and direct CNS involvement); autoimmune/inflammatory — systemic lupus erythematosus (~15-40%), HIV/AIDS (~20-40%, direct CNS effects); nutritional — vitamin B12 deficiency, anemia (reduced oxygen delivery); chronic pain — through sustained stress response and reduced quality of life. The key diagnostic requirement is evidence that the mood disturbance is the direct physiological consequence of the medical condition, not just a psychological reaction to being ill. Each condition produces depression through different mechanisms, and treatment always includes treating the underlying medical condition alongside CBT for the depression.

How is Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition different from Major Depressive Disorder or adjustment disorder?

Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (DSM-5 293.83), Major Depressive Disorder (MDD, DSM-5 296.x), and adjustment disorder (DSM-5) are distinct diagnoses. Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition: the mood disturbance is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition (e.g., hypothyroidism, Parkinson's, MS, stroke), there is a clear medical etiology, treating the medical condition is essential. MDD: the depression is a primary mental disorder with no single identifiable medical cause, though it involves neurobiological dysregulation; the depression is the primary target. Adjustment disorder: a psychological reaction to a stressor (which could include a medical diagnosis), the depression is a response to the stress of being ill rather than a direct physiological consequence. The key distinctions: (1) etiology — medical condition directly causes the mood disturbance (293.83) vs primary mental disorder (MDD) vs psychological reaction to stressor (adjustment); (2) treatment focus — treat the medical condition AND the depression (293.83) vs treat the depression as primary (MDD) vs treat the adjustment reaction (adjustment disorder). The cognitive distortions differ: 293.83 produces My-Medical-Condition-Defines-Me and It-Is-Not-Real-Depression; MDD produces Nothing-Will-Ever-Change and I-Am-My-Depression; adjustment disorder produces This-Should-Not-Have-Happened and I-Cannot-Adapt. All respond to CBT, but the medical form requires coordination with the medical team and CBT adapted for the medical context.

Does treating the medical condition automatically treat the depression?

Not always. Treating the underlying medical condition is necessary and can resolve or reduce the depression, but it is not always sufficient. For some conditions, effective medical treatment resolves the depression (e.g., hypothyroidism: thyroid hormone replacement often resolves the depression; vitamin B12 deficiency: supplementation often resolves the depression). For other conditions, the medical treatment controls the condition but the depression persists or requires its own treatment (e.g., Parkinson's: dopamine replacement manages motor symptoms but depression often persists and requires CBT and/or SSRIs; MS: disease-modifying treatments reduce relapses but depression often requires separate treatment; stroke: rehabilitation addresses physical deficits but post-stroke depression often requires CBT and/or SSRIs). The evidence-based standard is integrated care: treat the medical condition AND treat the depression, coordinating between the medical team and a CBT-trained clinician. The Nothing-Can-Help-Both distortion (the belief that treating one will not help the other and they are separate competing priorities) blocks this integrated approach. The The-Depression-Is-Just-The-Illness distortion (the belief that the depression will resolve automatically when the illness is treated) blocks mental health treatment that may be needed even after the medical condition is well-managed. If the depression persists despite adequate medical treatment, that is information that the depression needs its own evidence-based treatment — not evidence that nothing will work.
