Somatic symptom disorder is persistent distress about physical symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors related to those symptoms — the symptom may be mild or medically unexplained, but the distress, the medical seeking, and the identity-fusion are severe. ~5-7% of the population meets criteria; ~25-50% of primary-care visits involve somatic symptoms that drive the preoccupation. The harm is in the cycle: the body produces a sensation (chest tightness, stomach pain, fatigue, dizziness), the My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease catastrophizes the sensation into the central problem, the catastrophizing drives the Doctors-Are-Missing-Something and the medical-seeking loop, the seeking produces the Scan-Will-Prove-It and the reassurance that fades in a week, the fading reassurance drives the next seeking, the My-Body-Is-Broken frames the body as fragile and the If-I-Stop-Worrying-It-Will-Worsen treats vigilance as protection, the vigilance intensifies the symptom (attention amplifies sensation), the This-Symptom-Defines-Me fuses identity with the symptom, and the whole pattern confirms that the symptom is the life. CBT is evidence-based for somatic symptom disorder (NICE-recommended, strongest evidence among psychological treatments for somatic symptoms). CBT works by identifying the cognitive distortions layered on the symptom, examining the evidence (the symptom is real, but the conclusion that it is the disease, the identity, or the future is the distortion), reducing the safety behaviors (medical seeking, reassurance seeking, body scanning, symptom-checking apps) that maintain the preoccupation, and rebuilding engagement with life beyond the symptom. Somatic symptom disorder is distinct from health anxiety (symptom-specific distress vs disease-fear), medical gaslighting (preoccupation vs dismissal trauma), chronic illness (preoccupation vs condition coping), hypochondriasis (older DSM term now split into SSD and illness anxiety), and all occupation/identity/transition pages. All tools run in your browser. No signup, no backend, no data sent anywhere. Privacy-first by design — which matters especially when the symptom is stigmatized or the medical history is sensitive.

Why Somatic Symptom Disorder Needs Specific CBT

General health-anxiety tools say "you fear a disease, let's examine the probability." General anxiety tools say "you worry, let's examine the worry." General chronic-illness tools say "you have a condition, let's cope with the condition." General medical-gaslighting tools say "you were dismissed, let's process the dismissal." None of them address the specifics: the My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease catastrophizes a specific present symptom in a way general health-anxiety tools cannot address because the problem is the symptom-preoccupation not the disease-fear, the Doctors-Are-Missing-Something drives the medical-seeking loop in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the seeking is a safety behavior not a worry, the My-Body-Is-Broken frames the body as fragile in a way general chronic-illness tools cannot address because the framing is a distortion about a body that is mostly functioning not a coping challenge about a condition, the Scan-Will-Prove-It chains reassurance to the next test in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the reassurance is a safety behavior that fades in a week, the If-I-Stop-Worrying-It-Will-Worsen treats vigilance as protection in a way general anxiety tools cannot address because the vigilance amplifies the symptom through attention, and the This-Symptom-Defines-Me fuses identity with the symptom in a way general identity tools cannot address because the fusion is a personalization that collapses a life into a sensation. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT for somatic symptom disorder can target directly:

The "My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease" Distortion (Symptom-Catastrophizing / Magnification)

Distortion: "My symptom is the disease. The stomach ache is the cancer, and the cancer is the one thing that defines my body, and the body is the one thing that defines my life, and the life is the one thing that defines my future, and the future is the trap. The symptom is not one signal among many; the symptom is the central problem, and the central problem is the whole of existence."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease. The My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease (the stomach ache is the cancer) was a symptom-catastrophizing and a magnification: the mind takes a physical sensation and inflates it into the central problem, and because the inflation feels like evidence of severity, the inference is that the symptom is the disease. The symptom-catastrophizing (the stomach ache is cancer) is the distortion: the sensation is real, but the conclusion that it is the disease is the magnification of one signal into the whole — the stomach ache is one signal among many (the body also digests, breathes, moves, sleeps), and the base rate of stomach ache being cancer is far lower than the felt certainty. The magnification (the symptom is the central problem) is the distortion: the symptom is one event in a life that contains many events. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that this symptom is the disease versus the evidence that it is one signal among many, what would a neutral observer say about a person with this symptom versus what they would say about the symptom's severity, what is the base rate of this symptom being the disease versus being benign), challenging the catastrophizing (the sensation is real, but the conclusion that it is the disease is the distortion), and reducing the safety behaviors (body scanning, symptom-checking apps, medical seeking) that maintain the magnification — so the mind learns that the symptom is one signal, not the central problem. The symptom is real; the conclusion that it is the disease is the distortion.

The "Doctors-Are-Missing-Something" Distortion (Medical-Seeking-Loop / Safety-Behavior)

Distortion: "The doctors are missing something. The next specialist will find it, and the finding will be the proof, and the proof will be the relief, and the relief will be the safety, and the safety is the one thing that means I can stop, and the stop is the trap. The seeking is not the problem; the seeking is the solution, and the solution is the next appointment."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the Doctors-Are-Missing-Something. The Doctors-Are-Missing-Something (the next specialist will find it) was a medical-seeking-loop and a safety-behavior: the mind treats medical seeking as the solution, and because each appointment produces a brief relief, the inference is that the seeking is working. The medical-seeking-loop (the next specialist will find it) is the distortion: the seeking produces short-acting relief that fades in a week, and the fading drives the next seeking — the loop maintains the preoccupation rather than resolving it. The safety-behavior (the seeking is the solution) is the distortion: the seeking is the problem, because each negative result is interpreted as 'they missed it' rather than 'it is not there,' and the seeking prevents the mind from learning that the symptom is benign. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the next specialist will find something versus the evidence that the seeking is maintaining the preoccupation, what would a clinician say about the seeking versus what they would say about its benefit, what is the base rate of the seeking resolving the symptom versus maintaining it), challenging the loop (the seeking is real, but the conclusion that it is the solution is the distortion — the seeking is the safety behavior that keeps the preoccupation alive), and reducing the seeking — so the mind learns that the symptom tolerates being un-investigated, and the preoccupation fades when the safety behavior is removed. The seeking feels like the solution; the conclusion that it is the solution is the distortion.

The "This-Symptom-Defines-Me" Distortion (Symptom-Identity-Fusion / Personalization)

Distortion: "This symptom defines me. The fatigue is my identity, and the identity is the one thing that means I am the tired one, and the tired one is the one thing that means I cannot do what others do, and the cannot is the one thing that means I am less, and the less is the one thing that means I am the symptom, and the symptom is the trap. I have forgotten who I was before the symptom."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the This-Symptom-Defines-Me. The This-Symptom-Defines-Me (the fatigue is my identity) was a symptom-identity-fusion and a personalization: the mind takes a physical symptom and fuses it with the self, and because the fusion feels like evidence of identity, the inference is that the symptom is the person. The symptom-identity-fusion (I am the tired one) is the distortion: the symptom is real, but the conclusion that it is the identity is the personalization of a sensation into a self — the person is a parent, a friend, a worker, a reader, a walker, a laugher, and the fatigue is one experience among many, not the whole of who they are. The personalization (I am the symptom) is the distortion: the symptom is an event in a life, not the life itself. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that the symptom is the identity versus the evidence that it is one experience among many, what would a neutral observer say about a person with this symptom versus what they would say about the person's identity, what is the base rate of people with this symptom who are also parents, friends, workers, readers), challenging the fusion (the symptom is real, but the conclusion that it is the identity is the distortion), and rebuilding engagement with life beyond the symptom — so the mind learns that the self is larger than the sensation, and the identity returns when the fusion is broken. The symptom is real; the conclusion that it is the identity is the distortion.

If you are in crisis

Somatic symptom disorder, especially when the preoccupation is intense, the medical seeking is consuming, or the This-Symptom-Defines-Me is producing thoughts of self-harm, can coexist with severe distress, despair, or suicidal ideation. Frequently Asked Questions

Is somatic symptom disorder the same as health anxiety?

No. Somatic symptom disorder centers on distress about a specific physical symptom that is present — the chest tightness, the stomach pain, the fatigue — with excessive thoughts, feelings, and behaviors directed at that symptom. Illness anxiety disorder (health anxiety) centers on the fear of having or acquiring a disease when no significant somatic symptoms are present — the worry is about the disease, not a symptom. The My-Symptom-Is-The-Disease, the Scan-Will-Prove-It, and the This-Symptom-Defines-Me are somatic-specific patterns that general health-anxiety tools miss because they target the disease-fear, not the symptom-preoccupation. Use the somatic tools if the primary problem is distress about a specific symptom; use the health-anxiety tools if the primary problem is fear of a disease.

Is the physical symptom real?

Yes. The physical symptom in somatic symptom disorder is real — the person genuinely feels the chest tightness, the stomach pain, the fatigue, the dizziness. The symptom may be medically unexplained (no disease found) or medically explained (a known condition), but the sensation is real either way. Somatic symptom disorder is not about faking or imagining a symptom; it is about the distress, the preoccupation, and the behaviors around a real symptom being excessive and life-consuming. CBT does not tell you the symptom is not real; it targets the cognitive distortions layered on the symptom — the catastrophizing (the symptom is the disease), the safety behaviors (the seeking is the solution), the identity-fusion (the symptom is the self). The symptom is real; the conclusion that it is the disease, the identity, or the future is the distortion. A physician should evaluate any new or worsening physical symptom; the CBT tools address the preoccupation, not the symptom itself.

Why does reducing medical seeking help — shouldn't I keep investigating?

There is a difference between appropriate medical evaluation and the medical-seeking loop. Appropriate evaluation: a new symptom appears, you see a physician, the physician evaluates, you follow the plan. The medical-seeking loop: the symptom has been evaluated by multiple specialists, all results are negative or the condition is known and stable, but the preoccupation drives the next appointment, the next scan, the next opinion — and each negative result is interpreted as 'they missed it' rather than 'it is not there.' The loop maintains the preoccupation because it prevents the mind from learning that the symptom tolerates being un-investigated. CBT helps you distinguish appropriate evaluation (follow the physician's plan) from the loop (seeking that maintains the preoccupation), and reduces the loop — not the appropriate evaluation. If a new symptom appears or a known symptom worsens, see a physician; if the symptom is unchanged and the seeking is the loop, the CBT tools address the loop.
