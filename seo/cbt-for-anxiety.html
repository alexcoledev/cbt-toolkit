Why CBT for Anxiety?

Anxiety is maintained by a loop: an anxious thought triggers physical feelings, which trigger more anxious thoughts, which drive avoidance behaviors — and avoidance feeds the anxiety. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) breaks this loop at every point: it changes the thoughts (cognitive restructuring), calms the body (grounding, breathing), and reverses avoidance (behavioral experiments, exposure).

CBT is the most researched therapy for anxiety. Meta-analyses show large effect sizes for generalized anxiety, social anxiety, panic, and health anxiety. The techniques below are the core skills — you can practice them on your own or with a therapist.

Try It Now: 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Exercise

Anxiety pulls you into the future ("what if..."). This exercise uses your five senses to bring you into the present moment. Follow the prompts — it takes about 2 minutes.

5 things you can SEE
Look around. Name five distinct objects — a chair, a window, a pen, a shadow, a plant.
4 things you can TOUCH
Notice texture and temperature — the fabric of your sleeve, the desk surface, your feet on the floor.
3 things you can HEAR
Listen carefully — a fan, distant traffic, your own breathing.
2 things you can SMELL
If you can't smell much, name two scents you like — coffee, rain, soap.
1 thing you can TASTE
The taste in your mouth right now, or take a sip of water and notice it.

Well done — you're here, in the present.

You just interrupted an anxiety spiral by engaging your senses. The more you practice this, the faster it works. Save this exercise and use it whenever anxiety spikes.

The 6 Core CBT Techniques for Anxiety

Each technique targets a different part of the anxiety loop. Use them in combination — grounding for immediate relief, thought records and worry time for the thinking patterns, and behavioral experiments for the avoidance.

What is Anxiety?

Anxiety is an emotion characterized by an unpleasant state of inner turmoil and includes feelings of dread over anticipated events. Anxiety is different from fear in that fear is defined as the emotional response to a present threat, whereas anxiety is the anticipation of a future one. It is often accompanied by nervous behavior such as pacing back and forth, somatic complaints, and rumination.

1
Thought Records
Core skillCognitive

Anxiety is driven by automatic thoughts like "What if I mess up?" or "Everyone will judge me." A thought record captures these thoughts, identifies the cognitive distortions (catastrophizing, fortune-telling), examines the evidence, and builds a balanced alternative. This is the single most effective CBT exercise for anxiety.

  1. Write the situation that triggered anxiety
  2. Rate your anxiety 0–100
  3. Capture the automatic thoughts
  4. Identify distortions (catastrophizing? fortune-telling?)
  5. List evidence for and against the thought
  6. Write a balanced thought, re-rate anxiety

Cognitive Distortion Identification
CognitiveQuick check

Anxious thoughts follow predictable patterns — "thinking traps." Learning to spot them is half the battle. The most common in anxiety: catastrophizing (expecting the worst), fortune-telling (predicting a negative outcome), overgeneralization (one bad event = always), and what-if thinking.

  1. Write down the anxious thought
  2. Check it against the 11 common distortions
  3. Ask: "Would I say this to a friend?"
  4. Reframe using evidence

3
Worry Time (Stimulus Control)
BehavioralContainment

Anxiety spreads when worries intrude all day. Worry time contains it: you schedule a fixed 15–30 minute window (e.g., 4:00pm) to worry on purpose. The rest of the day, when a worry pops up, you note it and defer it: "I'll worry about this at 4:00." Most worries feel less urgent when you revisit them later.

  1. Pick a fixed worry window (same time daily, not before bed)
  2. Keep a "worry list" during the day
  3. At worry time: review each item, problem-solve or let go
  4. When time is up, stop — worries wait until tomorrow
4
Grounding (5-4-3-2-1)
Immediate reliefSomatic

When anxiety spikes, your mind races into the future. Grounding yanks you back to the present by engaging all five senses. It's the fastest in-the-moment anxiety technique — try it above in the interactive widget. Other grounding variants: name things in categories (animals, cities), count backward by 7s, or hold an ice cube.

5
Slow Diaphragmatic Breathing
SomaticPhysiological

Anxiety triggers fast, shallow chest breathing, which itself generates more anxiety (a physical feedback loop). Slow belly breathing reverses it by activating the parasympathetic nervous system. Aim for ~6 breaths per minute: inhale 4 seconds, exhale 6 seconds, from the diaphragm (belly rises, chest stays still).

  1. Hand on belly, hand on chest
  2. Inhale through nose 4 sec — belly rises
  3. Exhale through mouth 6 sec — belly falls
  4. Repeat for 2–5 minutes
6
Behavioral Experiments & Exposure
BehavioralAnti-avoidance

Avoidance keeps anxiety alive — every time you avoid the feared situation, your brain learns "that was dangerous." Behavioral experiments test your anxious predictions against reality. You predict an outcome, do the thing, and observe what actually happens. The gap between prediction and reality is what dissolves the anxiety.

  1. Identify the avoided situation + anxious prediction ("If I speak up, I'll freeze")
  2. Rate belief in the prediction 0–100
  3. Do the situation (start small, build a hierarchy)
  4. Record what actually happened
  5. Compare prediction vs. reality — update the belief
