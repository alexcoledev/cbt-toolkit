Why CBT for Depression?

Depression is maintained by a downward spiral: low mood leads to withdrawal and inactivity, and inactivity removes the sources of pleasure and achievement — which deepens the low mood, which drives more withdrawal. CBT breaks this spiral at two levels. Behaviorally, behavioral activation restarts activity before motivation returns (action comes first, feeling follows). Cognitively, thought records target the negative triad — the depressive beliefs about yourself, the world, and the future.

CBT is the most researched therapy for depression, with hundreds of randomized trials and large effect sizes. It works as well as medication for mild to moderate depression, and the skills persist after treatment because you learn them. The techniques below are the core skills — you can practice them on your own or with a therapist.

Try It Now: Activity Scheduler (Behavioral Activation)

Depression tells you nothing will feel good, so you stop doing things — and then nothing does feel good. This tool reverses it. Add a small activity, predict how much pleasure and mastery (sense of achievement) you expect (0–10). After you do it, rate the actual pleasure and mastery. The gap between predicted and actual is the key insight: depression makes you underpredict. Seeing the gap is what restarts the upward spiral.

The 6 Core CBT Techniques for Depression

Each technique targets a different part of the depressive spiral. Start with behavioral activation — it produces the fastest mood lift. Add thought records and the positive data log to retrain thinking. Use behavioral experiments to test depressive beliefs against reality.

1
Behavioral Activation (Activity Scheduling)
Core skillBehavioralFastest lift

The single most effective CBT exercise for depression. Depression says "wait until you feel like it" — behavioral activation says "do it first, the feeling follows." You schedule small activities, predict how rewarding they'll be, do them, then rate the actual reward. The predicted-vs-actual gap reveals that depression underestimates how good activities feel. Try the interactive scheduler above.

  1. List 5–10 small activities you used to enjoy or value
  2. Schedule one per day (tiny is fine — 5 minutes counts)
  3. Predict pleasure and mastery 0–10 before doing it
  4. Do it, regardless of motivation
  5. Rate actual pleasure and mastery — notice the gap

2
Thought Records (Negative Triad)
CognitiveCore skill

Depression is driven by the negative triad: negative views of yourself ("I'm worthless"), the world ("nothing good happens"), and the future ("it'll always be this way"). A thought record captures these automatic thoughts, identifies the distortions (all-or-nothing thinking, mental filter, disqualifying the positive), examines the evidence, and builds a balanced alternative.

  1. Write the situation that triggered the low mood
  2. Rate your mood 0–100
  3. Capture the automatic negative thoughts
  4. Identify distortions (all-or-nothing? mental filter? disqualifying positive?)
  5. List evidence for and against each thought
  6. Write a balanced thought, re-rate mood

3
Cognitive Distortion Identification
CognitiveQuick check

Depressive thoughts follow predictable patterns. The most common in depression: all-or-nothing thinking ("if I can't do it perfectly, I'm a failure"), mental filter (one negative detail drowns out everything), disqualifying the positive ("that doesn't count"), overgeneralization (one setback = "I always fail"), and should statements ("I should be over this by now"). Spotting the pattern is half the reframe.

  1. Write down the depressive thought
  2. Check it against the 11 common distortions
  3. Ask: "Would I judge a friend this harshly?"
  4. Reframe using evidence

4
Positive Data Log (Gratitude Log)
CognitiveCounter negative bias

Depression installs a negative attention bias — you notice and remember the bad, and filter out the good. A positive data log forces the opposite: each evening, write three things that went well or that you're grateful for, however small ("the coffee was hot", "I answered one email"). Over 2–3 weeks this retrains your brain to register positive events, directly countering the mental filter and disqualifying-the-positive distortions.

  1. Each evening, write 3 positive things from the day
  2. Include small things — "small" is the point
  3. Note your role in making each happen
  4. Review the log when mood dips
5
Behavioral Experiments
BehavioralTest beliefs

Depression holds beliefs like "I can't enjoy anything anymore" or "I'm useless to everyone." Behavioral experiments test these against reality. You predict an outcome, do the activity, and record what actually happened. The gap between the depressive prediction and reality is what updates the belief — far more powerfully than arguing with the thought.

  1. Identify the depressive belief ("If I go out, I'll feel worse")
  2. Rate belief in the prediction 0–100
  3. Do the activity (start small)
  4. Record what actually happened and how you felt
  5. Compare prediction vs. reality — update the belief
6
Activity – Mood Monitoring
BehavioralInsight

The link between what you do and how you feel is invisible during depression — mood seems to come from nowhere. Tracking activity and mood side by side makes the link visible: you see that mood rises on days with more activity, more social contact, more time outside. This evidence motivates behavioral activation even when motivation is low.

  1. Log daily activities + mood (use the mood tracker)
  2. After 1–2 weeks, look for patterns
  3. Identify which activities lift mood
  4. Schedule more of those, deliberately
