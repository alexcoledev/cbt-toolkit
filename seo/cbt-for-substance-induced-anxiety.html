Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (DSM-5 292.89) is an Anxiety Disorder characterized by prominent anxiety or panic attacks that are the direct physiological consequence of substance intoxication or withdrawal, or medication use. The anxiety symptoms (panic attacks, generalized anxiety, obsessions/compulsions) are caused by the pharmacological effects of a substance or medication — not by a primary mental disorder and not during delirium. Common substances of abuse that produce anxiety include alcohol (withdrawal produces prominent anxiety, tremor, and panic), caffeine (intoxication produces anxiety and palpitations), cocaine and amphetamines (intoxication produces severe anxiety, panic, and paranoia through sympathetic activation), cannabis (intoxication can produce anxiety and panic), opioids (withdrawal produces severe anxiety and restlessness), sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics (withdrawal produces rebound anxiety — especially benzodiazepine withdrawal), and hallucinogens/PCP (intoxication produces anxiety and panic). Common medications that produce anxiety include thyroid preparations (excessive dose produces anxiety), corticosteroids (prednisone produces anxiety, agitation, insomnia), sympathomimetic asthma medications (albuterol, theophylline produce anxiety and palpitations), anticholinergics, antidepressants (during initiation or at high doses), and insulin (hypoglycemia produces adrenaline release and panic-like symptoms). Specifiers include with panic attacks, with generalized anxiety, and with obsessive-compulsive symptoms. The PRIMARY treatment is addressing the substance use or adjusting the medication (e.g., stopping cocaine, tapering alcohol, switching asthma medications, adjusting the thyroid dose). CBT is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment that addresses residual anxiety symptoms, the cognitive distortions that interfere with substance cessation or medication adherence, and relapse prevention when substance use resumes or medication changes.

What is Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder?

Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (DSM-5 292.89) is an Anxiety Disorder where the anxiety is the direct physiological consequence of a substance (intoxication or withdrawal) or a medication. The DSM-5 criteria require: (a) prominent anxiety or panic attacks; (b) there is evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the disturbance is the direct physiological consequence of a substance (intoxication or withdrawal) or a medication; (c) the disturbance is not better explained by another anxiety disorder (e.g., Panic Disorder, GAD, where the anxiety is not attributable to a substance or medication); (d) the disturbance does not occur exclusively during delirium; (e) the disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. The critical feature is the causal substance/medication link: the anxiety is not coincidental with substance use or medication use but is PRODUCED BY the substance or medication through a known pharmacological mechanism. For example, in cocaine intoxication, the drug blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake, producing massive sympathetic activation (tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis) that manifests as severe anxiety, panic, and paranoia — the anxiety is the pharmacological consequence of the cocaine. In alcohol withdrawal, the abrupt cessation after chronic use produces rebound sympathetic activation (due to upregulated GABA receptors and downregulated glutamate receptors) that manifests as anxiety, tremor, and panic — the anxiety is the pharmacological consequence of the withdrawal. In prednisone therapy, the synthetic glucocorticoid produces anxiety, agitation, and insomnia through effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and limbic system — the anxiety is the pharmacological consequence of the medication. The distinction from a primary anxiety disorder is essential because the treatment sequence differs: the substance or medication must be addressed FIRST (stop the substance, adjust the medication), and CBT is an adjunct for residual symptoms, adherence, and relapse prevention — not the primary intervention.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder

CBT for Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (DSM-5 292.89) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the substance-dismissal and substance-fatalism distortions that block identification of the causative agent and treatment engagement, the monocausal-reductionism and treatment-hopelessness distortions that dismiss CBT and block psychological treatment, the dichotomous-attribution and brokenness-fusion distortions that block integrated care and recovery identity. CBT with substance-psychological integration is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment that addresses residual anxiety, adherence, and relapse prevention alongside substance use treatment or medication management.

1. Psychoeducation, Substance-Anxiety Link Education, Substance/Medication Management & Anxiety Monitoring

The foundation of CBT for Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder is understanding the substance-anxiety link, identifying the cognitive distortions that maintain the anxiety and interfere with substance cessation or medication adherence, ensuring the causative substance or medication is being addressed, and learning anxiety monitoring skills. Psychoeducation: learn the DSM-5 criteria (prominent anxiety/panic attacks, direct physiological consequence of substance intoxication/withdrawal or medication, not better explained by another anxiety disorder, not during delirium, clinically significant impairment), the common substances that produce anxiety (alcohol withdrawal, caffeine intoxication, cocaine/amphetamine intoxication, cannabis intoxication, opioid withdrawal, sedative/hypnotic withdrawal, hallucinogen intoxication), the common medications that produce anxiety (thyroid preparations, corticosteroids, sympathomimetic asthma meds, anticholinergics, antidepressants during initiation, insulin via hypoglycemia), the specifiers (with panic attacks, with generalized anxiety, with obsessive-compulsive symptoms), the treatment sequence (substance/medication addressed FIRST, CBT as adjunct), and the good prognosis with integrated care. Substance-anxiety link education is the cornerstone: many people with substance/medication-induced anxiety have never understood that their anxiety is the DIRECT pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication — the It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion frames the anxiety as purely psychological, the My-Anxiety-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological distortion splits anxiety dichotomously, and the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion dismisses the psychological treatment that addresses residual symptoms. CBT helps the person understand the specific mechanism by which the substance or medication produces anxiety (e.g., cocaine blocks catecholamine reuptake producing sympathetic activation; alcohol withdrawal produces rebound GABA downregulation and glutamate upregulation producing anxiety; prednisone affects the HPA axis and limbic system producing agitation; excessive thyroid hormone increases adrenergic receptor sensitivity producing panic-like episodes), the substance-anxiety temporal link (anxiety onset during intoxication or withdrawal or after medication initiation/adjustment, anxiety improvement when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted), and the importance of identifying and addressing the causative agent as the primary intervention.

2. Cognitive Restructuring of Substance-Dismissal, Substance-Fatalism, Monocausal-Reductionism, Treatment-Hopelessness, Dichotomous-Attribution & Brokenness-Fusion Distortions + Substance-Psychological Integration

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain the condition. Keep an anxiety-substance belief log: each day, write down the distressing thoughts (it is just the substance, my anxiety is permanent because my use is, stopping the substance is enough, the anxiety will never improve, my anxiety is all chemical or all psychological, I am broken at the root), identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the anxiety is the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication requiring identification of the causative agent — the symptoms have a pharmacological mechanism, they correlate with substance use or medication status, and they require addressing the causative agent; CBT reduces residual anxiety even when substance use recovery is ongoing — the nervous system sensitization and learned anxiety patterns respond to CBT even when the substance use recovery continues; residual anxiety and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment — the anxiety that persists after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted, the distortions that interfere with substance cessation or medication adherence, and the relapse anxiety when substance use resumes all require CBT; CBT produces meaningful improvement in substance/medication-induced anxiety — cognitive restructuring reduces symptom amplification, behavioral regulation reduces sympathetic activation, and adherence improves outcomes; anxiety is produced by both pharmacological and psychological factors requiring integrated care — the substance or medication produces the pharmacological anxiety, the psychological interpretation amplifies it, and both must be addressed; the substance use is a medical condition and the medication need is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person — the substance use is treatable, the medication need is manageable, and the identity is separate from the substance use or medication need), and generate a reframe. Use Socratic questioning: If it is just the substance, why do the symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status, why does stopping the substance or adjusting the medication reduce them, and why is there a known pharmacological mechanism? If the anxiety is permanent, why does CBT reduce residual anxiety in substance use recovery, why do people in recovery report lower anxiety over time, and why does the nervous system desensitize with treatment? If stopping the substance is enough, why does residual anxiety persist, why do adherence-interfering distortions worsen outcomes, and why does integrated care outperform substance-only care? The goal is not to deny the substance/medication etiology but to recognize that the anxiety has both pharmacological and psychological components, and both require treatment.

3. Behavioral Regulation, Substance/Medication Management, Anxiety Management Skills & Relapse Prevention

Behavioral regulation: establish regular sleep (sleep deprivation amplifies anxiety and worsens substance cravings), regular meals (irregular eating destabilizes blood sugar and triggers anxiety and cravings), regular activity (moderate exercise reduces sympathetic activation and supports recovery), and substance reduction (caffeine, alcohol, nicotine all interact with anxiety and substance use recovery). Substance/medication management: if the anxiety is substance-induced, engage substance use treatment (detoxification, medication-assisted treatment, counseling, support groups, relapse prevention planning); if the anxiety is medication-induced, work with the prescribing clinician to adjust the dose, switch to an alternative medication, or add an adjunctive anxiolytic — never stop a prescribed medication without medical supervision. Medication adherence tracking (pill diary, medication reminder system, adherence barriers identified and addressed), and medical communication skills (tracking symptoms to report to the medical team, preparing questions for appointments, advocating for integrated care). Anxiety management skills: breathing retraining (slow diaphragmatic breathing reduces sympathetic activation — particularly helpful for stimulant-induced anxiety where sympathetic activation is the mechanism), progressive muscle relaxation (reduces the muscle tension that both substance use and anxiety produce), mindfulness (non-judgmental observation of symptoms reduces the catastrophizing that amplifies them), and grounding (present-moment focus reduces the anticipatory anxiety about cravings or withdrawal). Relapse prevention plan: a written plan that includes the early warning signs of anxiety returning (increased anxiety intensity, increased substance craving, increased medication non-adherence, increased catastrophizing about symptoms), the action steps for each warning sign (return to CBT skills, contact the substance use treatment team or prescribing clinician, increase anxiety monitoring, restart the belief log, contact the CBT clinician), the high-risk situations for anxiety relapse (substance use resumption, medication changes, stressful life events, sleep disruption), and the coping plan for each high-risk situation. The plan is reviewed and updated regularly as recovery progresses and circumstances change.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder

Each distortion maintains the condition by blocking identification of the causative agent, dismissing CBT, blocking integrated care, or preventing recovery identity. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. It-Is-Just-The-Substance (substance-dismissal / pharmacology-etiology-dismissal): "It is just my anxiety. The substance is not the problem. The medication is not causing this. I have always been anxious, the substance just makes it worse." The person dismisses the substance/medication etiology, framing the anxiety as primary and blocking the investigation that would identify the causative agent.
Reframe: "The anxiety is the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication requiring identification of the causative agent. The It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion is a substance-dismissal: the person has dismissed the substance/medication etiology, treating the anxiety as purely psychological when it is the direct pharmacological consequence of a substance or medication. But the anxiety symptoms in this disorder have a known pharmacological mechanism — cocaine produces anxiety through catecholamine reuptake blockade and sympathetic activation, alcohol withdrawal produces anxiety through GABA downregulation and glutamate upregulation, prednisone produces anxiety through HPA axis and limbic system effects, excessive thyroid hormone produces anxiety through adrenergic receptor sensitization. The symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status (anxiety worsens during intoxication or withdrawal, improves when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted), they have a pharmacological mechanism (not just psychological), and they require addressing the causative agent (stopping the substance or adjusting the medication reduces the anxiety). The distortion is dangerous because it blocks the identification of the causative agent — the person attributes the symptoms to primary anxiety and never identifies the cocaine, the alcohol withdrawal, the prednisone, or the excessive thyroid dose that is producing the anxiety. The antidote is substance-anxiety link education: understand the specific mechanism by which substances and medications produce anxiety, recognize that the symptom pattern (correlation with substance use or medication status, pharmacological mechanism, response to addressing the causative agent) indicates substance/medication etiology, and pursue the substance use assessment or medication review that the symptoms warrant. The key question: if it is just the substance, why do the symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status, why is there a known pharmacological mechanism, and why does stopping the substance or adjusting the medication reduce them?"
2. My-Anxiety-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is (substance-fatalism / chronicity-transfer): "My substance use is ongoing, so my anxiety is ongoing. Nothing will help the anxiety because the use is permanent. I am stuck with this anxiety forever because the use is forever." The person fatalizes the anxiety as permanent by transferring the chronicity of the substance use to the anxiety, blocking CBT engagement.
Reframe: "CBT reduces residual anxiety even when substance use recovery is ongoing. The My-Anxiety-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is distortion is a substance-fatalism: the person has transferred the chronicity of the substance use to the anxiety, treating the anxiety as equally permanent. But even when the substance use recovery is ongoing (recovery is a process, not an event), the anxiety is NOT equally permanent — the anxiety has two components: (1) the direct pharmacological anxiety from the substance (which resolves as the substance is cleared and the nervous system normalizes), and (2) the residual anxiety from nervous system sensitization and learned patterns (which is reduced by CBT). The residual anxiety responds to CBT even when the substance use recovery continues — cognitive restructuring reduces the catastrophizing that amplifies symptoms, behavioral regulation reduces sympathetic activation, and exposure to recovery triggers reduces the anxiety about them. People in substance use recovery who receive CBT report lower anxiety than those who receive substance use treatment alone, even at the same recovery stage. The distortion is harmful because it blocks CBT engagement — if the anxiety is permanent, treatment is futile. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful anxiety reduction in substance use recovery. The antidote is recognizing that the anxiety and the substance use are not equally chronic — the substance use recovery may be ongoing, but the anxiety component is treatable. The key question: if my anxiety is permanent because my use is, why does CBT reduce residual anxiety in recovery, why do people in recovery report lower anxiety over time, and why does the nervous system desensitize with treatment?"
3. Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough (monocausal-reductionism / psychological-treatment-dismissal): "I just need to stop using. Therapy is not for me. Stopping will solve everything. Once I stop the substance, the anxiety will go away on its own." The person dismisses CBT as unnecessary, treating substance cessation as sufficient and blocking the psychological treatment that addresses residual anxiety and adherence.
Reframe: "Residual anxiety and adherence-interfering distortions require psychological treatment. The Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion is a monocausal-reductionism: the person has reduced the anxiety to a single cause (the substance) and dismissed the psychological treatment, treating substance cessation as sufficient. But three problems require CBT even after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted: (1) residual anxiety symptoms persist — the nervous system has been sensitized, the anxiety has become a learned response, and the symptom interpretation patterns amplify residual symptoms; (2) adherence-interfering distortions — the It-Is-Just-The-Substance blocks identification of the causative agent, the I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root produces avoidance of treatment settings, and these distortions interfere with the ongoing substance use recovery or medication management; (3) relapse anxiety — when substance use resumes or the medication changes, the anxiety flares with it, and without CBT skills the person catastrophizes the flare, the anxiety amplifies, and the recovery is slower. Integrated care (substance use treatment + CBT) outperforms substance-only care for anxiety — the CBT addresses the psychological factors that substance use treatment cannot reach. The distortion is harmful because it blocks the psychological treatment that would address residual symptoms, improve adherence, and prevent relapse. The antidote is recognizing that substance cessation is necessary but not sufficient — the anxiety has both pharmacological and psychological components, and both require treatment. The key question: if stopping the substance is enough, why does residual anxiety persist after cessation, why do adherence-interfering distortions worsen outcomes, and why does integrated care outperform substance-only care?"
4. The-Anxiety-Will-Never-Improve (treatment-hopelessness / treatment-resistance-fatalism): "Nothing works for my anxiety. I have tried everything. My case is different. The anxiety will never get better because it is caused by a substance." The person fatalizes the anxiety as treatment-resistant, using the substance etiology as evidence that psychological treatment cannot help.
Reframe: "CBT produces meaningful improvement in substance/medication-induced anxiety. The The-Anxiety-Will-Never-Improve distortion is a treatment-hopelessness: the person has fatalized the anxiety as treatment-resistant, using the substance etiology as evidence that CBT cannot help. But CBT produces meaningful improvement in substance/medication-induced anxiety through four mechanisms: (1) cognitive restructuring reduces the symptom amplification that the catastrophizing produces — even when the substance causes the pharmacological anxiety, the person's interpretation of the symptoms (the The-Anxiety-Will-Never-Improve distortion itself) amplifies and prolongs them, and restructuring reduces the amplification; (2) behavioral regulation reduces the sympathetic activation that both the substance and the anxiety produce — breathing retraining, relaxation, and activity regulation reduce the sympathetic tone that amplifies anxiety; (3) substance use recovery and medication adherence improve — the distortions that interfere with cessation or adherence are restructured, and better recovery/adherence reduces the pharmacological anxiety; (4) relapse prevention skills reduce the impact of substance use resumption or medication changes — early warning signs, coping plans, and treatment contact plans reduce the anxiety duration and intensity of each flare. The distortion is a self-fulfilling prophecy — if the anxiety will never improve, the person does not engage with CBT, and the anxiety does not improve. But the evidence shows CBT produces meaningful anxiety reduction in substance/medication-induced anxiety. The antidote is recognizing that the substance etiology does not make the anxiety treatment-resistant — the psychological components of the anxiety respond to psychological treatment. The key question: if the anxiety will never improve, why does CBT reduce symptom amplification, why does behavioral regulation reduce sympathetic activation, and why do integrated care outcomes exceed substance-only care?"
5. My-Anxiety-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological (dichotomous-attribution / pharmacological-psychological-split): "My anxiety is either chemical or mental, not both. Either it is the substance or it is me. I need to figure out which is which and treat them separately." The person splits anxiety dichotomously into all-chemical or all-psychological, blocking the integrated care that addresses both.
Reframe: "Anxiety is produced by both pharmacological and psychological factors requiring integrated care. The My-Anxiety-Is-All-Chemical-Or-All-Psychological distortion is a dichotomous-attribution: the person has split anxiety into mutually exclusive categories (chemical OR psychological), blocking the recognition that anxiety is produced by BOTH. But the anxiety in Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder is inherently both — the substance or medication produces the pharmacological anxiety (palpitations, tremor, sympathetic activation), AND the psychological interpretation amplifies it (catastrophizing about the symptoms increases their severity and duration). The palpitation is chemical (the cocaine produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation "this palpitation means I am dying" amplifies the anxiety about it). The withdrawal anxiety is chemical (the alcohol withdrawal produces it) AND psychological (the interpretation "I cannot cope with this" amplifies the panic about it). The dichotomous split is harmful because it blocks integrated care — if anxiety is all-chemical, only the substance use treatment or medication adjustment addresses it; if all-psychological, only the CBT clinician addresses it. But the anxiety requires BOTH — the substance use treatment or medication adjustment addresses the pharmacological anxiety, and the CBT clinician addresses the psychological interpretation that amplifies it. Integrated care (substance use treatment/medication management + CBT) outperforms either alone. The antidote is recognizing that anxiety is produced by both factors simultaneously — the substance or medication produces the pharmacological substrate, the psychological interpretation amplifies it, and both must be addressed in an integrated plan. The key question: if anxiety is all chemical or all psychological, why does the psychological interpretation amplify chemical symptoms, why does addressing the substance not fully resolve the anxiety, and why does integrated care outperform either alone?"
6. I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root (brokenness-fusion / substance-use-as-identity): "I am defective. I am an addict. I am broken at the root. The substance use means I am a broken person, not a person with a substance use problem." The person fuses the substance use or medication need with personal brokenness, treating a medical condition as evidence of fundamental defectiveness.
Reframe: "The substance use is a medical condition and the medication need is a medical event and the person is a person, not a broken person. The I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root distortion is a brokenness-fusion: the person has fused the substance use or medication need with their identity, treating the condition as evidence of fundamental brokenness rather than a medical condition that happened to a person. But substance use is a medical condition — addiction is a brain disorder involving neuroadaptation, not a character defect; medication-induced anxiety is a pharmacological side effect, not a personal failing; the need for medication is a medical event, not a brokenness. The person is a person who has a substance use condition or who needs a medication, not a broken person. The condition is treatable (substance use treatment manages the condition, CBT manages the anxiety, medication adjustment manages the side effect), the person is not defective (having a substance use condition or needing a medication is a human experience, not a defect), and the identity is separate from the condition (the person is a person, the condition is a condition, and the two are distinct). The distortion is harmful because it blocks recovery — if the person is broken at the root, treatment is futile (you cannot fix brokenness), treatment settings are avoided (they confirm the brokenness), and the identity collapses into the condition (I am my addiction, I am my medication need). But the evidence shows that people with substance use conditions and medication needs recover, manage, and live full lives — the condition is a medical event, not an identity. The antidote is recognizing that the condition is a medical event that happened to a person, that the person is not the condition, and that treatment addresses the condition while the person remains a whole person. The key question: if I am broken at the root, why is the condition treatable, why do people with the condition recover and live full lives, and why is the condition a medical event rather than an identity?"

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder requires coordinated care from both a substance use specialist or prescribing clinician (for the substance use or medication management) and a CBT-trained clinician (for the anxiety), especially when the anxiety is severe or the substance use is complex. Seek help if: the anxiety symptoms correlate with substance use or medication status (worsen during intoxication or withdrawal, improve when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted — this is the hallmark of substance/medication etiology); you are using a substance (alcohol, caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines, cannabis, opioids, sedatives) and the substance use is producing anxiety symptoms that cause significant distress or impairment; you are taking a medication (thyroid preparation, corticosteroid, asthma medication, antidepressant) and the medication is producing anxiety symptoms — do not stop the medication without consulting the prescribing clinician; you have anxiety symptoms and have NOT had a substance use assessment or medication review (the It-Is-Just-The-Substance distortion may be blocking the investigation that would identify a causative agent); the anxiety is producing panic attacks, significant generalized anxiety, or obsessive-compulsive symptoms that interfere with daily functioning; the cognitive distortions are interfering with substance cessation or medication adherence (the I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root produces avoidance of treatment settings, the It-Is-Just-The-Substance dismisses the substance use assessment, the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough dismisses the CBT); you have stopped the substance or adjusted the medication but residual anxiety persists (the residual anxiety requires CBT — the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion may be blocking the psychological treatment); the anxiety is producing secondary problems (depression, relationship strain, occupational limitation, avoidance of treatment settings that worsens the substance use); the substance use resumes or the medication changes and the anxiety flares with it (relapse prevention skills can reduce the anxiety impact of each flare); you have co-occurring conditions (depression, another anxiety disorder, substance use disorders, medical conditions) that complicate treatment. A coordinated care team (substance use specialist or prescribing clinician + CBT clinician) produces the best outcomes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder different from Health Anxiety?

Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (DSM-5 292.89) and Health Anxiety (illness anxiety) both involve anxiety, but the mechanism differs fundamentally. In Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder, the anxiety is the DIRECT PHARMACOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCE of a substance or medication — the substance (e.g., cocaine) is PRODUCING the anxiety symptoms through a known pharmacological mechanism (catecholamine reuptake blockade producing sympathetic activation). The anxiety is a symptom of the substance use or medication. In Health Anxiety, the anxiety is ABOUT having or acquiring a medical condition — the person is preoccupied with having an illness, but the anxiety is not caused by a substance or medication's physiological effects. A person with cocaine intoxication producing palpitations and panic has Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (the cocaine is causing the anxiety). A person without substance use who is preoccupied with the fear of having a heart condition has Health Anxiety (the anxiety is about the condition, not caused by a substance). The treatments differ: Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder requires addressing the substance use or adjusting the medication first with CBT as adjunct; Health Anxiety is treated primarily with CBT (cognitive restructuring of illness beliefs, behavioral experiments, exposure to health-related triggers) without substance cessation as the primary intervention.

What substances and medications most commonly cause anxiety symptoms?

The substances and medications that most commonly produce anxiety symptoms (Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder, DSM-5 292.89) fall into two categories. Substances of abuse: alcohol (withdrawal produces prominent anxiety, tremor, and panic — one of the most common substance causes of anxiety), caffeine (intoxication produces anxiety, palpitations, and panic-like symptoms — even moderate doses in sensitive individuals), cocaine and amphetamines/stimulants (intoxication produces severe anxiety, panic, and paranoia through sympathetic activation), cannabis (intoxication can produce anxiety and panic, especially in naive users or high-THC strains), opioids (withdrawal produces severe anxiety, restlessness, and panic), sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics (withdrawal produces rebound anxiety and panic — especially benzodiazepine withdrawal which can produce severe and prolonged anxiety), hallucinogens and PCP (intoxication produces anxiety and panic), inhalants (intoxication produces anxiety). Medications: thyroid preparations (excessive dose produces anxiety identical to hyperthyroidism — one of the most common medication causes), corticosteroids (prednisone, dexamethasone produce anxiety, agitation, and insomnia — common in inflammatory and autoimmune treatment), sympathomimetic asthma medications (albuterol, theophylline produce anxiety and palpitations), anticholinergics, antihypertensives (some produce anxiety as side effect), antiepileptics (some produce anxiety), antiparkinsonian drugs, antidepressants (can produce anxiety during initiation or at high doses), insulin (hypoglycemia produces adrenaline release and panic-like symptoms). The key diagnostic feature is that the anxiety symptoms correlate with the substance use or medication (worsen with intoxication or withdrawal, improve when the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted) and have a known pharmacological mechanism.

If the anxiety is caused by a substance or medication, do I still need CBT?

Yes, CBT is an evidence-based adjunctive treatment for Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder, even though the substance or medication is the primary cause. Three reasons. First, residual anxiety symptoms often PERSIST after the substance is stopped or the medication is adjusted — the nervous system has been sensitized, the anxiety has become a learned response, and the symptom interpretation patterns (catastrophizing about symptoms) amplify residual symptoms. CBT extinguishes the residual through cognitive restructuring and behavioral regulation. Second, the cognitive distortions (the It-Is-Just-The-Substance that blocks identification of the causative agent, the I-Am-Broken-At-The-Root that produces avoidance of treatment settings, the Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough that dismisses CBT) interfere with SUBSTANCE CESSATION OR MEDICATION ADHERENCE — the person does not engage with substance use treatment, does not take medication consistently, does not attend follow-up appointments. CBT corrects these distortions, improving cessation/adherence and outcomes. Third, substance use RESUMES and medications CHANGE (relapse is common in recovery, medications are adjusted over time), and the anxiety flares with each change. CBT provides relapse prevention skills (early warning signs, coping plan, treatment contact plan) that reduce the anxiety duration and intensity of each flare. Integrated care (substance use treatment/medication management + CBT) outperforms substance-only or medication-only care for anxiety. CBT is not a replacement for substance use treatment or medication management but a critical adjunct that addresses the psychological factors that maintain and amplify the substance/medication-induced anxiety.

Can I have both Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder AND a primary anxiety disorder?

Yes. A person can have both Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (DSM-5 292.89) AND a primary anxiety disorder (e.g., Panic Disorder 300.01, GAD 300.02). For example, a person with cocaine use may have anxiety symptoms directly caused by the cocaine intoxication (the 292.89 diagnosis) AND additional excessive worry about multiple topics that is not attributable to the cocaine (the GAD diagnosis). The DSM-5 allows both diagnoses when each set of symptoms meets full criteria and is not better explained by the other. The treatment addresses both: the substance use is treated (reducing the pharmacologically-caused anxiety), the primary anxiety disorder is treated with CBT and/or medication (reducing the primary anxiety), and the integrated plan coordinates the substance use treatment and psychological treatment. The cognitive distortions may overlap (the catastrophizing amplifies both the substance-induced and primary anxiety) but the etiology differs (the 292.89 anxiety is pharmacologically caused, the primary anxiety is not). A thorough diagnostic evaluation by a clinician who understands both substance-induced and primary anxiety disorders is essential to distinguish the components and design the integrated treatment.

What if I need the medication that is causing my anxiety?

If a prescribed medication (e.g., prednisone for an autoimmune condition, thyroid hormone for hypothyroidism, an asthma inhaler for asthma) is producing anxiety symptoms, you face a treatment dilemma: the medication is medically necessary but the anxiety is intolerable. Do not stop the medication on your own — work with your prescribing clinician to find the best balance. Options include: (1) dose adjustment — the lowest effective dose may produce fewer anxiety side effects; (2) alternative medication — a different medication in the same class may produce fewer anxiety side effects (e.g., a different corticosteroid, a different asthma medication); (3) adjunctive anxiolytic — a medication to counteract the anxiety (e.g., a beta-blocker for sympathomimetic-induced anxiety, a short-acting benzodiazepine during corticosteroid pulses — used cautiously); (4) timing adjustment — taking the medication at a time that minimizes anxiety impact (e.g., morning rather than evening to reduce insomnia-related anxiety); (5) CBT for the residual anxiety — even with optimal medication management, some anxiety may persist, and CBT addresses the residual through cognitive restructuring (reducing catastrophizing about the anxiety), behavioral regulation (reducing sympathetic activation), and relapse prevention (coping with medication cycles). The My-Anxiety-Is-Permanent-Because-My-Use-Is distortion may tell you the anxiety is permanent because the medication is necessary, but the anxiety is treatable even when the medication is ongoing — CBT reduces the residual anxiety component. The Stopping-The-Substance-Is-Enough distortion does not apply here — the medication is necessary, so the anxiety must be managed through CBT and adjunctive strategies, not through stopping the causative agent.
