What is Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder?

Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder (DSM-5 292.9) is a psychotic disorder in the Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders category, diagnosed when: (1) Criterion A — prominent delusions or hallucinations are present; (2) Criterion B — evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the symptoms developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal, or after medication use that is etiologically related to the symptoms; (3) Criterion C — the disturbance is not better explained by a non-substance psychotic disorder (schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder); (4) Criterion D — the symptoms do not occur exclusively during delirium; (5) Criterion E — causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. Three onset specifiers: with onset during intoxication (symptoms develop during intoxication with the substance), with onset during withdrawal (symptoms develop during withdrawal from the substance), and with onset after medication use (symptoms develop after exposure to a medication). Common substances that can induce psychosis include alcohol, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, phencyclidine and other arylcyclohexylamines, sedative-hypnotics-anxiolytics, and stimulants (cocaine, amphetamine, methamphetamine, and other sympathomimetics). Common medications include anesthetics, antihistamines, antiparkinsonian medications, anticholinergics, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics (paradoxically, during treatment or withdrawal), corticosteroids, dopamine agonists, immunomodulators, muscle relaxants, and others. The hallmark is the identified exogenous cause: the psychosis is triggered by the substance or medication, not by an endogenous process. However, the distinction from a primary psychotic disorder is not always clean: some individuals develop persistent psychosis after substance exposure that may represent a substance-triggered underlying psychotic disorder (the substance was the trigger, but the vulnerability was pre-existing). CBTp is evidence-based and addresses the cognitive distortions layered on top of the substance etiology — the meaning the person makes of the cause, the attribution of symptoms, and the relationship between substance use and psychotic symptoms.

The "The-Substance-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis" Distortion (etiology-dismissal / substance-as-not-real-gating)

Distortion: "The substance caused it, so it is not real psychosis. Real psychosis comes from within — mine was caused by a drug/medication, so it is just a drug effect, not a real psychotic episode. Because it was substance-induced, it is not a mental health problem, and I do not need psychiatric or psychological treatment."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-Substance-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis. The The-Substance-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis (the substance caused it, so it is not real psychosis) was an etiology-dismissal and a substance-as-not-real-gating: the mind treats the substance etiology as evidence the psychosis is not real, and because real psychosis is assumed to be endogenous (from within), the inference is that exogenous (substance-caused) psychosis is not a real psychotic episode. The etiology-dismissal (substance-caused = not real) is the distortion: substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder is a recognized DSM-5 diagnosis (292.9) whose defining feature is that prominent delusions or hallucinations develop during or soon after substance intoxication/withdrawal or medication use — the substance etiology is the diagnostic criterion, not evidence against the reality of the symptoms. The substance-as-not-real-gating (drug effect = not psychosis) is the distortion: the delusions and hallucinations are real symptoms regardless of whether the cause is endogenous or exogenous — the person experiences them as real, they cause real distress and impairment, and they meet Criterion A (prominent delusions or hallucinations). CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that substance-caused psychosis is not real versus the evidence that the delusions and hallucinations are experienced as real and cause functional impairment), challenging the etiology-dismissal (the substance caused the symptoms, but the conclusion that they are not real is the distortion), and validating the reality (the delusions and hallucinations are real symptoms, and the substance etiology does not make them less real or less distressing) — so the mind learns that the etiology is a feature of the diagnosis, not evidence against the symptoms. The substance caused the symptoms; the conclusion that they are not real is the distortion.

The "If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop" Distortion (cessation-certainty / substance-as-sole-cause)

Distortion: "If I stop the substance, the psychosis will just stop. The substance is the cause, so removing the cause removes the effect. I do not need treatment for the psychosis itself — I just need to stop using the substance (or stop the medication), and the delusions and hallucinations will go away on their own."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop. The If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop (if I stop the substance, the psychosis will just stop) was a cessation-certainty and a substance-as-sole-cause: the mind treats cessation as a guarantee that the psychosis will resolve, and because the substance is the cause, the inference is that removing the cause automatically removes the effect. The cessation-certainty (stopping = resolution) is the distortion: while substance-induced psychosis often resolves after the substance is cleared, this is not guaranteed — some cases persist for weeks, months, or longer after cessation (particularly with stimulants, where psychotic symptoms can persist well beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal), and a subset of cases do not fully resolve and may represent a substance-triggered underlying psychotic disorder. The substance-as-sole-cause (the substance is the only factor) is the distortion: the substance is the precipitating factor, but individual vulnerability (genetic loading, prior psychotic episodes, neurodevelopmental factors, stress) plays a role in who develops psychosis and how long it persists — cessation removes the precipitant but does not address the vulnerability or the persisting symptoms. CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual timeline of symptom resolution after cessation, what is the evidence that symptoms are persisting versus resolving, what is the base rate of persistent psychosis after cessation), challenging the cessation-certainty (cessation is necessary, but the conclusion that it is sufficient is the distortion), and building coping skills for the period during which symptoms may persist — so the mind learns that cessation is the first step, not the only step. Cessation is necessary; the conclusion that it is sufficient is the distortion.

The "My-Psychosis-Is-Only-A-Drug-Problem-Not-A-Mental-Health-Problem" Distortion (category-split / substance-vs-mental-health-dichotomy)

Distortion: "My psychosis is only a drug problem, not a mental health problem. This is about the substance, not about my mind. I should deal with the substance use (rehab, detox, stopping the drug) and I do not need to see a psychiatrist or a therapist — those are for real mental health problems, not for drug-induced symptoms."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the My-Psychosis-Is-Only-A-Drug-Problem-Not-A-Mental-Health-Problem. The My-Psychosis-Is-Only-A-Drug-Problem-Not-A-Mental-Health-Problem (my psychosis is only a drug problem, not a mental health problem) was a category-split and a substance-vs-mental-health-dichotomy: the mind treats the substance etiology as placing the psychosis in a separate category from mental health, and because drug problems are seen as separate from mental health problems, the inference is that psychiatric and psychological treatment is not appropriate. The category-split (drug problem ≠ mental health problem) is the distortion: substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder is classified in the DSM-5 within the Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders category — it is a mental health diagnosis, not just a drug problem, and the psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations) are mental health symptoms that warrant mental health treatment. The substance-vs-mental-health-dichotomy (treat the drug OR treat the mind) is the distortion: substance use and psychotic symptoms are often intertwined — the substance may have triggered the psychosis, the psychosis may drive continued substance use (self-medication, coping with distressing symptoms), and the vulnerability that made the person susceptible to substance-induced psychosis may also indicate an underlying mental health condition that requires attention. CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that drug-induced psychosis does not warrant mental health treatment versus the evidence that the delusions and hallucinations cause distress and impairment that CBTp can address), challenging the category-split (the substance caused the symptoms, but the conclusion that they are not a mental health problem is the distortion), and building integrated coping that addresses both the substance use and the psychotic symptoms — so the mind learns that the psychosis is a mental health problem regardless of the substance etiology. The substance caused the symptoms; the conclusion that they are not a mental health problem is the distortion.

The "The-Medication-Caused-This-So-My-Doctor-Made-It-Worse" Distortion (iatrogenic-blame / prescriber-causation-fusion)

Distortion: "The medication caused this, so my doctor made it worse. My doctor prescribed a medication that gave me psychosis — that is iatrogenic harm. I cannot trust my doctor anymore, and I should stop all medications because the treatment itself is dangerous."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-Medication-Caused-This-So-My-Doctor-Made-It-Worse. The The-Medication-Caused-This-So-My-Doctor-Made-It-Worse (the medication caused this, so my doctor made it worse) was an iatrogenic-blame and a prescriber-causation-fusion: the mind treats the medication-induced psychosis as evidence the prescriber caused harm, and because the doctor prescribed the medication, the inference is that the doctor is responsible for the psychotic symptoms and cannot be trusted. The iatrogenic-blame (the doctor made it worse) is the distortion: medication-induced psychotic disorder (DSM-5 292.11) is a recognized adverse effect of certain medications (corticosteroids, dopamine agonists, anticholinergics, immunomodulators, anesthetics, and others) — the prescriber did not intend the harm, the adverse effect is a known risk that was weighed against the benefit of treating the underlying condition, and the psychosis is typically reversible with dose adjustment or discontinuation. The prescriber-causation-fusion (the doctor is responsible) is the distortion: the doctor prescribed the medication for a legitimate medical reason, the psychotic reaction is an individual adverse response (not all patients develop it), and assigning blame to the prescriber collapses the complexity of medication risk-benefit into a simple causation that prevents continued treatment engagement. CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what was the underlying condition the medication was treating, what was the risk-benefit discussion, what is the actual evidence that the doctor was negligent versus the evidence that this is a known adverse effect), challenging the iatrogenic-blame (the medication caused the psychosis, but the conclusion that the doctor made it worse is the distortion), and rebuilding the treatment relationship (the doctor can adjust the medication, monitor for resolution, and continue treating the underlying condition) — so the mind learns that the adverse effect is a known risk, not prescriber harm. The medication caused the psychosis; the conclusion that the doctor made it worse is the distortion.

The "I-Cannot-Have-Psychosis-From-A-Substance-I-Use-Normally" Distortion (normal-use-immunity / dose-frequency-gating)

Distortion: "I cannot have psychosis from a substance I use normally. I only use this substance as prescribed (or in normal amounts, or only occasionally) — psychosis from substances only happens to addicts or abusers. Because I am not an addict, the substance cannot be causing my psychosis, so there must be another explanation."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the I-Cannot-Have-Psychosis-From-A-Substance-I-Use-Normally. The I-Cannot-Have-Psychosis-From-A-Substance-I-Use-Normally (I cannot have psychosis from a substance I use normally) was a normal-use-immunity and a dose-frequency-gating: the mind treats the normal/medical use pattern as evidence the substance cannot be the cause, and because psychosis is assumed to only happen with abuse or addiction, the inference is that a normal user is immune. The normal-use-immunity (normal use = no psychosis) is the distortion: substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder can occur with therapeutic doses of prescribed medications (corticosteroids, dopamine agonists, anticholinergics) and with recreational use that the person considers normal — the reaction depends on individual vulnerability and the specific substance, not solely on the dose or frequency. The dose-frequency-gating (only high doses/abuse causes psychosis) is the distortion: while higher doses increase the risk, psychotic reactions can occur at therapeutic doses of medications and at moderate doses of substances — the threshold is individual, and what is normal for one person may be sufficient to trigger psychosis in another. CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the actual evidence that normal use cannot cause psychosis versus the evidence that medication-induced psychosis occurs at therapeutic doses, what is the timeline of symptom onset relative to substance use, what is the individual vulnerability profile), challenging the normal-use-immunity (the use is normal, but the conclusion that it cannot cause psychosis is the distortion), and accepting the substance as the likely precipitant without moral judgment — so the mind learns that the reaction is about individual vulnerability, not about the use pattern. The use is normal; the conclusion that it cannot cause psychosis is the distortion.

The "My-Substance-Use-And-My-Psychosis-Are-Unrelated" Distortion (attribution-severance / independent-problems-fusion)

Distortion: "My substance use and my psychosis are unrelated. They are two separate problems that happen to co-occur. I will treat the substance use with one provider and the psychosis with another, and I do not need to connect them — they have nothing to do with each other."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the My-Substance-Use-And-My-Psychosis-Are-Unrelated. The My-Substance-Use-And-My-Psychosis-Are-Unrelated (my substance use and my psychosis are unrelated) was an attribution-severance and an independent-problems-fusion: the mind treats the substance use and the psychotic symptoms as independent problems, and because they co-occur, the inference is that the co-occurrence is coincidental rather than causal. The attribution-severance (they are unrelated) is the distortion: substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder is diagnosed when there is evidence from the history, physical exam, or laboratory that the symptoms developed during or soon after substance intoxication/withdrawal or medication use — the temporal relationship is the diagnostic evidence, and severing the attribution ignores the criterion that defines the disorder. The independent-problems-fusion (treat them separately) is the distortion: substance use and psychotic symptoms are often bidirectionally related — the substance may trigger the psychosis, the psychosis may drive continued substance use (self-medication of distressing symptoms, coping with hallucinations, managing the fear), and treating them separately misses the interaction that maintains both. CBTp helps by examining the evidence (what is the temporal relationship between substance use and symptom onset, what is the evidence that the symptoms worsen with use and improve with cessation, what is the evidence that the psychosis drives substance use), challenging the attribution-severance (the problems co-occur, but the conclusion that they are unrelated is the distortion), and building integrated coping that addresses the substance-psychosis link — so the mind learns that the problems are connected and integrated treatment is more effective than parallel separate treatment. The problems co-occur; the conclusion that they are unrelated is the distortion.

If you are in crisis

Substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder, especially during intoxication, withdrawal, or if symptoms are persisting after cessation, can involve distress, fear, confusion, or thoughts of self-harm. The symptoms are real; the conclusions that the substance-caused psychosis is not real, that cessation will automatically resolve everything, that this is only a drug problem, that the prescriber is to blame, that normal use cannot cause psychosis, or that the substance use and psychosis are unrelated are the distortions CBTp targets. Frequently Asked Questions

Is CBTp evidence-based for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder?

Yes. CBT for psychosis (CBTp) is evidence-based for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder. NICE guidelines recommend CBTp for all people with psychosis, including substance-induced psychosis, as a first-line adjunct to medication. The approach is particularly valuable after the acute intoxication or withdrawal period has resolved, when the person has the cognitive resources to engage in attribution work, meaning-making, and integrated coping. The If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop distortion defers this work until after cessation; the evidence shows that CBTp engagement during and after the acute period improves outcomes regardless of whether the symptoms resolve quickly or persist. The identified exogenous cause makes CBTp particularly suitable: the cognitive distortions layered on top of the substance etiology (the etiology-dismissal, the cessation-certainty, the category-split, the iatrogenic-blame, the normal-use-immunity, the attribution-severance) are the primary treatment target, and addressing them improves the outcome whether the symptoms resolve with cessation or persist and require ongoing treatment.

What happens in a CBTp session for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder?

A CBTp session for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder involves: (1) Engagement and assessment — the therapist builds a therapeutic relationship, assesses the current symptoms, the substance or medication involved, the timeline of symptom onset relative to substance exposure, the functional impact, and the meaning the person has made of the substance etiology. (2) Formulation — the therapist builds a personalized model of the substance-symptom relationship, the attribution patterns, and the cognitive distortions layered on top (the The-Substance-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis, the If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop, etc.). (3) Psychoeducation about substance-induced psychosis — helping the person understand that the symptoms are real regardless of the cause, that cessation is necessary but not always sufficient, and that the substance and the psychosis are bidirectionally related. (4) Cognitive restructuring — targeting the specific distortions (the etiology-dismissal, the cessation-certainty, the category-split, the iatrogenic-blame, the normal-use-immunity, the attribution-severance). (5) Integrated coping skills — coping with persisting psychotic symptoms, managing substance use or medication concerns, and building skills that address both problems together. (6) Relapse-prevention planning — identifying early warning signs, developing a plan for future substance exposure or medication changes, and building skills that support recovery. CBTp for substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder is typically 8-16 sessions, delivered by a therapist trained in CBTp, often in collaboration with an addiction medicine specialist or the prescribing physician. The approach is collaborative and integrated — the goal is to support engagement, address the meaning made of the cause, and build coping skills that improve the outcome whether the symptoms resolve with cessation or persist.

Why does substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder need CBTp instead of just stopping the substance?

Stopping the substance or medication (cessation) is the necessary first step and can reduce the psychotic symptoms, but it does not address the cognitive distortions that maintain the meaning made of the substance etiology and the attribution of symptoms. The The-Substance-Caused-It-So-It-Is-Not-Real-Psychosis, the If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop, the My-Psychosis-Is-Only-A-Drug-Problem-Not-A-Mental-Health-Problem, the The-Medication-Caused-This-So-My-Doctor-Made-It-Worse, the I-Cannot-Have-Psychosis-From-A-Substance-I-Use-Normally, and the My-Substance-Use-And-My-Psychosis-Are-Unrelated are thought patterns that cessation does not directly target — they require cognitive restructuring. CBTp addresses these distortions by examining evidence, building integrated coping skills, and supporting treatment engagement. Additionally, the If-I-Stop-The-Substance-The-Psychosis-Will-Just-Stop can itself delay treatment engagement past the point where symptoms are persisting — cessation alone does not address this deferral pattern. The persisting symptoms (which can last weeks to months after cessation, particularly with stimulants) require coping skills that cessation does not provide. The relationship between substance use and psychotic symptoms is often bidirectional (the psychosis may drive continued use as self-medication) — cessation alone does not address this interaction. The integrated approach (cessation for the acute cause + CBTp for the meaning, the attribution, and the integrated coping) produces better outcomes than cessation alone: cessation removes the precipitant, CBTp addresses the meaning and the coping, and the combination improves the outcome whether the symptoms resolve quickly or persist.
