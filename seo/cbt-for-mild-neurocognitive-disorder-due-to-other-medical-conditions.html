Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Other Medical Conditions is diagnosed when there is modest cognitive decline from a previous level of performance due to a systemic medical condition affecting the brain secondarily — such as endocrine disorders (hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism), metabolic disorders, vitamin deficiencies (B12, folate, thiamine), autoimmune conditions (lupus cerebritis, Hashimoto encephalopathy), infections (Lyme, neurosyphilis), organ failure (hepatic encephalopathy, uremic encephalopathy), sleep disorders (obstructive sleep apnea), or toxic exposures — the decline does not meet the major threshold, and independence in everyday activities is preserved, though greater effort or compensatory strategies may be needed. Distinctive features include: (a) the cognitive decline is SECONDARY to a systemic medical condition (not a primary neurodegenerative or vascular process), (b) the cognitive decline is often POTENTIALLY REVERSIBLE or MODIFIABLE with treatment of the underlying medical condition (B12 deficiency cognitive impairment may reverse with replacement, hypothyroid cognitive impairment may reverse with thyroid replacement, hepatic encephalopathy may improve with liver function optimization, sleep apnea cognitive dysfunction may improve with CPAP), (c) the cognitive profile varies with the underlying condition, (d) IDENTIFICATION AND TREATMENT OF THE UNDERLYING MEDICAL CONDITION is the primary intervention, (e) preserved independence in instrumental activities of daily living (managing medications, finances, transportation, complex tasks), though with greater effort or compensatory strategies, (f) a WINDOW FOR EARLY INTERVENTION where treating the underlying condition may reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline, making early identification and treatment especially important.

What is Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Other Medical Conditions?

Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Other Medical Conditions (DSM-5 294.0x) is a diagnosis in the Neurocognitive Disorders category, used when: (1) there is evidence of modest cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition) — based on both concern from the individual, a knowledgeable informant, or the clinician, AND objective evidence of decline; (2) the cognitive deficits do NOT interfere with independence in everyday activities (IADLs are preserved), though greater effort, compensatory strategies, or assistance may be needed — this is the 'mild' threshold, distinguishing it from Major NCD; (3) the deficits do not occur exclusively during delirium; (4) the deficits are not better explained by another mental or medical disorder; (5) there is evidence that the cognitive decline is due to a SYSTEMIC MEDICAL CONDITION affecting the brain secondarily — a condition other than the specific etiologies already classified (Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, prion, HIV, Huntington, TBI, alcohol). The distinctive clinical features include: the cognitive decline is secondary to a systemic medical condition (endocrine, metabolic, vitamin deficiency, autoimmune, infectious, organ failure, sleep disorders, toxic exposures — the medical condition affects the brain indirectly through systemic metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, or toxic mechanisms, unlike the direct neurodegeneration of Alzheimer's or the direct vascular damage of vascular disease), often potentially reversible or modifiable with treatment of the underlying condition (B12 deficiency cognitive impairment may reverse with B12 replacement, hypothyroid cognitive impairment may reverse with thyroid hormone replacement, hepatic encephalopathy may improve with optimization of hepatic function, uremic encephalopathy may improve with dialysis or renal function optimization, sleep apnea cognitive dysfunction may improve with CPAP treatment, autoimmune encephalopathy may improve with immunomodulatory treatment — this reversibility potential is a key distinction from the progressive neurodegenerative etiologies), the cognitive profile varies with the underlying condition (hypothyroidism often produces generalized slowing and memory impairment, B12 deficiency may produce subacute combined degeneration with cognitive and myelopathic features, hepatic encephalopathy often produces executive dysfunction and attention deficits, sleep apnea often produces attention and executive deficits — unlike the more uniform profiles of specific neurodegenerative disorders), identification and treatment of the underlying medical condition is the primary intervention (the cognitive decline is managed primarily by treating the medical condition causing it, not by cognitive-specific interventions alone — this means comprehensive medical evaluation is essential and the cognitive symptoms cannot be addressed in isolation), preserved independence in IADLs (the person can still manage medications, finances, appointments, and complex tasks, though with greater effort or compensatory strategies), and a window for early intervention where treating the underlying condition early may reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline before changes become established — making early identification and treatment especially important.

The "The-It-Is-Just-Aging" Distortion (age-attribution / aging-as-explanation)

Distortion: "I am just getting older. Everyone gets forgetful as they age. The memory problems, the slower thinking, the trouble concentrating — that is just normal aging. My mother had the same thing at this age. There is no point seeing a doctor about getting old. It is not a brain condition, it is just aging."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Aging. The age-attribution (it is just getting older) is the distortion: WHILE SOME COGNITIVE CHANGE OCCURS WITH NORMAL AGING, the cognitive decline in mild neurocognitive disorder due to other medical conditions is DISTINCT FROM NORMAL AGING — the decline represents a change from the individual's previous level of performance that is objectively measurable on neuropsychological testing as beyond the expected age-related change, and the medical condition causing the cognitive decline (hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, hepatic encephalopathy, sleep apnea, or another systemic condition) is a TREATABLE cause that normal aging is not. The aging-as-explanation is the distortion: attributing the cognitive changes to aging drives missed evaluation — the laboratory testing that could identify the hypothyroidism is never done, the B12 level that could reveal the deficiency is never checked, the sleep study that could diagnose the sleep apnea is never ordered, and the potentially reversible cognitive decline is left untreated. The everyone-gets-forgetful is the distortion: treating the changes as expected aging drives missed early intervention — the thyroid replacement that could reverse the cognitive impairment is never initiated, the B12 replacement that could reverse the deficiency-related cognitive changes is never started, the CPAP that could improve the sleep apnea cognitive dysfunction is never tried, and the window for potentially reversing the cognitive decline is missed. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does neuropsychological testing show — decline beyond normal aging, what do the laboratory tests show — a medical condition causing the cognitive decline, what is the timeline — the changes correspond to the medical condition) and building engagement with the medical evaluation and treatment that could reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline."

The "The-It-Is-Not-Bad-Enough-For-It-To-Be-Dementia" Distortion (threshold-dismissal / mild-as-non-diagnosis)

Distortion: "It is not bad enough to be dementia. I can still manage my medications, pay my bills, drive, and do everything I need to do. The doctor said it is mild, and mild means it is not really anything. If it were real dementia, I would not be able to function. Since I am still independent, there is nothing wrong. The mild label means it is basically nothing."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Not-Bad-Enough-For-It-To-Be-Dementia. The threshold-dismissal (mild means not real) is the distortion: MILD NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDER IS A CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS, NOT A NON-DIAGNOSIS — the modest cognitive decline represents a change from the previous level that is objectively measurable on neuropsychological testing, is caused by a systemic medical condition affecting the brain, and warrants evaluation, monitoring, treatment of the underlying medical condition, and early intervention, even though independence in IADLs is preserved. The mild-as-non-diagnosis is the distortion: treating preserved independence as evidence of nothing wrong drives missed monitoring — the cognitive decline that could progress to Major NCD is never tracked, the underlying medical condition that could be treated is never properly managed, the baseline that could detect progression is never established, and the potentially reversible cognitive decline is left to worsen. The independence-as-no-brain-damage is the distortion: reducing the diagnosis to the functional threshold drives disengagement — the person stops attending follow-up appointments, stops monitoring the underlying medical condition, stops engaging with the treatment that could reverse the cognitive decline, and the mild stage that could be a window for early intervention becomes a missed opportunity. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does the neuropsychological testing show — objective decline from the previous level, what does the medical evaluation show — a systemic condition causing the cognitive decline, what does the diagnosis mean — a clinical condition warranting treatment) and building engagement with the monitoring and treatment that the mild stage requires."

The "The-It-Is-Just-Mild-So-Nothing-Needs-To-Be-Done" Distortion (mild-as-benign / wait-and-see-as-default)

Distortion: "It is just mild, so nothing needs to be done. The doctor said to just keep an eye on it. Why would I do anything when it is not bad yet? Treatment is for when it gets bad. Right now, it is just mild cognitive stuff, so there is no point. I will deal with it if it gets worse. Mild means wait."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Mild-So-Nothing-Needs-To-Be-Done. The mild-as-benign (mild means nothing to do) is the distortion: THE MILD STAGE IS A WINDOW FOR EARLY INTERVENTION, NOT A PERIOD OF INACTION — treatment of the underlying medical condition may reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline (thyroid replacement for hypothyroidism, B12 replacement for deficiency, optimizing hepatic function for hepatic encephalopathy, CPAP for sleep apnea), behavioral strategies may manage the cognitive changes, cognitive engagement and physical exercise may support neuroplasticity and function, monitoring establishes the baseline to detect the cognitive profile early, and the adjustment difficulties, anxiety, and depression that accompany early cognitive decline are themselves treatable. The wait-and-see-as-default is the distortion: treating watchful waiting as the only response drives missed early intervention — the thyroid replacement that could reverse the cognitive impairment is never initiated, the B12 replacement that could reverse the deficiency-related changes is never started, the CPAP that could improve the sleep apnea cognitive dysfunction is never tried, and the window for potentially reversing the cognitive decline is missed. The mild-as-no-action is the distortion: assuming nothing needs to be done drives disengagement — the person stops attending follow-up, stops engaging with the treatment of the underlying condition, stops building the cognitive and lifestyle habits that could help, and the mild stage that could be an opportunity for early intervention becomes a period of missed opportunity where the potentially reversible cognitive decline is left to progress. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does the research show about early treatment of the underlying condition — may reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline, what does the medical evaluation show — a treatable condition causing the cognitive changes, what are the consequences of waiting — the potentially reversible decline may become established) and building engagement with the early treatment that the mild stage warrants."

The "The-It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition-Not-The-Brain" Distortion (medical-brain-separation / condition-cognition-split)

Distortion: "The doctor said I have hypothyroidism, so I am taking thyroid medication for my thyroid. The memory problems and the slow thinking are a separate issue — maybe that is just age or stress. The thyroid is the thyroid and the brain is the brain. I am treating the thyroid, so why would I also need to do something about my thinking? They are different problems."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-The-Medical-Condition-Not-The-Brain. The medical-brain-separation (the medical condition and the cognitive symptoms are separate) is the distortion: THE COGNITIVE DECLINE IS CAUSED BY THE MEDICAL CONDITION AFFECTING THE BRAIN — the hypothyroidism produces cognitive slowing and memory impairment through systemic metabolic effects on the brain, the B12 deficiency produces cognitive impairment through demyelination and neurotoxic effects, the hepatic encephalopathy produces executive dysfunction through the accumulation of neurotoxins the liver cannot clear, the sleep apnea produces attention and executive deficits through recurrent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation — the medical condition and the cognitive symptoms are NOT separate problems, they are the same problem expressed in two domains. The condition-cognition-split is the distortion: treating the medical diagnosis and the cognitive symptoms as unrelated drives missed recognition that treating the medical condition IS the cognitive treatment — the thyroid replacement that treats the hypothyroidism also treats the cognitive slowing, the B12 replacement that treats the deficiency also treats the cognitive impairment, the CPAP that treats the sleep apnea also treats the cognitive dysfunction — the cognitive symptoms are expected to improve as the medical condition is treated, and monitoring the cognitive changes is part of monitoring the medical condition. The treat-the-body-not-the-mind is the distortion: separating medical and cognitive management drives incomplete care — the person treats the medical condition but never monitors whether the cognitive symptoms are improving, never builds the compensatory strategies that could help during the recovery period, never addresses the anxiety and distress about the cognitive changes, and never engages with the neuropsychological follow-up that could confirm reversibility. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the mechanism — the medical condition causes the cognitive decline through systemic effects on the brain, what does the research show — treating the medical condition may reverse the cognitive symptoms, what does monitoring show — cognitive changes should track with treatment of the medical condition) and building the integrated engagement that recognizes treating the medical condition IS treating the cognitive decline."

The "The-It-Will-All-Reverse-Once-The-Condition-Is-Treated" Distortion (reversibility-overconfidence / treatment-as-complete-cure)

Distortion: "Once I take the B12 pills, my memory will go back to normal completely. The doctor said B12 deficiency can cause memory problems, so fixing the B12 will fix the memory. I do not need to keep checking my thinking or do any brain exercises — the treatment will take care of everything. It is reversible, so once it is treated, it is over. No need for follow-up cognitive testing."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Will-All-Reverse-Once-The-Condition-Is-Treated. The reversibility-overconfidence (treatment means complete reversal) is the distortion: WHILE MANY MEDICAL CONDITIONS HAVE COGNITIVE EFFECTS THAT ARE PARTIALLY OR FULLY REVERSIBLE WITH TREATMENT, THE REVERSIBILITY IS NOT GUARANTEED TO BE COMPLETE — some cognitive changes may persist even after the underlying condition is treated (prolonged B12 deficiency may leave residual cognitive changes, chronic hepatic encephalopathy may produce lasting executive changes, long-standing sleep apnea may leave residual attention deficits), the degree of reversibility depends on the duration and severity of the condition before treatment, the individual's age and comorbidities, and some conditions have a window of reversibility that closes if treatment is delayed. The treatment-as-complete-cure is the distortion: assuming treatment is a total cure drives missed monitoring — the cognitive changes that may persist after treatment are never assessed, the residual cognitive impairment that could benefit from compensatory strategies is never addressed, the anxiety and distress about persistent cognitive changes are never treated, and the neuropsychological follow-up that could confirm the degree of reversibility is never done. The no-monitoring-needed is the distortion: assuming no follow-up is needed drives disengagement — the person stops attending cognitive follow-up, stops monitoring for progression or residual changes, stops building the compensatory strategies that could help with any persistent cognitive difficulties, and the adjustment to any residual cognitive changes is never supported. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does the research show about reversibility — often partial, depends on duration and severity, what does the neuropsychological follow-up show — the degree of reversibility after treatment, what does monitoring show — whether cognitive changes are improving, stable, or persisting) and building engagement with the monitoring, compensatory strategies, and adjustment support that the post-treatment period still warrants."

The "The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same" Distortion (dementia-oversimplification / category-as-uniform)

Distortion: "Dementia is dementia. Whether it is mild or major, Alzheimer's, vascular, from a medical condition, or anything else, it is all the same thing — memory loss and confusion. The specific type does not matter. The treatment is the same, the prognosis is the same, and there is no point in distinguishing them. Mild dementia from a medical condition is just early dementia, and dementia is dementia."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same. The dementia-oversimplification (all neurocognitive disorders are the same) is the distortion: the different types and stages of neurocognitive disorder have DISTINCTIVE FEATURES, DIFFERENT MANAGEMENT, and DIFFERENT PROGNOSIS — mild NCD due to other medical conditions has modest cognitive decline with preserved independence (unlike Major NCD where independence is impaired), the cognitive decline is SECONDARY TO A SYSTEMIC MEDICAL CONDITION (unlike the primary neurodegeneration of Alzheimer's or the direct vascular damage of vascular disease), the cognitive decline is often POTENTIALLY REVERSIBLE with treatment of the underlying condition (unlike the progressive neurodegenerative etiologies where reversibility is not expected), the cognitive profile varies with the underlying condition (unlike the more uniform profiles of specific neurodegenerative disorders), and there is a WINDOW FOR EARLY INTERVENTION where treating the underlying condition may reverse or stabilize the cognitive decline; these features require DIFFERENT MANAGEMENT: (1) the systemic medical etiology means the primary intervention is treating the underlying medical condition (thyroid replacement, B12 replacement, CPAP, optimizing organ function), whereas neurodegenerative disorders focus on symptomatic and supportive care; (2) the potentially reversible nature means active engagement with treatment could reverse the cognitive decline, whereas progressive disorders offer only stabilization; (3) the mild stage means early treatment may be more effective at reversing the cognitive changes, whereas later stages may have less reversibility potential; (4) the preserved independence means compensatory strategies can maximize function during the recovery period, whereas later stages require more support; (5) the monitoring needs are different — cognitive changes should track with treatment of the medical condition, and neuropsychological follow-up confirms the degree of reversibility. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what is the etiology — a systemic medical condition, what is the reversibility potential — often partial or full with treatment, what is the management — treat the underlying condition and monitor cognitive changes, what is the prognosis — may improve with treatment unlike progressive disorders) and building engagement with the specific management that mild NCD due to other medical conditions requires."

If you are in crisis
