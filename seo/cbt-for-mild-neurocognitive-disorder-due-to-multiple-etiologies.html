What is Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Multiple Etiologies?

Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Multiple Etiologies (DSM-5 294.0x) is a diagnosis in the Neurocognitive Disorders category, used when: (1) there is evidence of modest cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition) — based on both concern from the individual, a knowledgeable informant, or the clinician, AND objective evidence of decline; (2) the cognitive deficits do NOT interfere with independence in everyday activities (IADLs are preserved), though greater effort, compensatory strategies, or assistance may be needed — this is the 'mild' threshold, distinguishing it from Major NCD; (3) the deficits do not occur exclusively during delirium; (4) the deficits are not better explained by another mental or medical disorder; (5) there is evidence of TWO OR MORE CONTRIBUTING ETIOLOGIES (e.g., Alzheimer's pathology plus vascular disease on neuroimaging, Alzheimer's plus alcohol-related neurotoxicity on history and imaging, vascular disease plus Lewy body pathology). The distinctive clinical features include: a mixed cognitive profile reflecting the combination of etiologies (e.g., episodic memory impairment from Alzheimer's plus processing speed and executive deficits from vascular disease, or executive dysfunction from alcohol plus visuospatial deficits from Lewy body pathology — unlike the single-profile presentation of a single-etiology disorder), evidence of two or more contributing etiologies (neuroimaging showing both amyloid pathology and vascular changes, history of both alcohol use and vascular risk factors, biomarkers suggesting multiple pathologies), preserved independence in IADLs (the person can still manage medications, finances, appointments, and complex tasks, though with greater effort or compensatory strategies), different trajectories for each contributing etiology (the vascular component may be stabilized with risk factor management while the neurodegenerative component progresses, the alcohol component may be partially reversible with abstinence while the Alzheimer's component progresses), potentially modifiable components at the mild stage (vascular risk factor control, abstinence if alcohol is a contributor, cognitive engagement, physical exercise may stabilize or slow progression of some components), and a window for early intervention where comprehensive management of each contributing etiology may maximize function and quality of life while the trajectory is still early.

Why does mild neurocognitive disorder due to multiple etiologies need CBT instead of just monitoring the symptoms medically?

Monitoring the symptoms medically (regular cognitive assessment, monitoring, neuropsychological testing, neuroimaging, vascular assessment, management of each contributing etiology, referral if progression occurs) is necessary but not sufficient. The cognitive distortions layered on top of the early cognitive decline from multiple etiologies — the The-It-Is-Just-Aging, the The-It-Is-Not-Bad-Enough-For-It-To-Be-Dementia, the The-It-Is-Just-Mild-So-Nothing-Needs-To-Be-Done, the The-It-Is-Just-One-Thing-Not-Multiple, the The-It-Is-Just-The-Worse-One-That-Matters, and the The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same — are thought patterns that medical monitoring alone does not address, and they drive disengagement from the very monitoring, comprehensive management, and early intervention that helps. The The-It-Is-Just-Aging distortion drives missed evaluation. The The-It-Is-Just-Mild-So-Nothing-Needs-To-Be-Done distortion drives missed early intervention. The The-It-Is-Just-One-Thing-Not-Multiple distortion drives missed identification of additional contributors. The The-It-Is-Just-The-Worse-One-That-Matters distortion drives missed management of modifiable smaller contributors. The The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same distortion drives generic care that misses the multiple-etiology-specific features, the mixed cognitive profile, the different trajectories, and the stage-specific early intervention that mild NCD due to multiple etiologies requires. CBT addresses these distortions by examining evidence, building coping skills, and supporting engagement with the evaluation, monitoring, comprehensive management, and early intervention that the mild stage enables. The integrated approach produces better outcomes than medical monitoring alone.
