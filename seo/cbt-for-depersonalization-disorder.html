Cognitive Behavioral Therapy is an evidence-based treatment for depersonalization/derealization disorder, with growing evidence that it reduces dissociation catastrophizing, improves grounding and affect regulation, retrains attentional bias, and increases the capacity to tolerate the unreality without amplifying it. The core maintaining mechanisms are three: unreality-self with insanity-fear (the self is experienced as not real, and the dissociation is interpreted as a sign of breakdown, which produces anxiety that amplifies the dissociation in a feedback loop), unreality-world with permanence-fear (the surroundings are experienced as not real, and the dissociation is interpreted as permanent, which produces hypervigilant monitoring that maintains and deepens the dissociation), and resistance-amplification with body-alienation (fighting the feeling intensifies it, and the body is experienced as foreign, which produces monitoring of the body that deepens the alienation). CBT works by restructuring the catastrophizing core beliefs through Socratic questioning and evidence examination, by grounding and affect regulation that builds the capacity to anchor in the present and to tolerate the unreality without fighting it, and by attention retraining that redirects the monitoring attention that maintains the dissociation. Depersonalization/derealization disorder is a real and treatable condition — not a psychotic break, not a permanent unreality, not a sign that you are losing your mind. The feeling of unreality is real (and distinguishes the disorder from ordinary absence of dissociation); the conclusions that you are not real, that you are going crazy, and that the state is permanent are the distortions. The CBT for Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder Protocol

CBT adapted for depersonalization/derealization disorder is not a single technique but a 3-component protocol designed for the unreality-self, the insanity-fear, and the resistance-amplification presentation. Each component addresses a specific mechanism that maintains the pattern. The components work together — cognitive restructuring without grounding leaves the catastrophizing intact, grounding without attention retraining leaves the monitoring that re-triggers the dissociation, and attention retraining without the other two leaves the attention redirected but the core beliefs unchallenged.

Component 1: Cognitive Restructuring

Identify and challenge the catastrophizing core beliefs that structure the depersonalization experience — the deep, often early-acquired beliefs about the meaning of dissociation. The primary core beliefs are: I am not real (the self is not present), this means I am going crazy (dissociation is breakdown), and dissociation means permanent damage (the state is irreversible). Examine the evidence through Socratic questioning: what is the evidence that you are not real vs. that you are real and feel unreal (reality testing is intact, you are reading this, you have a history, you have a future — the unreality is a feeling, not a fact), what is the evidence that dissociation means psychosis vs. that depersonalization/derealization disorder is a recognized dissociative disorder with intact reality testing (the DSM-5 distinguishes it from psychotic disorders precisely because reality testing is preserved), what is the evidence that the state is permanent vs. that episodes come and go and that chronic cases still have moments of presence and that treatment produces improvement. The key technique is the dissociation-belief log: track situations that activate the unreality and the catastrophizing, identify the belief, examine the evidence for and against, and build an alternative belief that is more accurate and more functional. The distortion here is the unreality-self, the insanity-fear, and the permanence-fear; the reframe is that the unreality is a feeling rather than a fact, that the dissociation is a defense rather than a breakdown, and that the state is transient rather than permanent.

Component 2: Grounding and Affect Regulation

The dissociation in depersonalization/derealization disorder is maintained by the resistance-amplification loop: each effort to fight the unreality produces anxiety, the anxiety produces more dissociation, and the dissociation produces more resistance. Grounding and affect regulation targets this loop directly, building the capacity to anchor in the present, to regulate the affect that drives the dissociation, and to tolerate the unreality without fighting it. Three sub-skills: sensory grounding (use the five senses to anchor in the present — name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, one you can taste; the sensory input is real even when the feeling of unreality is present, and the anchoring builds the evidence that the present is real even when it does not feel real), affect regulation (identify and label the emotions that underlie the dissociation — often anxiety, often overwhelm, often grief — and regulate them through breathing, through self-compassion, through the acknowledgment that the feeling is real even when the unreality is a distortion), and tolerance training (deliberately sit with the unreality for short periods without fighting it, observe that the feeling comes and goes, that the resistance is the amplifier, and that the tolerance reduces the intensity — building the evidence that the unreality is tolerable and that the fighting is the problem). The key techniques are the grounding log (track grounding exercises, the intensity of the unreality before and after, the belief update, building an evidence base against the resistance-amplification), the sensory anchor (a specific sensory input — a cold object, a strong smell, a textured surface — that reliably anchors you in the present, used as a first response when the dissociation spikes), and the tolerance exposure (deliberately sit with the unreality for 30 seconds without fighting it, observe that the intensity does not escalate, building the evidence that the resistance is the amplifier). This directly targets the resistance-amplification and the unreality-self distortions: the unreality is a feeling that can be tolerated, the resistance is the amplifier rather than the solution, and the present is real even when it does not feel real. The distortion here is the resistance-amplification and the unreality-self; the reframe is that the unreality is tolerable, that the resistance is the amplifier, and that the grounding anchors the self in the present that is real.

Component 3: Attention Retraining

The dissociation in depersonalization/derealization disorder is maintained by attentional bias: the monitoring attention that checks whether the unreality is present, whether the self is real, whether the world is real — the checking that is meant to reassure but that instead re-triggers the dissociation by directing attention to the unreality itself. Attention retraining builds the capacity to redirect the monitoring attention toward the external world, toward engaged activity, toward the present moment — directly countering the self-monitoring that maintains the dissociation. Three sub-skills: external attention redirection (when the monitoring begins, deliberately redirect attention to an external task — a conversation, a puzzle, a walk, a craft — that requires engagement with the world rather than observation of the self, building the evidence that engagement reduces the dissociation), present-moment focus (practice mindfulness of the present moment — the breath, the body, the environment — without monitoring for unreality, building the capacity to attend to the present without checking whether it feels real), and attentional control training (practice deliberately shifting attention between internal and external foci, building the capacity to control where attention goes rather than being captured by the monitoring, and building the evidence that attention is a skill that can be trained). The key techniques are the attention log (track situations where monitoring began, the redirection, the intensity of the unreality before and after, building an evidence base against the monitoring), the engagement experiment (deliberately engage in an absorbing activity for 20 minutes and observe that the dissociation reduces, testing the monitoring-maintains-dissociation hypothesis), and the attentional-shift practice (deliberately shift attention between self and world on a schedule, building the capacity to control attention rather than be controlled by it). The key is to build the capacity to redirect the monitoring attention, which is the space where the resistance-amplification and the body-alienation collapse. The distortion here is the resistance-amplification and the body-alienation; the reframe is that attention can be redirected, that the monitoring is the maintainer rather than the reassurer, and that engagement with the world is the path to presence.

Why Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder Needs Specific CBT

General anxiety tools say "reduce your worry." General depression tools say "challenge your negative thoughts." Neither captures depersonalization/derealization disorder: the i-am-not-real unreality-self produces a specific self-as-not-present distortion that general anxiety tools conflate with ordinary dissociation, the the-world-is-not-real unreality-world produces a specific world-as-dreamlike distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the dreamlike quality of the world is structurally different from ordinary anxiety, the this-means-i-am-going-crazy insanity-fear produces a specific dissociation-as-breakdown distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the catastrophizing of dissociation as psychosis is not ordinary worry, the dissociation-means-permanent-damage permanence-fear produces a specific state-as-irreversible distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the permanence fear is structurally different from ordinary future worry, the i-must-fight-the-feeling resistance-amplification produces a specific fight-as-solution distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the resistance is the amplifier rather than the solution, and the my-body-is-not-mine body-alienation produces a specific body-as-foreign distortion that general anxiety tools cannot address because the somatic depersonalization is not ordinary body image concern. This produces specific, identifiable thought patterns that CBT for depersonalization/derealization disorder can target directly:

The "I Am Not Real" Unreality-Self

Distortion: "I am not real. I am not present. I am watching myself from outside, and the self observing is not the self acting. The gap between observer and actor means the self is not there. I look at my hands and they are not mine. I hear my voice and it is not mine. I move through my day and I am not the one moving. The unreality is a fact about my existence, not a feeling about my state. I know that other people feel real and I know that my case is different: their presence is solid and mine is hollow, their self is continuous and mine is fractured, their inside is inside and mine is observed. The best I can do is monitor the unreality, check whether it has lifted, and hope that the self returns — because if it does not, I am gone."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that I am real and feel unreal, not that I am not real, through the unreality-self lens. The unreality-self (I am not real) treats the self as not present, when the reality is that the self is present and the unreality is a feeling about the state, that reality testing is intact (I know I am real even though I do not feel real), and that the dissociation is a defense against overwhelm rather than an absence of self. The insanity-fear (this means I am going crazy) treats the dissociation as breakdown, when the reality is that depersonalization/derealization disorder is a recognized dissociative disorder with intact reality testing, that the DSM-5 distinguishes it from psychotic disorders precisely because reality testing is preserved, and that the feeling of unreality is a symptom rather than a sign of psychosis. The permanence-fear (dissociation means permanent damage) treats the state as irreversible, when the reality is that episodes come and go, that chronic cases still have moments of presence, and that treatment produces improvement. CBT helps by examining the evidence for and against the unreality-self core-belief, by tracing the dissociation to its origin (often early experiences of overwhelm, trauma, or chronic stress that the dissociation defended against), by examining the actual relationship between the feeling and the fact, and by building the capacity to hold the unreality as a feeling rather than a fact. The feeling of unreality is real; the conclusion that you are not real is the distortion."

The "I Must Fight The Feeling" Resistance-Amplification

Distortion: "I must fight the feeling. Resistance is the solution. To push back against the unreality is to defeat it. The effort to feel real is the path to feeling real. I monitor the dissociation, I check whether it has lifted, I try to force the presence back, I tense against the unreality, I distract from the feeling, I do everything I can to make it stop — because the fighting is helping, because the effort is the cure, because the resistance is the path to reality. And when the fighting does not work, I fight harder. And when the harder fighting does not work, I fight harder still. The loop is: feel unreal, fight, feel more unreal, fight more. I frame the resistance as effort, as courage, as the refusal to surrender to the unreality — but the truth is that the fighting is the amplifier and the monitoring is the maintainer."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that tolerance reduces the dissociation, not that resistance defeats it, through the resistance-amplification lens. The resistance-amplification (I must fight the feeling) treats resistance as the solution, when the reality is that the resistance is the amplifier, that the monitoring is the maintainer, and that the fighting produces the anxiety that produces the dissociation that produces the fighting. The unreality-self (I am not real) treats the self as not present, when the reality is that the self is present and the unreality is a feeling that can be tolerated. The permanence-fear (dissociation means permanent damage) treats the state as irreversible, when the reality is that the state is transient and that the tolerance reduces the intensity. CBT helps through tolerance training (deliberately sit with the unreality for short periods without fighting it, observe that the intensity does not escalate, building the evidence that the resistance is the amplifier) and through cognitive restructuring of the fight-as-solution core-belief. The desire to feel real is real; the conclusion that fighting is the path is the distortion."

The "My Body Is Not Mine" Body-Alienation

Distortion: "My body is not mine. The body is a foreign object. The limbs are tools rather than parts of me. The reflection is a stranger. The sensations belong to someone else. The body is a vehicle I am operating rather than a self I am being. I look at my hands and they do not belong to me. I feel my heartbeat and it is not my heartbeat. I move my legs and they move as if by remote control. The alienation is a fact about ownership, not a feeling about embodiment. I monitor the body to check whether it has returned to me, and the monitoring deepens the alienation, and the alienation produces more monitoring."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence that the body is self, not that the body is foreign, through the body-alienation lens. The body-alienation (my body is not mine) treats the body as a foreign object, when the reality is that the body is self, that the alienation is a feeling about embodiment rather than a fact about ownership, and that the monitoring of the body deepens the alienation rather than resolving it. The resistance-amplification (I must fight the feeling) treats the monitoring as the solution, when the reality is that the monitoring is the maintainer. The unreality-self (I am not real) treats the self as not present, when the reality is that the self is present and the body is part of the self. CBT helps through sensory grounding (use touch, movement, and body-based sensory input to anchor in the body without monitoring it, building the evidence that the body is self rather than object) and through cognitive restructuring of the body-as-foreign core-belief. The feeling of alienation is real; the conclusion that the body is not yours is the distortion."

When to See a Professional

These tools are self-help based on CBT for depersonalization/derealization disorder principles, useful as a complement to therapy with a mental health professional, especially one trained in CBT for dissociative disorders. Depersonalization/derealization disorder can involve significant distress, impairment, and co-occurring conditions (anxiety, depression, trauma, PTSD) that warrant professional treatment. These tools are a self-help starting point, not a replacement for professional treatment. If you are experiencing suicidal ideation, severe distress, or any condition affecting your safety, please contact a licensed mental health professional or a crisis line immediately. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. In the UK, call 116 123 for Samaritans. NAMI (nami.org) provides mental health resources and support. Seeking help is a sign of courage, not a failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can CBT help with depersonalization/derealization disorder?

Yes. CBT is an evidence-based treatment for depersonalization/derealization disorder, with growing evidence that it reduces dissociation catastrophizing, improves grounding and affect regulation, retrains attentional bias, and increases the capacity to tolerate the unreality without amplifying it. Depersonalization/derealization disorder produces specific cognitive distortions (unreality-self, unreality-world, insanity-fear, permanence-fear, resistance-amplification, body-alienation) that CBT targets directly through cognitive restructuring, grounding and affect regulation, and attention retraining. Depersonalization/derealization disorder is a real and treatable condition, not a psychotic break or permanent unreality.

What is the difference between depersonalization/derealization disorder and psychosis?

The critical difference is intact reality testing. Depersonalization/derealization disorder (DSM-5 300.6) is a dissociative disorder: the person experiences unreality or detachment but knows that the unreality is a feeling, not a fact — reality testing remains intact. Psychotic disorders involve impaired reality testing: delusions (fixed false beliefs) and hallucinations (perceptions without stimuli) — the person may believe the unreality is real rather than recognizing it as a subjective experience. This distinction matters because the treatments are different: CBT for depersonalization targets the catastrophizing and the resistance that amplify the dissociation, while antipsychotics and different therapy approaches are used for psychosis. About 1-2% of the population meets criteria for depersonalization/derealization disorder, and it is often underdiagnosed because people are afraid to report feeling unreal.

I feel like I am not real and the world is dreamlike. Can CBT help with the depersonalization?

Yes, and the i-am-not-real unreality-self and the the-world-is-not-real unreality-world are two of the most maintaining cognitive distortions in depersonalization/derealization disorder. The distortion is a core belief combined with the catastrophizing (insanity-fear) and the resistance (resistance-amplification) that together form a feedback loop: the unreality produces fear, the fear produces resistance, the resistance produces monitoring, the monitoring amplifies the unreality. The reality is that the unreality is a feeling, not a fact (reality testing is intact), that the dissociation is a defense against overwhelm rather than a breakdown, and that the resistance is the amplifier rather than the solution. CBT helps by examining the evidence for and against the catastrophizing core-beliefs, by tracing the dissociation to its origin, and by building the capacity to tolerate the unreality without fighting it. The feeling of unreality is real; the conclusion that you are not real or going crazy is the distortion.

Is depersonalization/derealization disorder the same as normal dissociation?

No. Brief, transient dissociation is a normal stress response — most people experience moments of "spacing out" or feeling on autopilot during high stress, fatigue, or threat. Depersonalization/derealization disorder (DSM-5 300.6) is persistent or recurrent: the experiences are frequent or continuous, cause significant distress or impairment, and are not better explained by another condition. The key differences: the disorder is persistent (the unreality is frequent or continuous rather than brief), distressing (the person is bothered by it rather than barely noticing), and impairing (it interferes with functioning rather than passing without consequence). Normal dissociation is transient, non-distressing, and non-impairing. The disorder is the persistence and the distress that make the dissociation a clinical condition warranting treatment.
