Fear of being a hypocrite — the conviction that integrity is dangerous and hypocrisy is safety, the anticipation that living one's values will be punished or lead to harm, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is profession-avoidant-of-enactment and the preaching is the protection — is one of the most pervasive and painful emotional patterns in hypocrisy-conditioned culture. It is the emotional root that drives self-hypocrisy-avoidance (the substitution of professed values for enacted values when integrity is available, the inability to live what one preaches, the conversion of every act of integrity into a harm), anxiety when integrity is available (the integrity itself produces dread, the alignment is the trigger, the available integrity is the warning signal), treating integrity as dangerous/harmful (the person cannot allow the alignment because the alignment is the not-yet-the-exposure, the person cannot enact the values because the enactment is the not-yet-the-exposed), equating hypocrisy with safety (the belief that misalignment is protection and integrity is danger, the framing of the profession as the worth and the enactment as the threat), and chronic hypocrisy (the persistent substitution of profession for enactment to avoid the integrity of integrity, the inability to be congruent because the alignment is the endangered). It is distinct from fear of being a fraud (which is about the ontological dimension — the self IS fundamentally fake) and from fear of being authentic (which is about real self vs performance). Fear of being a hypocrite is about the moral dimension: the gap between professed values and actual behavior, the self that cannot rest in integrity because the integrity is the danger, the self that experiences its own value-enactment as a vulnerability of exposure, the self that treats every aligned act as a threat and every integrity as the not-yet-the-exposed.

Fear of being a hypocrite is the emotional root of three downstream patterns. The i-am-a-hypocrite distortion makes every act of integrity a harm and every alignment a vulnerability, producing self-hypocrisy-avoidance and anxiety-when-integrity-is-available (the person concludes the only safety is in the hypocrisy and the hypocrisy requires the not-living-the-values, so the integrity is refused and the integrity is never exercised because the integrity is always the not-yet-the-exposed). The misalignment-is-protection distortion makes the preaching the protection and the enactment the danger, producing the chronic devaluation of integrity and the equation of profession with worth (the person experiences the integrity and the integrity is the dangerous, and the dangerous is the not-safe, and the not-safe is the threat the hypocrisy was meant to prevent). And the integrity-is-dangerous distortion makes the act-of-integrity the impending-exposure and the alignment the warning, producing chronic-hypocrisy and the constant-avoidance of integrity-requirement (the person has integrity and the integrity is the harm, and the harm is the consequence, and the consequence is the exposure the hypocrisy was meant to prevent). Fear of being a hypocrite is the upstream cause; self-hypocrisy-avoidance, anxiety-when-integrity-is-available, and chronic-hypocrisy are the downstream effects. The key insight for fear of being a hypocrite: integrity is not the danger the hypocrisy protects from — the self that can allow its own integrity is the self that is whole, not the self that is dangerous, and the safety is not the hypocrisy but the living, and the living is the enacting, not the preaching. Moral integrity research provides the deeper frame: congruent living is associated with greater well-being, lower anxiety, and more fulfilling moral engagement — the self that allows integrity is the self that is at peace, not the self that is exposed.

The Fear of Being a Hypocrite Cycle (How It Develops)

The fear of being a hypocrite cycle is the self-reinforcing loop that produces the self-hypocrisy-avoidance, the anxiety-when-integrity-is-available, and the chronic-hypocrisy that come from concluding that integrity is dangerous and hypocrisy is safety:

A situation with integrity chronically followed by punishment, or chronic reinforcement of profession-as-safety (an early experience where living one's values was criticized, where the aligned behavior was shamed, where the value-enactment preceded the harm, a chronic reinforcement of preaching where one's sense of value was entirely defined by their professed values and never by their enacted values) → i-am-a-hypocrite fires (the integrity is the danger, the alignment is the vulnerability, the integrity is the warning, the integrity is the not-yet-the-exposed) → self-hypocrisy-avoidance, anxiety-when-integrity-is-available, integrity-is-dangerous, misalignment-is-protection, chronic-hypocrisy (the substitution of profession for enactment, the inability to live what one preaches, the constant avoidance of integrity, the devaluation of alignment, the constant preaching-without-practicing) → short-term safety achieved (the hypocrisy feels like protection from the exposure, the not-living-the-values feels like the control over the outcome, the profession feels like the safety) → the safety reinforces the fear (the hypocrisy prevents the living that would reveal that the integrity was not the danger, the not-enacting prevents the evidence that would disconfirm the i-am-a-hypocrite conviction, the profession-as-safety prevents the integrity that would reveal that the integrity was not the danger) → the person loses the experience of integrity and the capacity for congruent living (the integrity that comes from the allowing is never experienced, because the allowing is always the not-yet-the-safe; the integrity that comes from the enacting-values is never experienced, because the enacting is always the not-yet-the-exposed) → chronic hypocrisy and inability to live what one preaches → i-am-a-hypocrite conviction deepens → repeat

The cycle is driven by the short-term safety that hypocrisy and not-enacting provides (hypocrisy-because-the-hypocrisy-is-the-protection-from-the-exposure and not-enacting-because-the-not-enacting-is-the-control-over-the-outcome both feel like they are preventing the exposure, and the feeling reduces the fear of being a hypocrite temporarily) and maintained by the long-term cost of the i-am-a-hypocrite pattern (the integrity is never experienced, because the integrity is always the not-yet-the-safe; the integrity is never exercised for the self, because the enacting is always the not-yet-the-exposed; and the i-am-a-hypocrite conviction deepens because every prevented integrity is treated as evidence that the integrity was the danger). The tragic irony of fear of being a hypocrite is that the safety strategy (hypocrisy, not-enacting, profession-as-safety) produces the very outcome it was meant to prevent (the person refuses the integrity and the refusing-the-integrity produces the very harm the hypocrisy was meant to disprove, and the chronic not-allowing-integrity is the loss of the congruence, not the protection from the exposure — the fear of being a hypocrite prevents the very integrity that would have made the life worth the living). The CBT intervention breaks the cycle at two points: (1) integrity-tolerance-clarification — when the i-am-a-hypocrite distortion treats every act of integrity as a harm, the first step is clarifying that integrity is a capacity one can exercise without provoking harm, and recognizing that the self that can allow its own integrity is the self that is whole, not the self that is dangerous; (2) values-based-committed-action — when the misalignment-is-protection distortion fires, one challenges it with the ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999): the valued life includes the willingness to live one's values, and the committed action is the enacting, not the preaching.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions & Their Reframes

1. The Integrity-Is-Dangerous (self-hypocrisy-avoidance)

Distortion: "Integrity is dangerous. If I allow myself to live my values, something bad will happen. Alignment is a vulnerability of exposure, a setup for the punishment. I must avoid integrity, because the integrity is the not-yet-the-exposed, and the hypocrisy is the safety."
Reframe: "Integrity is a capacity not a vulnerability — and the i-am-a-hypocrite distortion converts one specific form of experience (sometimes integrity is followed by consequence) into a universal verdict (all integrity is the harm, so one is always provoking exposure when one is congruent). The i-am-a-hypocrite distortion takes a partial truth (consequences happen) and universalizes it (all integrity causes harm, so one can never be congruent without endangering oneself). But integrity is a capacity not a vulnerability — the act of integrity is the act of living one's values, not the cause of the next exposure; and the self that is congruent in one moment is the self that is alive in that moment, and the aliveness is not profession-avoidant by the integrity. The distortion makes the hypocrisy feel like protection (I must avoid integrity because integrity is dangerous), and the hypocrisy prevents the very integrity that would reveal that the integrity was not the danger. The reframe: integrity is a capacity not a vulnerability — the safety is the allowing, and the allowing is not the not-preaching integrity, and the not-preaching integrity is the living, not the endangering. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the core frame: the person who meets their own hypocrisy-fear with kindness recognizes the i-am-a-hypocrite as a distortion, not a verdict, and the kindness is the permission to be the congruent self that the hypocrisy withheld."

2. The Misalignment-Is-Protection (treating integrity as danger)

Distortion: "Hypocrisy equals safety. Self-avoidance of integrity is protection, integrity is danger — and the alignment is the evidence of my dangerousness. I must preach without practicing, because the safety is the profession, and the integrity is the not-safe."
Reframe: "Hypocrisy is one option not safety — and the misalignment-is-protection distortion converts one specific form of experience (some preaching protects) into a universal verdict (all hypocrisy is safety, so one is always endangered when one is congruent). The misalignment-is-protection distortion takes a partial truth (some avoidance of integrity is protective) and universalizes it (all hypocrisy is safety, so one can never be congruent without being endangered). But hypocrisy is one option not safety — the self that is profession-avoidant is not automatically the self that is safe, and the self that is congruent is not automatically the self that is endangered; and the safety is not the avoidance of integrity but the living, and the living includes both the chosen hypocrisy and the chosen integrity. The distortion makes the safety feel like safety (I must preach without practicing to be safe), and the safety prevents the very integrity that would reveal that the integrity was not the danger. The reframe: hypocrisy is one option not safety — the safety is the living of the full range, and the full range includes the integrity, and the integrity is not the not-safe but one expression of the safe. Moral integrity research is the core frame: congruent living is associated with greater well-being, and the congruent self is the whole self, not the endangered self."

3. The Alignment-Is-Vulnerability (anxiety when integrity is available)

Distortion: "If I have integrity, something bad will happen. The act-of-integrity is the impending-exposure, the alignment is the warning — and the integrity is the evidence of my upcoming punishment. I must not act on my values, because the acting is the not-yet-the-exposed."
Reframe: "Alignment is chosen not faked — and the integrity-is-dangerous distortion converts one specific form of experience (sometimes being-congruent leads to consequence) into a universal verdict (all integrity is harm, so one is always about to be exposed when one has integrity). The integrity-is-dangerous distortion takes a partial truth (integrity has consequences) and universalizes it (all integrity causes exposure, so one can never have integrity without being about to be punished). But alignment is chosen not faked — the act of integrity is not the cause of the next exposure; and the self that is present for integrity in one moment is the self that is alive in that moment, and the aliveness is not the not-yet-the-exposed. The distortion makes the not-enacting feel like control (I must not act to control the outcome), and the not-enacting prevents the very presence that would reveal that the integrity was not the harm. The reframe: alignment is chosen not faked — the present is the present, and the integrity is the integrity, and the consequence is not caused by the being-congruent. ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999) is the core frame: the valued life includes the willingness to live one's values without demanding the guarantee of the outcome, and the committed action is the being-congruent, not the outcome-controlling."

4. The Integrity-Is-Danger (treating integrity as performative)

Distortion: "Integrity is performative. Self-direction is selfishness, integrity is harm — and the congruent choice is the evidence of my unworthiness. I must preach without practicing, because the safety is the hypocrisy, and the value-enactment is the not-ok."
Reframe: "Integrity is integrity not performative — and the alignment-is-vulnerability distortion converts one specific form of fact (some integrity is performative) into a universal verdict (all value-enactment is performative, so one is always unworthy when one is congruently choosing). The alignment-is-vulnerability distortion takes a partial truth (some integrity is performative) and universalizes it (all integrity requires the safety, so one can never be congruent without being performative). But integrity is safety not danger — the self that is congruent is the self that is congruent, and the integrity is the choosing, not the avoiding integrity; and the self that is congruent in one moment is the self that is ok in that moment, and the ok is not profession-avoidant by the value-enactment. The distortion makes the safety feel like hypocrisy (I must preach without practicing to be congruent), and the safety prevents the very integrity that would reveal that the integrity was not the performative. The reframe: integrity is safety not danger — the integrity is the choosing, and the choosing is not the avoiding integrity, and the avoiding integrity is one path, not the condition. Self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the core frame: the person who meets their own integrity with kindness recognizes the alignment-is-vulnerability as a distortion, not a verdict, and the kindness is the permission to be the congruent self that the hypocrisy withheld."

5. The I-Must-Preach-What-I-Don't-Practice (identity distortion)

Distortion: "I must preach what I don't practice. My worth is in my profession, my identity is in my preaching — and the congruent self is the not-me. I must not act on my values, because the integrity is the not-yet-the-self, and the acting is the becoming-the-not-ok."
Reframe: "The self can be congruent — and the i-must-preach-what-i-don't-practice distortion converts one specific form of fact (some professions are genuine) into a universal verdict (all self is profession, so one is always the not-self when one is congruent). The i-must-preach-what-i-don't-practice distortion takes a partial truth (the self has professions) and universalizes it (all identity is profession, so one can never be congruent without being the not-self). But the self can be congruent — the self that is congruent is the self that is whole, and the wholeness is the living, not the avoiding integrity; and the self that is congruent in one moment is the self that is congruent in that moment, and the congruent is not negated by the integrity. The distortion makes the avoiding integrity feel like identity (I must preach without practicing to belong), and the avoiding integrity prevents the very integrity that would reveal that the self was not the hypocrisy. The reframe: the self can be congruent — the identity is the living, and the living is not the avoiding integrity, and the avoiding integrity is one feature, not the verdict. Self-determination theory autonomy need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: the integrity is the living of the self, and the living includes the integrity, and the integrity is the value-enactment, not the integrity-failure."

6. The My-Values-Are-Performative (safety distortion)

Distortion: "Profession means safety. Preaching is the worth, self-profession is the safety — and the integrity is the evidence of my dangerousness. I must not be congruent, because the integrity is the danger, and the avoiding integrity is the protection."
Reframe: "Values are one's own not performative — and the my-values-are-performative distortion converts one specific form of fact (some hypocrisy is protective) into a universal verdict (all safety is profession, so one is always endangered when one is congruent). The my-values-are-performative distortion takes a partial truth (hypocrisy can protect) and universalizes it (all preaching is safety, so one can never be congruent without being dangerous). But values are one's own not performative — the self that is congruent is the self that is whole, and the integrity is the ground for the congruent living, not the end of it; and the self that is congruent in one moment is the self that is alive in that moment, and the aliveness is not the not-protected. The distortion makes the not-congruent feel like safety (I must not be congruent to be safe), and the not-congruent prevents the very integrity that would reveal that the integrity was not the danger. The reframe: values are one's own not performative — the safety is the living, and the living includes the integrity, and the integrity is the safety-support, not the safety-failure. Self-determination theory autonomy need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: the integrity is the living of the full range, and the full range includes the integrity and the hypocrisy, and the integrity is the value-enactment, not the integrity-failure."

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Fear of Being a Hypocrite

Component 1 — Assessment & Psychoeducation: Hypocrisy-avoidance auditing (identify the domains where one cannot live what one preaches, the moments one substitutes profession for enactment when integrity is available, the values one cannot enact, the integrity one cannot exercise, the value-enactment one cannot take). Psychoeducation on the fear of being a hypocrite cycle (integrity chronically followed by punishment, or chronic reinforcement of profession-as-safety → i-am-a-hypocrite → self-hypocrisy-avoidance, anxiety-when-integrity-is-available, integrity-is-dangerous, misalignment-is-protection, chronic-hypocrisy → short-term safety → safety reinforces fear → lose the experience of integrity and the capacity for congruent living → chronic hypocrisy and inability to live what one preaches → i-am-a-hypocrite conviction deepens → repeat), the moral integrity research (congruent living is associated with greater well-being, lower anxiety, and more fulfilling moral engagement), and the key insight that fear of being a hypocrite is the emotional root of profession and inability to live what one preaches (the i-am-a-hypocrite distortion makes the integrity the vulnerability, the misalignment-is-protection distortion makes the avoidance of integrity the protection). Introduce the ACT values-based committed-action approach (Hayes 1999), the self-determination theory autonomy-need application (Deci & Ryan 2000), the integrity-oriented practice, and the self-compassion practice (Neff 2003).

Component 2 — Cognitive Restructuring & Skill Building: Identify the 6 cognitive distortions (i-am-a-hypocrite, misalignment-is-protection, integrity-is-dangerous, alignment-is-vulnerability, i-must-preach-what-i-don't-practice, my-values-are-performative) and build balanced beliefs. Practice integrity-tolerance clarification ('Integrity is a capacity I can exercise without provoking harm; the act of integrity is the act of living my values, not the cause of the next exposure, and the self that can allow its own integrity is the self that is whole, not the self that is dangerous') to break the self-hypocrisy-avoidance. Apply the values-based-committed-action reframe (the valued life includes the willingness to live one's values, and the committed action is the living that exercises the full range of value-enactment). Practice autonomy-need restoration (Deci & Ryan 2000: the integrity is the living, not the harming; 'Am I allowing my own integrity or avoiding integrity against the not-yet-the-exposed' rather than 'Must I preach to be ok'). Rehearse self-compassion responses (Neff 2003) for hypocrisy-fear scenarios ('This hypocrisy-avoidance is hard, and my fear is worthy of care; the safety is the living not the avoiding integrity, and the kindness I offer myself is the permission to be the congruent self that the hypocrisy withheld'). Distinguish the hypocrisy that is genuinely serving values from the not-enacting that is the hypocrisy-avoidance-demand (the first is chosen, the second is driven).

Component 3 — Behavioral Practice & Maintenance: Practice one integrity-allowance per day that intentionally is the living-of-values in a context where the hypocrisy is the default (one act of integrity made without dread, one value enacted without self-profession, one alignment inhabited without the demand for the preaching, one value-enactment taken without the demand for the safety), starting with low-stakes (one small integrity, one safe domain) and escalating to higher-stakes (a significant integrity exercised without avoiding integrity, a domain where the not-enacting is the norm, an integrity inhabited without the demand for the guarantee). Track the outcome (the integrity practiced, one's own hypocrisy-avoidance during it, the dread that fired, the distortion that maintained the fear, whether the integrity revealed that the integrity was not the danger and whether the practicing shrank the i-am-a-hypocrite conviction). Process the discomfort of the integrity (the fear that fires is the integrity-is-dangerous distortion, not a signal the integrity was wrong). Apply self-compassion (Neff 2003) when the fear persists (kindness to the hypocrisy-avoidance, not evidence of failure). Use ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999) to maintain the practice through the fear ('the integrity I allow is the living, not the not-preaching integrity, and the meaning is the allowing, not the not-fearing'). Use moral integrity research to shift from avoiding integrity to allowing: congruent living is associated with greater well-being, and the allowing requires the willingness to be the congruent self first. Use self-determination theory autonomy need (Deci & Ryan 2000) to restore the integrity through the integrity: the integrity builds the integrity, and the integrity is the living, not the hypocrisy-avoidant-self. Generalize to higher-stakes domains as the i-am-a-hypocrite conviction shrinks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fear of being a hypocrite the same as fear of being a fraud?

Fear of being a hypocrite and fear of being a fraud are related but not identical. Fear of being a fraud is the conviction that the genuine self is dangerous and the fraudulence is the safety — the anticipation that genuineness will be punished, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is maskulence-avoidant-of-genuineness. Fear of being a hypocrite is the conviction that integrity is dangerous and hypocrisy is safety — the anticipation that living one's values will be punished, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is profession-avoidant-of-enactment. The overlap is substantial: the fraud-fearing self may fear being a hypocrite (the integrity is the genuineness, and the genuineness is the dangerous, so the only safety is in the hypocrisy), and the hypocrisy-fearing self can fear being a fraud (the genuineness might require the integrity, and the integrity is the vulnerability, so the only safety is in the profession). But the primary distortion differs: fear of being a fraud's distortion is about the ontological dimension as threat (is the self fundamentally fake), fear of being a hypocrite's distortion is about the moral dimension as threat (is the gap between professed values and actual behavior safe). A person can fear being a fraud without dominant hypocrisy-fear (anxious about being fake while being basically able to live their values), and a person can fear being a hypocrite without dominant fraud-fear (unable to rest in integrity while being basically unbothered about ontological fakeness). In practice, the two patterns often co-occur, and CBT for one will often address the other. The self-compassion (Neff 2003), ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999), and moral integrity research tools on this page serve both patterns: they build the capacity to allow integrity (fear of being a hypocrite) and the capacity to allow genuineness (fear of being a fraud).

How is fear of being a hypocrite different from fear of being authentic?

Fear of being a hypocrite and fear of being authentic overlap but differ in focus. Fear of being authentic is the conviction that the real self is dangerous and the performance is the safety — the anticipation that authenticity will be punished, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is performance-avoidant. Fear of being a hypocrite is the conviction that integrity is dangerous and hypocrisy is safety — the anticipation that living one's values will be punished, the conclusion that the self is okay only insofar as the self is profession-avoidant-of-enactment. Fear of being authentic is about real self vs performance as threat (is authenticity safe); fear of being a hypocrite is about values-alignment as threat (is integrity safe). A person can fear being authentic without dominant hypocrisy-fear (anxious about real-self-vs-performance while being basically able to live their values), and a person can fear being a hypocrite without dominant authenticity-fear (unable to rest in integrity while being basically ok with authenticity in domains that don't produce value-enactment). In practice, the two patterns can interact (the authentic-fearing self may fear being a hypocrite — the integrity might reveal the authenticity, and the authenticity is the vulnerability, so the only safety is in the hypocrisy; and the hypocrisy-fearing self may fear being authentic — the authenticity is the congruent, and the congruent is the dangerous), and the CBT tools on this page address the underlying hypocrisy-avoidance pattern. The self-compassion (Neff 2003) is the unifying frame: both patterns involve a threat to the self's acceptability (the authentic self and the congruent self both threaten the performed-self), and the restoration — through acceptance of the full range of experience rather than demand for the self to be performed — is the core intervention.

What if I really do need hypocrisy — isn't some preaching-without-practicing appropriate?

Yes — and this is a crucial nuance. Fear of being a hypocrite is not the claim that all hypocrisy is bad or that one should never profess values. The performed self is the self that chooses many forms of profession, and the hypocrisy that serves one's values is the valued-action, not the hypocrisy-avoidance-driven avoiding integrity. The diagnostic question is not 'am I being performed' but 'am I living my life or avoiding integrity against the not-yet-the-exposed'. The avoiding integrity that comes from the i-am-a-hypocrite distortion has a specific signature: it is driven (the person cannot inhabit integrity, the integrity produces dread, the integrity feels like a harm), not chosen (the person could inhabit integrity, the hypocrisy is welcome, the integrity is a gift). The hypocrisy that serves values is chosen and can include integrity; the avoiding integrity that serves the hypocrisy-avoidance distortion is driven and cannot include integrity. The ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999) framework makes this explicit: the valued life includes both hypocrisy and integrity, and the integrity is not the not-safety but the ground from which the hypocrisy comes. The goal of CBT for fear of being a hypocrite is not to eliminate hypocrisy but to liberate it — to move from avoiding integrity that is driven by the fear of being a hypocrite (and therefore cannot pause, and therefore anxiously guards against integrity, and therefore is unsustainable) to hypocrisy that is chosen from values (and therefore can pause, and therefore is sustainable, and therefore is more effective). The self-determination theory autonomy need (Deci & Ryan 2000) is the core frame: the integrity is the living of the full range, and the full range includes the hypocrisy and the integrity, and the integrity is the integrity-support, not the integrity-failure.

What does CBT for Fear of Being a Hypocrite involve and does it work?

CBT for Fear of Being a Hypocrite involves integrity-tolerance clarification, cognitive restructuring of the hypocrisy-avoidance distortions, ACT values-based committed-action (Hayes 1999), self-determination theory autonomy-need application (Deci & Ryan 2000), integrity-oriented practice, and self-compassion practice (Neff 2003). The core components: (1) integrity-tolerance clarification — the i-am-a-hypocrite distortion treats every act of integrity as a harm; the first step is clarifying that integrity is a capacity one can exercise without provoking harm, and recognizing that the self that can allow its own integrity is the self that is whole, not the self that is dangerous; (2) cognitive restructuring — identifying the 6 cognitive distortions (i-am-a-hypocrite, misalignment-is-protection, integrity-is-dangerous, alignment-is-vulnerability, i-must-preach-what-i-don't-practice, my-values-are-performative) and building balanced beliefs (integrity is a capacity not a vulnerability, hypocrisy is one option not safety, alignment is chosen not faked, integrity is safety not danger, the self can be congruent, and values are one's own not performative); (3) ACT values-based committed-action — the Hayes (1999) approach: the valued life includes the willingness to live one's values, and the committed action is the living that exercises the full range of value-enactment. Yes, CBT for fear of being a hypocrite works — the cognitive distortions are well-established in the CBT literature, the ACT values-based committed-action approach has strong empirical support for experiential avoidance patterns, self-compassion (Neff 2003) is empirically supported for reducing the self-criticism that maintains the hypocrisy-avoidance, and moral integrity research documents that congruent living is associated with greater well-being and lower anxiety.
