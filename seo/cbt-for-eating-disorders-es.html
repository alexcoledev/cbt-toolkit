¿Qué son los trastornos alimentarios?

An eating disorder (ED) is a mental disorder defined by abnormal eating behaviors that adversely affect a person's physical or mental health. These behaviors may include eating too much food or too little food, as well as body image issues. Types of eating disorders include anorexia, where the person has an intense fear of gaining weight, thus restricts food and/or over exercises to manage this fear; bulimia, where individuals eat a large quantity (binging) then try to rid themselves of the food (purging), in an attempt to not gain any weight; binge eating disorder, where the person suffering keeps eating large amounts in a short period of time typically while not being hungry, often leading to weight gain; pica, where the patient eats non-food items; rumination syndrome, where the patient regurgitates undigested or minimally digested food; avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), where people have a reduced or selective food intake due to some psychological reasons; and a group of other specified feeding or eating disorders. Anxiety disorders, depression and substance abuse are common among people with eating disorders. These disorders do not include obesity. People often experience comorbidity between an eating disorder and OCD.

Por qué el estrés del trastorno alimentario necesita una TCC específica del trastorno

Las herramientas genéricas para el estrés dicen "practica el autocuidado. -Está bien, pero falta la capa cognitiva. El estrés del trastorno alimentario no es ansiedad aleatoria: tiene patrones de pensamiento específicos e identificables arraigados en la relación cuerpo-alimento a los que la TCC puede dirigirse directamente:

The "I Can't Eat That" Food Fear Distortion

Distortion: "I cannot eat that. That food is bad. If I eat it, I will lose control and not be able to stop. It will ruin everything I have worked for. One bite and the whole day is gone. I have to be strong, I have to resist, I cannot give in. Clean food only. If it is not clean, it is dangerous. I know it is irrational but the fear is real: my heart races, my hands shake, I cannot think about anything else. Eating it means I have failed, means I am weak, means I am out of control."
Reframe: "I am challenging the food fear with evidence, not willpower. The cognitive distortions are catastrophizing (one bite ruins everything), magical thinking (this food is bad, that food is clean), and all-or-nothing thinking (a food is either safe or dangerous). Food does not have a moral character; there is no such thing as a good or bad food, only foods with different nutritional profiles that my body can handle in a range of amounts. The fear is real, but it is a phobic response, not evidence of danger, and phobias respond to graded exposure: I can reintroduce feared foods in a controlled, supported way, tolerate the anxiety that arises without compensatory behaviors, and let the anxiety extinguish as the predicted catastrophe does not occur. Losing control is not a property of the food; it is a consequence of the restriction that has built up deprivation, which means the path to control is not more restriction but adequate consistent nutrition. I will work with a treatment team on exposure, use the anxiety tools to tolerate the urge, and reframe food as neutral rather than moral. My fear is real and it is treatable."

The "My Worth Is My Weight" Body & Self-Worth Distortion

Distortion: "My worth is my weight. If the number goes up, I am worse. If it goes down, I am better. I check the scale every morning and it determines my mood for the whole day. I check the mirror, I pinch my stomach, I measure my thighs, I compare myself to every body I see. I cannot stop. The checking is the only thing that makes the anxiety bearable, but it never lasts. I am terrified of gaining weight because that would mean I am a failure, I am undisciplined, I am less. My body is the most important thing about me, and if it is not right, nothing else matters."
Reframe: "I am separating my worth from my body size. The cognitive distortions are personalization (my worth is my weight) and all-or-nothing thinking (if I am not thin, I am worthless). Body checking is a compulsion that temporarily reduces anxiety but maintains it long-term by reinforcing the belief that body size is the most important thing about me. I can target the checking with response prevention: delaying the weigh-in, reducing the frequency, and ultimately stopping, while tolerating the anxiety that arises without the check. The evidence that weight determines worth does not hold up: I can list the qualities that make me valuable, my character, my values, my relationships, my curiosity, my integrity, my capacity to love and to think, none of which change with the number on the scale. I will build alternative measures of self-worth rooted in who I am rather than what I weigh, practice body-neutral attention rather than body-evaluative attention, and let my worth be a fact rather than a number. My body is part of my life, not the measure of my value."

The "I've Ruined It" Binge-Shame Distortion

Distortion: "I have ruined it. I ate the thing I said I would not eat, and now I have lost control, and now the whole day is gone, and now I might as well keep going because it is already ruined. I am so ashamed. I am weak, I have no willpower, I am a failure. I will start again tomorrow, I will be stricter, I will not let this happen again. I have to exercise to make up for it, I have to restrict tomorrow to balance it out. I cannot tell anyone. They would be disgusted. I am disgusted."
Reframe: "I am reframing the slip as a data point, not a character verdict. The cognitive distortions are all-or-nothing thinking (one slip means the day is ruined, recovery is over), should-statements (I should not have eaten that, I should have more control), and personalization (bingeing means I am weak). The binge-restrict cycle is not a willpower failure; it is the predictable consequence of restriction creating deprivation, which drives bingeing, which triggers shame, which drives more restriction, which drives more bingeing. The path out is not more control but less restriction: adequate consistent nutrition is the first intervention, because a fed body does not binge the way a starved one does. A slip is information, not identity: it tells me something about my restriction, my stress, my triggers, my deprivation, and I can use that information to adjust my recovery plan. I will not compensate with exercise or restriction, which would restart the cycle, I will tell someone I trust because shame grows in secrecy and shrinks in shared light, and I will treat the next meal as the next opportunity, not the continuation of a ruined day. Recovery is not a straight line; it is a process, and a slip is part of the process, not the end of it."

These reframes are not toxic positivity or minimizing the real difficulty of eating disorders. They are evidence-based cognitive restructuring — the core CBT technique, adapted for the body-food relationship. The tools on this page walk you through generating your own reframes using your actual evidence, not generic affirmations.

When to See a Professional

Eating disorders are serious, potentially life-threatening conditions with the highest mortality rate of any mental illness. These tools are self-help for mild to moderate eating-disorder-related distress, not a substitute for a licensed therapist (ideally one with eating disorder specialization), a registered dietitian, a medical doctor, and a support network. If you are experiencing severe restriction, frequent purging, medical instability, thoughts of self-harm, or any condition affecting your safety, please contact a licensed mental health professional, an eating disorder specialist, or a crisis line. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available 24/7. The National Eating Disorders Association (NEDA) Helpline is 1-800-931-2237. The National Association of Anorexia Nervosa and Associated Disorders (ANAD) is 1-630-300-0111. Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741. In the UK, Beat is 0808 801 0677. Seeking help is a sign of strength, not a weakness.

Preguntas frecuentes

Can CBT help with the mental health impact of eating disorders?

Yes. Eating disorders carry the highest mortality rate of any mental illness, and the psychological load extends far beyond food: the need for control through restriction, the shame of the binge-restrict cycle, the weight-based self-worth that ties identity to a number, the fear of foods that have been labeled bad or dangerous, the exercise compulsion that frames movement as punishment or earned food, the social isolation from avoiding meals with others, the comparison to bodies in media, the recovery fear of who you are without the disorder, the relapse guilt of all-or-nothing recovery, the family conflict around meals, and the anxiety about medical complications. CBT targets the specific cognitive distortions that maintain this distress: all-or-nothing thinking (one slip means recovery is ruined), catastrophizing (eating this food means I have lost control and it will never stop), personalization (my worth is my weight), should-statements (I should not have eaten that, I should exercise to make up for it), and emotional reasoning (I feel guilty so I must have done something wrong). CBT is the leading evidence-based treatment for eating disorders and helps by examining the evidence for and against food and body beliefs, separating self-worth from body size, building tolerance of food fear and body distress, and constructing a recovery identity that is not defined by the disorder.

How does CBT address the binge-restrict cycle and the shame that maintains it?

The binge-restrict cycle is maintained by a cognitive trap: restriction creates physical and psychological deprivation, which drives bingeing, which triggers shame and the resolution to restrict harder, which creates more deprivation, which drives the next binge. The cognitive distortions are all-or-nothing thinking (I have ruined everything, the day is lost, I might as well keep going), should-statements (I should not have eaten that, I should have more control), and personalization (bingeing means I am weak, I have no willpower). CBT breaks the cycle by challenging the restriction that drives it (adequate consistent nutrition is the first intervention, not more control), reframing a slip as a data point rather than a character verdict, building distress tolerance for the urge without acting on it, and separating the behavior from identity (bingeing is something that happened, not something you are). The shame and thought record tools on this page walk through this process.

How does CBT help with body checking and weight-based self-worth?

Body checking (repeated weighing, measuring, mirror checking, pinching, comparing) is a compulsion that temporarily reduces anxiety but long-term maintains it by reinforcing the belief that body size is the most important thing about you. Weight-based self-worth is the cognitive distortion that your value as a person is determined by your weight or shape: personalization (my worth is my weight) and all-or-nothing thinking (if I am not thin, I am worthless). CBT helps by targeting the checking behavior with response prevention (delaying, reducing, and ultimately stopping the checks), examining the evidence that weight determines worth (listing the qualities that make you valuable that have nothing to do with body size), building alternative measures of self-worth rooted in character, values, relationships, and contributions, and practicing body-neutral rather than body-evaluative attention. The self-esteem and body image tools on this page address this directly.

Can CBT help with the fear of foods and food anxiety?

Yes. Food anxiety in eating disorders is not picky eating; it is a genuine fear response to specific foods or food groups that have been labeled dangerous, bad, or contaminating. The cognitive distortions are catastrophizing (eating this food will make me lose control, it will ruin everything), magical thinking (this food is bad, this food is clean, this food is safe), and all-or-nothing thinking (a food is either safe or dangerous, there is no middle ground). CBT uses graded exposure (the same technique used for phobias) to systematically reintroduce feared foods in a controlled way, challenges the good/bad food binary with evidence about nutrition and the body's capacity to handle a range of foods, builds tolerance of the anxiety that arises during exposure without compensatory behaviors, and reframes food as neutral rather than moral. The anxiety and thought record tools on this page support this work.

How does CBT help with recovery identity and the fear of who you are without the disorder?

After years of an eating disorder, the disorder can become the organizing structure of identity: the rules, the rituals, the numbers, the control, the specialness of being the one who does not eat. Recovery means losing that structure, and the cognitive distortion is all-or-nothing thinking (without the disorder, I am nothing, I have no identity) and catastrophizing (recovery means giving up the thing that makes me me). CBT helps by examining the evidence of who you are outside the disorder (values, interests, relationships, capacities that the disorder suppressed), building a recovery identity around what you are moving toward rather than only what you are leaving, processing the grief of losing the disorder without romanticizing it, and recognizing that the disorder was a coping mechanism, not a personality. The self-esteem and values tools on this page walk through this process.
