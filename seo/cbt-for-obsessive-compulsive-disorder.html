What is Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder?

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) is an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder characterized by obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming or cause clinically significant distress or impairment. Obsessions are recurrent, persistent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges, or images. The content of obsessions varies widely: contamination fears (the person fears germs, dirt, or illness), doubt (the person doubts whether they locked the door, turned off the stove, or harmed someone), aggressive intrusive thoughts (the person has unwanted thoughts of harming themselves or others), sexual intrusive thoughts (the person has unwanted sexual thoughts or images), religious/blasphemous intrusive thoughts (the person has unwanted thoughts that violate their religious beliefs), symmetry/ordering needs (the person needs things arranged just right), and hoarding urges (the person feels driven to save items). Compulsions are repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules. Common compulsions include washing/cleaning, checking, counting, ordering/arranging, repeating routines, praying, and mental reviewing. The compulsion is performed to reduce the anxiety triggered by the obsession or to prevent a feared outcome, but the compulsion is not connected in a realistic way to the feared outcome and is clearly excessive. The OCD cycle is: obsession triggers anxiety, the person performs the compulsion to reduce the anxiety, the compulsion reduces the anxiety in the short term (negative reinforcement), which reinforces the belief that the ritual is necessary, which strengthens the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle. The cycle is self-maintaining: the compulsion prevents the person from learning that the obsession is not dangerous and the anxiety would habituate on its own. The person usually recognizes that the obsessions and compulsions are excessive or. DSM-5 specifiers for insight include: good or fair insight (recognizes symptoms as unreasonable), poor insight (thinks symptoms may be reasonable), and absent insight/delusional beliefs (convinced symptoms are reasonable). The ~1-2% lifetime prevalence is equally common in men and women. Onset is typically gradual, beginning in adolescence or early adulthood (mean age of onset ~20 years). Males often have earlier onset. Comorbidity is high with anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, and other Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. The gold-standard treatment is CBT with Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), often combined with serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SRIs) for moderate-to-severe cases.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

CBT for Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) combines three evidence-based components. The gold-standard approach is Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), which is supported by the strongest evidence base of any treatment for OCD. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the thought-action fusion and ritual-causation fusion that drive the compulsion, the mental-control fusion and incompleteness fusion that maintain the obsession, the inflated responsibility and identity fusion that block engagement with ERP. Cognitive restructuring addresses the 6 distortions while ERP breaks the negative reinforcement cycle that maintains the obsession-anxiety-compulsion loop.

1. Psychoeducation, Intrusive-Thought Normalization, Symptom Identification & Obsession/Compulsion Mapping

The foundation of CBT for OCD is normalizing intrusive thoughts, identifying the cognitive distortions that maintain the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle, and conducting a thorough assessment that maps the person's specific obsessions, compulsions, triggers, and the functional relationship between them. Psychoeducation: learn what OCD is (a neurobiological condition characterized by obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming or cause clinically significant distress or impairment), what obsessions are (recurrent, persistent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges, or images — NOT character revelations), what compulsions are (repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce obsession-triggered anxiety — NOT useful actions), the OCD cycle (obsession triggers anxiety, compulsion reduces anxiety via negative reinforcement, reinforcement strengthens the belief the ritual is necessary, cycle strengthens), the negative reinforcement mechanism (the compulsion reduces anxiety in the short term but prevents habituation and strengthens the cycle in the long term), the ERP rationale (exposure without the ritual teaches the brain that the obsession is not dangerous and the anxiety habituates on its own), and the good prognosis with ERP (~70% response rate). Intrusive-thought normalization is a cornerstone: approximately 80-99% of people experience unwanted intrusive thoughts similar in content to OCD obsessions. The difference is not the content but the interpretation: people without OCD dismiss intrusive thoughts as mental noise; people with OCD interpret them as meaningful via thought-action fusion.F This psychoeducation alone can significantly reduce distress. Symptom identification: map the specific obsessions (what are the intrusive thoughts, urges, images?), the specific compulsions (what behaviors or mental acts are performed in response?), the triggers (what situations, thoughts, or feelings provoke the obsessions?), the functional relationship (which compulsion is performed for which obsession?), the time occupied (how much time per day?), the distress level, the impairment areas, and the avoidance behaviors. Obsession/compulsion mapping creates a hierarchy for ERP: rank the obsessions and their triggers by anxiety level (Subjective Units of Distress, SUDS 0-100), identify the compulsion performed for each, and plan the exposure exercises from lowest to highest anxiety.

2. Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) + Cognitive Restructuring of Thought-Action-Fusion, Ritual-Causation-Fusion, Mental7Mental-Control-Fusion, Incompleteness-Fusion, Inflated-Responsibility & Identity-Fusion Distortions

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the gold-standard CBT technique for OCD and the core of treatment. ERP has two components performed simultaneously: (1) Exposure — the person is gradually and repeatedly exposed to the situations, thoughts, images, and triggers that provoke the obsessions and anxiety. The exposure is done hierarchically, starting with less anxiety-provoking triggers (low SUDS) and working up to more difficult ones (high SUDS). Exposure can be in vivo (real situations) or imaginal (imagining the feared scenario). (2) Response Prevention — the person refrains from performing the compulsion (the ritual, the checking, the washing, the mental act) that they feel driven to perform. Through repeated exposure without the ritual, the anxiety habituates (decreases through the natural process of habituation), and the person learns through experience that the feared outcome does not occur and the anxiety is tolerable without the ritual. ERP breaks the negative reinforcement cycle: the compulsion is removed, the brain learns the obsession is not dangerous and the anxiety habituates on its own, and the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle weakens. ERP is typically conducted over 12-20 sessions with daily exposure homework. Cognitive restructuring targets the six distortions that maintain OCD: keep an OCD belief log (identify the obsession, the anxiety, the compulsion, the distortion, the evidence, the reframe), challenge thought-action fusion (the thought is not the action, intrusive thoughts are universal, the content does not reveal character), challenge ritual-causation fusion (the ritual and the outcome are independent, the ritual does not prevent harm, magical thinking is not causation), challenge mental-control fusion (thought-suppression amplifies thoughts via ironic process theory, intrusive thoughts are normal, acceptance reduces frequency), challenge incompleteness fusion (the not-just-right feeling is the symptom not a signal, perfection is not safety, tolerance of imperfection is the skill), challenge inflated responsibility (responsibility is shared and finite, the person is not the sole preventer of harm, responsibility is proportional to actual control), and challenge identity fusion (OCD is a treatable condition not a character trait, the thoughts are symptoms not self, the rituals are driven by the disorder not personality). The cognitive restructuring supports ERP by reducing the beliefs that make exposure feel dangerous and response prevention feel impossible.

3. Relapse Prevention, Lifestyle Consolidation, Comorbid Treatment & Insight Monitoring

Relapse prevention plan: a written plan that includes the early warning signs of symptom exacerbation (increased ritual time, new obsessions, increased avoidance, increased anxiety, decreased ERP compliance), the action steps for each warning sign (return to ERP practice, increase session frequency, use specific ERP exercises for the exacerbation), the high-risk situations (stress, life transitions, illness, sleep deprivation, discontinuation of medication) and the coping plan for each, and the support system (who to call, when to return to treatment, how to use ERP skills independently). Lifestyle consolidation: integrate ERP principles into daily life — practice tolerating uncertainty in small daily exposures (not checking twice, sitting with a not-just-right feeling, allowing an intrusive thought to pass without suppression), maintain a regular ERP practice schedule, and build a life oriented around values rather than around OCD avoidance. Comorbid treatment: OCD has high comorbidity with anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, and other Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders — assess for and treat comorbid conditions, as they can maintain OCD symptoms and interfere with ERP compliance. Insight monitoring: OCD insight can fluctuate (good/fair insight, poor insight, absent insight/delusional beliefs) — monitor insight level, as poor or absent insight may require adaptation of ERP (more cognitive work, possible medication consultation) and can affect treatment adherence. Medication consultation: for moderate-to-severe OCD, serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SRIs) combined with ERP produce better outcomes than either alone — medication consultation is an adjunct to ERP, not a replacement.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

Each distortion maintains the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle by blocking engagement with the Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) that would break the cycle. Each has a reframe backed by evidence from the ERP and cognitive therapy literature.

1. My-Thoughts-Mean-Something-Terrible-About-Me (thought-action fusion / moral thought-action fusion): "If I think about harming my child, it means I want to do it. Having the thought is as bad as doing it. I am a terrible person for thinking this. The thought reveals my true character." The person fuses the intrusive thought with moral character, treating the thought as equivalent to the action or as a revelation of who they really are.
Reframe: "Intrusive thoughts are universal mental noise, not character revelations. The My-Thoughts-Mean-Something-Terrible-About-Me distortion is a thought-action fusion: the person has an intrusive thought (e.g., a thought of harming their child) and fuses the thought with the action (thinking it is as bad as doing it) or with character (the thought means they want to do it or are a bad person). But intrusive thoughts are a universal human experience — approximately 80-99% of people experience unwanted intrusive thoughts with content similar to OCD obsessions (aggressive thoughts, sexual thoughts, blasphemous thoughts). The difference between people with OCD and people without OCD is not the content of the thoughts but the interpretation: people without OCD dismiss intrusive thoughts as mental noise; people with OCD interpret them as meaningful via thought-action fusion. The thought-action fusion is maintained by the egodystonic nature of OCD obsessions: the person is distressed by the thoughts precisely because the thoughts violate their values (the person who has thoughts of harming their child is distressed because they love their child and would never harm them — the distress is evidence against the distortion, not for it). The distortion is harmful because it transforms normal mental noise into a source of shame, anxiety, and concealment, which drives the compulsion (mental rituals to suppress the thought, avoidance of the child, reassurance-seeking) and blocks treatment (the person is too ashamed to disclose the thoughts). The antidote is recognizing that intrusive thoughts are universal mental noise, not character revelations — the content of the thought does not determine character, the distress about the thought is evidence that the thought violates the person's values, and the thought-action fusion is a cognitive distortion that maintains OCD. The key question: if the thought means I want to do it, why am I distressed by the thought, why do the thoughts violate my values, and why do 80-99% of people have similar thoughts without acting on them?"
2. I-Must-Perform-The-Ritual-Or-Something-Bad-Will-Happen (ritual-causation fusion / magical thinking): "If I don't check the lock 7 times, someone will break in. The ritual prevents the harm. Not doing the ritual means danger. If I don't wash exactly right, I'll get sick." The person fuses the ritual with the outcome, treating the ritual as causally connected to preventing the feared event.
Reframe: "The ritual and the outcome are independent events connected by magical thinking, not causation. The I-Must-Perform-The-Ritual-Or-Something-Bad-Will-Happen distortion is a ritual-causation fusion: the person has learned that performing the ritual reduces anxiety (because it does, in the short term, via negative reinforcement), and has generalized this to the belief that the ritual prevents the feared outcome. But the ritual and the outcome are independent events — checking the lock 7 times does not make the lock more secure than checking it once, washing exactly right does not prevent illness more than washing adequately, and the ritual does not control events that are not causally connected to it. The belief that the ritual prevents harm is magical thinking — the treatment of two unrelated events as causally connected because they are paired in the person's experience. The distortion is harmful because it drives the compulsion — if the ritual prevents harm, the person must perform the ritual, and the compulsion strengthens the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle. The distortion also prevents ERP: if the ritual is necessary to prevent harm, response prevention feels dangerous, and the person cannot engage with the exposure. The antidote is recognizing that the ritual and the outcome are independent — the ritual does not prevent the harm, the anxiety reduction from the ritual is negative reinforcement (not harm prevention), and ERP teaches the brain that the obsession is not dangerous and the ritual is not necessary. The key question: if the ritual prevents harm, why does the harm not occur when the ritual is prevented during ERP, why does the anxiety habituate without the ritual, and why is the ritual connected to the outcome only in my experience and not in reality?"
3. My-Intrusive-Thoughts-Are-Dangerous (mental-control fusion / thought-suppression necessity): "I must not think these thoughts. If I let them in, they will take over. Thinking them makes them more likely to happen. I need to suppress them." The person treats normal intrusive thoughts as dangerous, believing that thinking the thoughts amplifies them or makes the feared outcome more likely.
Reframe: "Thought-suppression amplifies the thoughts (ironic process theory) and intrusive thoughts are universal. The My-Intrusive-Thoughts-Are-Dangerous distortion is a mental-control fusion: the person treats intrusive thoughts as dangerous (believing that thinking the thoughts makes them more likely to happen or lets them take over) and drives thought-suppression as a mental ritual. But thought-suppression amplifies the thoughts — Daniel Wegner's ironic process theory demonstrates that attempting to suppress a thought makes the thought more frequent and more accessible (the white bear problem). The thoughts that the person tries to suppress become more intrusive, not less. The distortion is harmful because it drives mental rituals (suppression, neutralization, distraction) that maintain the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle, and the suppression amplifies the thoughts that the person is trying to eliminate. The distortion also blocks ERP: if the thoughts are dangerous, exposure to the thoughts (imaginal exposure) feels impossible, and the person cannot engage with the treatment. The antidote is recognizing that intrusive thoughts are universal mental noise, thought-suppression amplifies them, and acceptance (allowing the thoughts to pass without suppression or engagement) reduces their frequency and distress. The key question: if thinking the thoughts makes them more likely to happen, why does thought-suppression make the thoughts more frequent, why do 80-99% of people have similar thoughts without the outcomes, and why does accepting the thoughts reduce their frequency?"
4. Everything-Must-Be-Just-Right (incompleteness fusion / not-just-right intolerance): "I can't function until things feel right. The arrangement must be perfect. If it's not exactly right, something is wrong. I need to fix it until it feels complete." The person fuses the feeling of rightness with safety and completion, treating the not-just-right feeling as a signal that something is wrong.
Reframe: "The not-just-right feeling is the OCD symptom, not a signal of actual danger or incompleteness. The Everything-Must-Be-Just-Right distortion is an incompleteness fusion: the person experiences a distressing feeling of incompleteness or not-just-rightness when things are not arranged, ordered, or done in a specific way, and fuses this feeling with safety and completion (treating the feeling as a signal that something is wrong and the ritual as necessary to fix it). But the not-just-right feeling is the OCD symptom — it is a neurobiological signal produced by the disorder, not a signal of actual danger, incompleteness, or wrongness. The feeling is distressing but the distress is the symptom, not a valid signal. The distortion is harmful because it drives ordering, arranging, repeating, and redoing compulsions that maintain the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle, and the pursuit of the just-right feeling is endless (the feeling is never satisfied for long, and the threshold for just-right can escalate). The distortion also blocks ERP: if things must be just right, sitting with the not-just-right feeling (ERP) feels impossible, and the person cannot engage with response prevention. The antidote is recognizing that the not-just-right feeling is the OCD symptom, not a signal — the feeling is distressing but not dangerous, tolerance of the feeling is the skill (ERP teaches the brain that the not-just-right feeling is tolerable and habituates), and the pursuit of just-right is the compulsion, not the solution. The key question: if things must be just right, why does the just-right feeling never last, why does the threshold for just-right escalate, and why does tolerating the not-just-right feeling (ERP) reduce the distress?"
5. I-Am-Responsible-For-Preventing-All-Harm (inflated responsibility / responsibility fusion): "I am the one who must prevent harm. If I don't check, and something happens, it's my fault. I am responsible for all outcomes. If I don't wash, I will cause illness." The person inflates personal responsibility for harm prevention, treating themselves as the sole preventer of harm and as causally responsible for any harm that occurs.
Reframe: "Responsibility is shared, finite, and proportional to actual control. The I-Am-Responsible-For-Preventing-All-Harm distortion is an inflated responsibility: the person overestimates their personal responsibility for preventing harm (treating themselves as the sole preventer) and overestimates the severity and probability of the harm. But responsibility is shared (other people also act to prevent harm, the world has safeguards, harm is not solely the person's responsibility), finite (the person cannot prevent all harm, and the attempt to do so is the OCD symptom), and proportional to actual control (the person's responsibility is proportional to their actual ability to prevent the harm, not to the severity of the harm). The distortion is harmful because it drives checking, washing, and other compulsions (if I am responsible for preventing all harm, I must check, wash, and ritualize to fulfill my responsibility), and the inflated responsibility makes ERP feel negligent (if I don't check and something happens, it's my fault — response prevention feels like abandoning responsibility). The distortion also produces guilt and shame (the person blames themselves for any harm that occurs, even harm unrelated to their actions). The antidote is recognizing that responsibility is shared, finite, and proportional to actual control — the person is not the sole preventer of harm, the attempt to prevent all harm is the OCD symptom, and ERP teaches the brain that the responsibility is tolerable and the harm is not solely the person's to prevent. The key question: if I am responsible for preventing all harm, why is harm not prevented by my rituals (the lock can be picked regardless of checking, illness can occur regardless of washing), why do other people share the responsibility, and why does tolerating uncertainty (ERP) not increase harm?"
6. My-OCD-Is-Who-I-Am (identity fusion / OCD-as-identity): "I am my OCD. These thoughts are me. My rituals are my personality. I am a controlling person. I am a checking person. I am a clean person." The person fuses the disorder with identity, treating the OCD symptoms as character traits rather than as symptoms of a treatable condition.
Reframe: "OCD is a treatable medical condition, not a character trait, and the thoughts are symptoms produced by the disorder. The My-OCD-Is-Who-I-Am distortion is an identity fusion: the person fuses the OCD symptoms (the obsessions, the compulsions, the avoidance) with their identity (treating the symptoms as character traits rather than as symptoms of a disorder). But OCD is a neurobiological condition — the obsessions are produced by the disorder (they are intrusive, unwanted, and egodystonic, meaning they violate the person's values), and the compulsions are driven by the disorder (the person feels driven to perform them, not choosing to perform them). The symptoms are not character traits — they are symptoms that remit with treatment (ERP produces significant symptom reduction in ~70% of patients, which would not be possible if the symptoms were character traits). The distortion is harmful because it blocks treatment engagement — if the OCD is who I am, treatment is an attack on the self, and the person resists ERP. The distortion also produces shame (the person treats the symptoms as evidence of being a bad or defective person) and conceals the symptoms (the person does not disclose the symptoms because they believe the symptoms are who they are). The antidote is recognizing that OCD is a treatable condition, not a character trait — the thoughts are symptoms produced by the disorder (egodystonic, violating the person's values), the rituals are driven by the disorder (the person feels driven, not choosing), and the symptoms remit with treatment (ERP). The key question: if the OCD is who I am, why are the thoughts unwanted and distressing (egodystonic), why do the symptoms remit with ERP (character traits do not remit with treatment), and why do the thoughts violate my values (the distress is evidence the thoughts are not me)?"

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder requires assessment and treatment by a clinician trained in ERP. Seek help if: you have obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming (more than 1 hour per day) or causing distress or impairment in your life; you are performing rituals (checking, washing, counting, ordering, mental acts) that you recognize as excessive but feel driven to perform; you are experiencing intrusive thoughts that are distressing and that you attempt to suppress or neutralize; you are avoiding situations, people, or activities because they trigger obsessions; you are experiencing thought-action fusion (believing your intrusive thoughts mean something about your character or make harm more likely); you are experiencing magical thinking (believing your rituals prevent harm); you are experiencing inflated responsibility (believing you are solely responsible for preventing harm); your OCD symptoms are worsening over time; you are having difficulty functioning at work, in relationships, or in self-care due to OCD symptoms; you want to work with an ERP-trained clinician on the gold-standard treatment for OCD. An ERP-trained clinician can assess the OCD, develop an exposure hierarchy, guide ERP, monitor progress, and adapt the treatment plan. If you are in crisis or having thoughts of suicide, call your local emergency number or a crisis hotline immediately — these tools are not a substitute for emergency care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can OCD be treated?

Yes. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) is treatable, and CBT with Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the gold-standard treatment with the strongest evidence base. ERP produces significant symptom reduction in approximately 70% of people with OCD, and the gains are maintained at follow-up. ERP works by gradually exposing the person to the obsession triggers while refraining from the compulsion, which breaks the negative reinforcement cycle that maintains the obsession-anxiety-compulsion loop. Through repeated exposure without the ritual, the anxiety habituates and the person learns that the feared outcome does not occur. For moderate-to-severe OCD, ERP combined with serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SRIs) produces better outcomes than either treatment alone. The treatment also addresses the 6 cognitive distortions that maintain OCD (My-Thoughts-Mean-Something-Terrible-About-Me, I-Must-Perform-The-Ritual-Or-Something-Bad-Will-Happen, My-Intrusive-Thoughts-Are-Dangerous, Everything-Must-Be-Just-Right, I-Am-Responsible-For-Preventing-All-Harm, My-OCD-Is-Who-I-Am) because these distortions block engagement with ERP. The prognosis is good with engagement in ERP.

What is the difference between obsessions and compulsions?

Obsessions and compulsions are the two core symptoms of Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3), and they function together in the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle. Obsessions are recurrent, persistent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges, or images that the person attempts to ignore, suppress, or neutralize. Obsessions produce anxiety, distress, or disgust. Common obsession themes include contamination fears, doubt (did I lock the door?), aggressive intrusive thoughts (harming others), sexual intrusive thoughts, religious/blasphemous thoughts, symmetry/ordering needs, and hoarding urges. Compulsions are repetitive behaviors (hand washing, checking, ordering) or mental acts (counting, praying, repeating words silently) that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules. Compulsions are performed to reduce the anxiety triggered by the obsession or to prevent a feared outcome, but they are not connected in a realistic way to what they are designed to neutralize and are clearly excessive. The functional relationship is: obsession triggers anxiety, compulsion reduces anxiety (negative reinforcement), reinforcement strengthens the belief the ritual is necessary, cycle strengthens. ERP breaks the cycle by exposing the person to the obsession while preventing the compulsion, teaching the brain that the obsession is not dangerous and the anxiety habituates without the ritual.

Why won't the intrusive thoughts go away when I try to suppress them?

Thought-suppression amplifies intrusive thoughts through a well-documented psychological process called ironic process theory (Daniel Wegner). When you try to suppress a thought, your mind must monitor for the thought to check whether the suppression is working — and the monitoring process keeps the thought accessible in your mind. The harder you try to suppress the thought, the more your mind checks for it, and the more the thought surfaces. This is the white bear problem: if you try not to think of a white bear, you think of it constantly. For people with OCD, the My-Intrusive-Thoughts-Are-Dangerous distortion drives thought-suppression (the person believes the thoughts are dangerous and must be suppressed), but the suppression amplifies the thoughts, which reinforces the belief that the thoughts are dangerous and out of control, which drives more suppression. The cycle is self-reinforcing. The ERP alternative is acceptance: instead of suppressing the intrusive thoughts, the person allows the thoughts to pass without suppression, engagement, or neutralization. Acceptance reduces the frequency and distress of intrusive thoughts because it removes the monitoring process that keeps the thoughts accessible. This is why imaginal exposure (deliberately thinking the intrusive thought without suppressing or neutralizing) is a core ERP technique: the exposure teaches the brain that the thought is not dangerous and the thought habituates (becomes less distressing and less frequent) through the natural process of habituation.

How is OCD different from just being neat or careful?

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (DSM-5 300.3) is fundamentally different from personality traits like neatness, carefulness, or preference for order. The key differences are: (1) Egodystonic vs egosyntonic: OCD symptoms are egodystonic — the person does not want the thoughts or the rituals, finds them distressing, and recognizes them as excessive (at least initially). Personality traits like neatness are egosyntonic — the person values and identifies with the traits. (2) Distress and impairment: OCD causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. Personality traits may be preferences that do not cause distress or impairment. (3) Time-consuming: OCD obsessions and compulsions take more than 1 hour per day. Personality traits do not occupy this time. (4) The cycle: OCD involves the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle — the obsession triggers anxiety, the compulsion reduces anxiety via negative reinforcement, the cycle strengthens. Personality traits do not involve this cycle. (5) Content: OCD obsessions often involve content that violates the person's values (aggressive thoughts, sexual thoughts, contamination fears) — the person is distressed because the thoughts are unwanted. Personality traits involve content aligned with the person's values. (6) Treatment response: OCD symptoms remit with ERP (~70% response rate). Personality traits do not remit with ERP. If you are unsure whether your behaviors are OCD or personality traits, the key question is: do the behaviors cause distress, take more than 1 hour per day, and involve the obsession-anxiety-compulsion cycle? If yes, assessment by an ERP-trained clinician is recommended.
