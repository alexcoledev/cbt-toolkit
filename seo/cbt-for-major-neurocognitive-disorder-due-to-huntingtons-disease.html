Major Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Huntington Disease is diagnosed when there is significant cognitive decline from Huntington disease, characterized by the distinctive clinical features: (a) progressive cognitive decline with a subcortical profile (executive dysfunction, slowed processing speed, impaired recognition memory with relatively preserved recall), (b) choreiform movements (involuntary, jerky, dance-like movements that flow from one body part to another), (c) psychiatric symptoms (depression, irritability, apathy, disinhibition, sometimes psychosis), (d) autosomal dominant inheritance with complete penetrance (CAG repeat expansion in the HTT gene on chromosome 4, 36+ repeats causes HD), (e) younger onset (typically 30-50 years, juvenile onset <20 years is more severe), (f) progressive neurodegeneration primarily of the striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen) with eventual cortical involvement. The cognitive deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities (the 'major' threshold). CBT is evidence-based for both the person with HD (reducing depression, anxiety, irritability, and distress) and the caregiver (reducing burden, depression, and anxiety). The harm is in the cognitive distortions layered on top of the diagnosis: the The-It-Is-Just-A-Movement-Disorder drives cognitive and psychiatric neglect, the The-The-Genetic-Test-Means-I-Should-Give-Up drives therapeutic nihilism and disengagement, the The-It-Is-Just-A-Family-Curse drives fatalistic acceptance, and the The-It-Is-Just-Depression drives antidepressant-only treatment that misses the neurodegenerative cognitive decline.

What is Major Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Huntington Disease?

Major Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Huntington Disease (DSM-5 294.1x) is a diagnosis in the Neurocognitive Disorders category, used when: (1) there is evidence of significant cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition) — based on both concern from the individual, a knowledgeable informant, or the clinician, AND objective evidence of decline; (2) the cognitive deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities (paying bills, managing medications, appointments, travel, complex tasks) — this is the 'major' threshold; (3) the deficits do not occur exclusively during delirium; (4) the deficits are not better explained by another mental or medical disorder; (5) the disorder is not better explained by another neurocognitive disorder. The distinctive clinical features supporting a Huntington disease etiology include: genetic etiology (autosomal dominant inheritance with complete penetrance, CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the HTT gene on chromosome 4, 36+ repeats causes HD, genetic testing is definitive), choreiform movements (involuntary, jerky, dance-like movements that flow randomly from one body part to another, the hallmark motor feature, present in >90% of patients), psychiatric symptoms (depression in ~40-70%, irritability, apathy, disinhibition, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and sometimes psychosis — these are CORE FEATURES of the disease, not just reactions to the diagnosis), subcortical cognitive profile (executive dysfunction and slowed processing speed are prominent early, with impaired recognition memory but relatively preserved recall, in contrast to the cortical profile of Alzheimer's), striatal neurodegeneration (progressive loss of medium spiny neurons in the caudate and putamen, with eventual cortical involvement), and younger onset (typically 30-50 years, with juvenile onset <20 years associated with a more rapidly progressive course and bradykinesia/rigidity rather than chorea). There is currently no disease-modifying treatment, but symptom management (medication for chorea, depression, psychosis; physical, occupational, and speech therapy; CBT for distress and coping) can meaningfully improve quality of life and function.

The "The-It-Is-Just-A-Movement-Disorder" Distortion (movement-as-whole-disease / chorea-as-everything)

Distortion: "Huntington disease is a movement disorder. The chorea — the jerking, the twitching — that is the disease. The thinking problems, the mood problems, those are just reactions to having a movement disorder. If we can control the movements, everything else will follow. The cognitive decline is secondary to the movements. I do not need to address the thinking or the mood separately — treat the chorea and the rest takes care of itself."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-A-Movement-Disorder. The movement-as-whole-disease (HD is only chorea) is the distortion: HUNTINGTON DISEASE IS A MULTI-DOMAIN NEURODEGENERATIVE DISORDER — the cognitive decline (executive dysfunction, processing speed deficits, impaired recognition memory) and the psychiatric symptoms (depression, irritability, apathy, disinhibition) are CORE FEATURES produced by striatal degeneration, not secondary reactions to the movements. The chorea-as-everything is the distortion: while chorea is the most visible feature, cognitive and psychiatric symptoms often PRECEDE the motor symptoms by years, and the cognitive decline drives functional impairment (job loss, financial errors, driving safety) independently of the movements. The motor-reduction is the distortion: reducing HD to a movement disorder drives cognitive and psychiatric neglect — the movements get treated (medication, therapy) while the cognitive decline and the depression go unaddressed, producing worse outcomes than integrated management. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does neuropsychological testing show, do cognitive and psychiatric symptoms precede or follow the chorea, what drives functional decline — movements or cognition or both), challenging the movement-as-whole-disease, and building engagement with the cognitive and psychiatric management alongside the motor management. This distortion is harmful because it drives cognitive and psychiatric neglect, misses the features that often appear first, and produces worse functional outcomes than integrated multi-domain management."

The "The-It-Is-Just-Depression" Distortion (psychiatric-as-primary / depression-as-cause)

Distortion: "I am depressed and that is why I cannot think clearly. The memory problems, the slow thinking, the difficulty planning — that is what depression does. It is pseudodementia. If we treat the depression, the thinking will come back. The Huntington disease causes movements, but the cognitive problems are just the depression. I do not need to worry about the cognitive decline being permanent — it is just the depression talking."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Depression. The psychiatric-as-primary (cognitive decline means depression) is the distortion: COGNITIVE DECLINE IN HUNTINGTON DISEASE IS A CORE NEURODEGENERATIVE FEATURE produced by progressive loss of medium spiny neurons in the caudate and putamen, not a consequence of depression — the subcortical profile (executive dysfunction, processing speed deficits, impaired recognition memory with preserved recall) is distinct from the cognitive pattern of depression (pseudodementia), and neuropsychological testing can distinguish the two. The depression-as-cause is the distortion: while depression is common in HD (~40-70%) and CAN cause cognitive symptoms, the cognitive decline in HD PROGRESSES independently of mood — treating the depression improves mood and may improve some cognitive function, but the neurodegenerative cognitive decline continues because the striatal degeneration continues. The mood-as-explanation is the distortion: attributing all cognitive decline to depression drives antidepressant-only treatment that misses the neurodegenerative cognitive decline, delaying the functional accommodations and cognitive strategies that could help. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does neuropsychological testing show — HD pattern vs depression pattern, does the cognitive decline progress despite depression treatment, what is the recognition vs recall memory profile), challenging the psychiatric-as-primary, and reframing the cognitive decline as a core feature of striatal degeneration that co-exists with (and is worsened by) depression but is not caused by it. This distortion is harmful because it drives antidepressant-only treatment, misses the neurodegenerative cognitive decline, and delays the cognitive strategies and accommodations that could improve function."

The "The-The-Genetic-Test-Means-I-Should-Give-Up" Distortion (genetic-fatalism / gene-as-sentence)

Distortion: "The genetic test is positive. I have the HD gene. There is no cure. Nothing can stop it. Nothing can slow it. So what is the point? Why bother with therapy, with strategies, with management? The gene is a death sentence. I should just wait for it to take its course. Trying to manage the symptoms is futile when the underlying cause is genetic and progressive and unstoppable."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-The-Genetic-Test-Means-I-Should-Give-Up. The genetic-fatalism (gene positive means give up) is the distortion: A POSITIVE GENETIC TEST MEANS THE DISEASE IS REAL, NOT THAT NOTHING CAN HELP — while there is currently no disease-modifying treatment, symptom management (medication for chorea, depression, irritability, psychosis; physical, occupational, and speech therapy; CBT for distress, coping, and caregiver support) can MEANINGFULLY improve quality of life, function, and the trajectory of disability. The gene-as-sentence is the distortion: the CAG repeat expansion predicts the disease, but the SEVERITY, the PROGRESSION RATE, and the ONSET AGE vary — active management, engagement with care, and healthy lifestyle can influence the clinical course, and gene-environment interactions mean the genetic result is not a deterministic script. The mutation-as-doom is the distortion: treating the genetic result as doom drives therapeutic nihilism and disengagement from the very management that helps — the person stops attending clinic, stops using strategies, stops engaging with support, and the disease progresses faster without management. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does the research say about symptom management outcomes, what does the clinical course show with vs without management, what is the range of severity and progression), challenging the genetic-fatalism, and building engagement with the symptom management and supportive care that improve quality of life. This distortion is harmful because it drives disengagement from care, therapeutic nihilism, and a self-fulfilling spiral where giving up accelerates the functional decline that management could slow."

The "The-It-Is-Just-A-Family-Curse" Distortion (family-history-normalization / inheritance-as-destiny)

Distortion: "My mother had it, my grandfather had it, my uncle had it. It is just the family curse. Everyone in our family gets it eventually. There is nothing special about my case — it is just what happens to us. I do not need to do anything differently. It is in the blood. Fighting it is fighting fate. I just accept that this is what our family goes through."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-A-Family-Curse. The family-history-normalization (it runs in the family so it is just our fate) is the distortion: THE FAMILY HISTORY IS A RISK FACTOR, NOT A REASON TO DISENGAGE — the autosomal dominant inheritance means the disease is genetic, but active management (symptom treatment, therapy, support, planning) improves outcomes for everyone with HD, regardless of family history. The inheritance-as-destiny is the distortion: while the gene is inherited, the CLINICAL COURSE is not fully determined — severity, progression rate, and onset age vary, and management can influence the trajectory of disability. The bloodline-as-fate is the distortion: treating the family history as fate drives fatalistic acceptance that prevents the active management, the advance planning, and the support engagement that could improve quality of life — the family curse framing also isolates the family and prevents them from seeking the broader HD community support that could help. CBT helps by examining the evidence (what does the research say about outcomes with vs without management, how does the clinical course vary within families, what does the HD community offer), challenging the family-history-normalization, and building engagement with the management and support that help regardless of inheritance. This distortion is harmful because it drives fatalistic acceptance, prevents active management, isolates the family, and allows the disease to progress faster without the interventions that could help."

The "The-The-Movements-Are-Just-Tics" Distortion (chorea-as-tics / movement-as-habit)

Distortion: "The jerking, the twitching — those are just tics. I have always been a fidgety person. Lots of people have tics. It is just a habit, a nervous tic. It does not mean anything serious. The doctor is overreacting. I do not need a genetic test or a neurologist — these are just tics that I have had for years. They are not getting worse, I am just noticing them more."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-The-Movements-Are-Just-Tics. The chorea-as-tics (the movements are just tics) is the distortion: CHOREIFORM MOVEMENTS IN HUNTINGTON DISEASE ARE A CORE MOTOR SYMPTOM of striatal degeneration, not tics — chorea is involuntary, irregular, non-rhythmic, and flows from one body part to another, distinct from tics which are stereotyped, suppressible, and preceded by a premonitory urge. The movement-as-habit is the distortion: dismissing chorea as a habit or a long-standing fidget prevents the neurological evaluation that could diagnose HD early, when management and planning can have the most impact — the movements in HD PROGRESS, whereas habits and tics are typically stable. The jerk-as-involuntary-tic is the distortion: the chorea in HD is not suppressible (unlike tics), not preceded by an urge (unlike tics), and is accompanied by cognitive and psychiatric features (unlike a primary tic disorder) — the differential diagnosis matters because HD requires genetic testing, multidisciplinary management, and family counseling that a tic disorder does not. CBT helps by examining the evidence (are the movements suppressible, are they preceded by an urge, are they progressive, is there a family history, are there cognitive or psychiatric features), challenging the chorea-as-tics, and building engagement with the neurological evaluation that could diagnose HD and initiate management. This distortion is harmful because it drives misdiagnosis, delays the HD evaluation, and allows the disease to progress without the management and planning that could improve outcomes."

The "The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same" Distortion (dementia-oversimplification / category-as-uniform)

Distortion: "Dementia is dementia. Whether it is Alzheimer's, Huntington's, Lewy body, or vascular, it is all the same thing — memory loss and confusion. The specific type does not matter. The treatment is the same, the prognosis is the same, and there is no point in distinguishing them. Huntington dementia is just another word for Alzheimer's that happens in younger people with a family history."
Reframe: "I am examining the evidence and challenging the The-It-Is-Just-Dementia-All-The-Same. The dementia-oversimplification (all dementia is the same) is the distortion: the different types of dementia have DISTINCTIVE FEATURES, DIFFERENT MANAGEMENT, and DIFFERENT PROGNOSIS — Huntington disease has a genetic etiology (CAG repeat expansion), a younger onset (30-50 years), prominent motor symptoms (chorea), prominent psychiatric symptoms (depression, irritability, apathy), and striatal neurodegeneration, which Alzheimer's does not have; these features require DIFFERENT MANAGEMENT: (1) the motor symptoms in HD require movement disorder management (chorea medication, physical therapy) that Alzheimer's does not use; (2) the psychiatric symptoms in HD are core features requiring specific treatment, not just reactions to be comforted; (3) the genetic etiology requires genetic counseling for at-risk family members, including testing decisions and reproductive planning; (4) the younger onset affects employment, family roles, and financial planning in ways that late-onset dementia does not; (5) the striatal neuroanatomical pattern produces a subcortical cognitive profile (executive dysfunction, processing speed) requiring different cognitive strategies than the cortical profile of Alzheimer's. The category-as-uniform (the type does not matter) is the distortion: the specific diagnosis drives the treatment, the care planning, the family counseling, and the prognosis. CBT helps by examining the evidence, challenging the oversimplification, and building engagement with the HD-specific management. This distortion is harmful because it drives generic treatment that misses the HD-specific interventions (motor management, psychiatric treatment, genetic counseling, young-family support) and the prognostic information that is critical for planning."

Is CBT evidence-based for major neurocognitive disorder due to Huntington disease?

Yes. CBT is evidence-based for two populations affected by major neurocognitive disorder due to Huntington disease. For the person with HD, CBT addresses depression, anxiety, irritability, and distress that accompany cognitive decline and motor symptoms — these symptoms are common, distressing, and treatable, and CBT reduces their severity and improves quality of life and engagement with care. For the caregiver, CBT reduces caregiver burden, depression, and anxiety by targeting the cognitive distortions that maintain distress (the movement-as-whole-disease, the psychiatric-as-primary, the genetic-fatalism, the family-history-normalization, the chorea-as-tics, the dementia-oversimplification). The genetic nature of HD adds a unique psychosocial burden (genetic testing decisions, at-risk family members, reproductive planning) that makes CBT especially important for building coping skills and reducing isolation. Clinical guidelines recognize CBT as a non-pharmacological intervention for Huntington disease. The evidence shows that CBT improves quality of life, function, and outcomes for both the person with HD and the caregiver.

What happens in a CBT session for major neurocognitive disorder due to Huntington disease?

A CBT session for major neurocognitive disorder due to Huntington disease involves different approaches for the person with HD and the caregiver. For the person with HD: (1) Engagement and assessment — the therapist builds a relationship adapted to the cognitive level, assesses current symptoms, distress, motor function, and quality of life. (2) Depression and anxiety work — cognitive restructuring adapted to the cognitive level, targeting the depression and anxiety that are core features of HD. (3) Irritability and behavioral work — addressing the irritability, apathy, and disinhibition that strain relationships and function. (4) Genetic coping — addressing the genetic-fatalism and family-curse distortions, building engagement with management. (5) Symptom coping — strategies for cognitive deficits, chorea, functional changes, and progression. For the caregiver: (1) Assessment of burden, depression, anxiety, and the cognitive distortions. (2) Psychoeducation about Huntington disease, the subcortical profile, the genetic inheritance, the motor and psychiatric features, and the prognosis. (3) Cognitive restructuring — targeting the six distortions. (4) Behavioral activation — building engagement with support, self-care, and meaningful activity. (5) Caregiving skills — communication, symptom management, coordination with the neurology and psychiatry teams, and planning for the progressive course. CBT for Huntington disease and caregivers is typically delivered by a therapist trained in CBT with neurodegenerative experience.
