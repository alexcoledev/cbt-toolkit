What is Encopresis?

Encopresis (DSM-5 307.7) is characterized by repeated passage of feces into inappropriate places (clothes, floor, other places), by a child age 4 or older. The soiling occurs either ~1+ times per month for ~3+ months, or causes clinically significant distress or impairment. The soiling is NOT due to a substance (e.g., laxative abuse), medication, or general medical condition (e.g., celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, Hirschsprung disease, spinal cord abnormality). Subtypes: retentive (constipation-associated, ~80-90% of cases — the child withholds stool, the stool hardens and accumulates, the colon stretches and loses sensation, and liquid stool leaks around the hard mass as overflow incontinence; the child is often unaware of the leakage) vs nonretentive (~10-20% — the child passes stool in inappropriate places without constipation, often associated with emotional/behavioral factors such as oppositionality, trauma, anxiety, regression). The condition is common: ~1-3% of children, more common in boys. Onset is typically after toilet training age. The key distinction from enuresis is that encopresis is fecal vs urinary; from voluntary soiling is that encopresis is involuntary or overflow; from medical incontinence is that encopresis excludes medical causes. Retentive encopresis is the most common form and is often misframed as voluntary soiling — the leakage is overflow, not choice. CBT combined with bowel training (scheduled toilet sits, cleanout if constipated, stool softeners, dietary changes) and constipation management is evidence-based — CBT improves adherence, reduces shame, addresses the withholding fear, and treats comorbid anxiety/depression/oppositionality.

The 3-Component CBT Protocol for Encopresis

CBT for encopresis (DSM-5 307.7) combines three evidence-based components. Each component targets a different maintaining factor — the cognitive distortions, the behavioral pattern, and the family/systemic context.

1. Cognitive Restructuring

Identify and challenge the six cognitive distortions that maintain shame and avoidance. Keep an encopresis-belief log: each time the child or parent notices a shame thought (I am broken, I should have grown out of this, it is just laziness, something is wrong with my body, no one can help, I must hide this), write it down, identify the distortion, examine the evidence (the soiling is involuntary or overflow, the condition is common and treatable, bowel training works, disclosure enables support), and generate a reframe. Use Socratic questioning: Is the soiling a choice? Do many children soil? Does bowel training work? Has hiding ever reduced the soiling? Does blame improve bowel control? Psychoeducation about the involuntary or overflow nature of encopresis, the developmental trajectory of bowel control, the prevalence, the retentive vs nonretentive mechanism, and the effectiveness of bowel training — the goal is to replace shame with accurate understanding. For retentive encopresis, specific psychoeducation about overflow incontinence (the leakage is liquid stool around a hard mass, not voluntary soiling) is critical — this single reframe often dissolves the It-Is-Just-Laziness distortion.

2. Behavioral Intervention

For retentive encopresis (constipation-associated, ~80-90%): Disimpaction (cleanout) — enema, suppository, or oral polyethylene glycol to clear the hardened mass. Stool softeners (polyethylene glycol, lactulose) to keep stool soft and prevent re-accumulation. Scheduled toilet sits: 5-10 minutes after meals (especially breakfast, when the gastrocolic reflex is strongest), 1-2 times per day, with a footstool for proper positioning and no pressure. Dietary changes: increase fiber (fruits, vegetables, whole grains), increase fluids, reduce constipating foods (excessive dairy, bananas, white rice). Behavioral reinforcement: sticker chart or reward for toilet sits and clean underwear, never for "not soiling" (which the child cannot control). For nonretentive encopresis (~10-20%): Scheduled toilet sits at regular times. Behavioral intervention for the emotional/behavioral drivers (oppositionality, anxiety, trauma). Comorbid treatment for ADHD, ODD, anxiety, or enuresis. The child is never punished for soiling — the behavioral component is collaborative: the child and family do the protocol together.

3. Family/Systemic Support

Non-shaming communication: the parent frames the soiling as a medical-behavioral pattern, not a character defect or a choice. No punishment, no teasing, no public shaming, no comparison to siblings. For retentive encopresis, the parent understands that the leakage is overflow — the child is not choosing to soil. Collaborative routine: the child participates in the toilet sits, the clean underwear changes, the dietary changes — the child is a partner in the treatment, not the problem. Relapse plan: soiling episodes are not failures, they are data; the family adjusts the protocol and continues. Comorbid treatment: if the child has anxiety, depression, ADHD, ODD, trauma, or enuresis (common comorbidities), treat the comorbid condition alongside the encopresis. School/social support: if the child avoids school or social activities due to odor or shame, involve the school nurse discreetly and provide a change-of-clothes kit. The family/systemic component is where the cognitive and behavioral gains are sustained — shame-free family context is the foundation of long-term bowel regularity.

The 6 Cognitive Distortions in Encopresis

Each distortion maintains the shame-avoidance-treatment-blockage cycle. Each has a reframe backed by evidence.

1. I-Am-Broken (shame-fusion / self-defect): "I soil my pants, therefore I am defective." The child fuses identity with the behavior — the soiling becomes a character trait, not a medical-behavioral pattern.
Reframe: "I am a child who soils. The soiling is involuntary or overflow, common, and treatable. It is a pattern I have, not a trait I am. I am not broken — I have a condition that responds to treatment."
2. I-Should-Have-Grown-Out-Of-It (developmental-shame / timeline-fatalism): "I am 7 and I still soil myself — something is wrong with my development." The child or parent expects bowel control to resolve on a fixed timeline and interprets variation as defect.
Reframe: "Bowel control develops on a variable timeline. ~1-3% of children soil past age 4. I am not behind a fixed schedule — I am on my own developmental trajectory, and treatment can help."
3. It-Is-Just-Laziness (laziness-misattribution / willpower-frame): "If I tried harder, I would make it to the toilet." The parent or child frames the involuntary or overflow soiling as a voluntary choice or a willpower failure.
Reframe: "The soiling is overflow (liquid leaking around a hard mass I cannot feel) or involuntary — I cannot try harder to not soil, because I am not choosing to soil. Willpower is not the mechanism; bowel training and constipation management are, and that is what the protocol treats."
4. Something-Is-Wrong-With-My-Body (medical-catastrophizing / structural-fear): "My bowels are broken — my colon is damaged, my nerves are dead, it will never recover." The child or parent fears structural damage from the constipation or the stretching.
Reframe: "The colon stretching from constipation is reversible with treatment — once the stool is cleared and regularity is maintained, the colon shrinks back and sensation returns. My body is not permanently broken; it is temporarily stretched, and bowel training restores it."
5. No-One-Can-Help (treatment-hopelessness / futility): "Nothing works — we have tried everything, the doctor does not know, it will never stop." The family gives up on treatment before trying the evidence-based options.
Reframe: "Bowel training with disimpaction, stool softeners, and scheduled toilet sits is evidence-based with high success rates. CBT improves adherence and reduces shame. We have not tried everything — we have not tried the evidence-based protocol, and it works."
6. I-Must-Hide-This (secrecy-isolation / shame-concealment): "No one can know — not my friends, not my school, not my doctor." The shame produces secrecy, the secrecy blocks support and treatment, the lack of treatment maintains the soiling.
Reframe: "Disclosure to the right people (doctor, school nurse, a trusted adult) is the path to support and treatment. Hiding maintains the shame; sharing enables the bowel training, the CBT, and the family routine that end the pattern."

When to seek professional help

CBT tools on this page are educational, not a substitute for professional care. Seek help from a CBT-trained clinician or pediatrician if: the encopresis is retentive (constipation-associated) and requires medical management (disimpaction, stool softeners); the child has significant shame, anxiety, or depression about the soiling; the child avoids school, sleepovers, or social activities; the family is in conflict over the soiling; there are signs of a medical cause (painful defecation, blood in stool, abdominal distension, failure to thrive, ribbon-like stools); the encopresis persists past age 5-6 without improvement; or bowel training has not been tried. A pediatrician can rule out medical causes (Hirschsprung disease, celiac, IBD, spinal cord abnormality) and refer to a CBT clinician. Encopresis is common, treatable, and never the child's fault.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is encopresis the same as voluntary soiling?

No. Encopresis (DSM-5 307.7) is NOT voluntary soiling. In retentive encopresis (~80-90% of cases), the soiling is overflow incontinence — the child withholds stool (often due to painful past bowel movements or shame), the stool hardens and accumulates, the colon stretches and loses sensation, and liquid stool leaks around the hard mass without the child's awareness or control. The child is not choosing to soil. In nonretentive encopresis (~10-20%), the child passes stool in inappropriate places, but this is often driven by emotional/behavioral factors (oppositionality, trauma, anxiety, regression) rather than a simple choice. The It-Is-Just-Laziness distortion is the most damaging misframe — it produces blame and punishment that worsen the shame and the withholding, never treating the underlying pattern. The distinction from voluntary soiling is critical: encopresis is a medical-behavioral pattern, not a character defect, and it responds to CBT combined with bowel training, not to discipline.

What is the difference between retentive and nonretentive encopresis?

Retentive encopresis (constipation-associated, ~80-90% of cases): the child withholds stool (often due to painful past bowel movements, shame, or control-seeking), the stool accumulates and hardens in the colon, the colon stretches and loses sensation, and liquid stool leaks around the hard mass and out into the underwear — this is overflow incontinence, not voluntary soiling. The child is often unaware of the leakage. Treatment focuses on disimpaction (cleanout), stool softeners, scheduled toilet sits, and dietary changes to restore normal bowel function, combined with CBT for the withholding fear and shame. Nonretentive encopresis (~10-20% of cases): the child passes stool in inappropriate places without constipation or retention — this is more often associated with emotional/behavioral factors (oppositionality, trauma, anxiety, regression, enuresis comorbidity). Treatment focuses on CBT for the emotional/behavioral drivers, scheduled toilet sits, and family/systemic support. The cognitive distortions differ: retentive encopresis produces more Something-Is-Wrong-With-My-Body (fear of structural damage from constipation) and No-One-Can-Help (fear that the bowel will never recover); nonretentive encopresis produces more I-Am-Broken (shame about voluntary-feeling soiling) and I-Must-Hide-This (secrecy about the behavior). Both respond to CBT combined with bowel training, but retentive encopresis requires medical management of the constipation first.

How is encopresis different from enuresis?

Encopresis (DSM-5 307.7) and enuresis (DSM-5 307.6) are both Elimination Disorders, but they differ in the substance, the age criterion, the prevalence, and the typical mechanism. Encopresis: repeated passage of feces into inappropriate places, age 4+, ~1-3% of children, more common in boys, often constipation-associated (retentive, ~80-90%). Enuresis: repeated involuntary voiding of urine into bed or clothes, age 5+, ~5-10% of 7-year-olds, more common in boys, often developmental (primary) or stress-triggered (secondary). The cognitive distortions are parallel (I-Am-Broken, I-Should-Have-Grown-Out-Of-It, It-Is-Just-Laziness, Something-Is-Wrong-With-My-Body, No-One-Can-Help, I-Must-Hide-This) but take different forms: in encopresis the shame is often more intense (fecal soiling carries more social stigma than urinary wetting), the It-Is-Just-Laziness is more often from the parent (who assumes the child is choosing not to use the toilet), and the Something-Is-Wrong-With-My-Body is more often about the bowel (constipation, colon damage, permanent loss of sensation). Both are treated with CBT combined with behavioral intervention (bowel training for encopresis, alarm therapy for enuresis) and family/systemic support. The two conditions are often comorbid (~15-25% of children with encopresis also have enuresis).
